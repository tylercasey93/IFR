# Chapter Thirteen — Diversion

*Wren*

The forecast had said light snow showers, diminishing.

The forecast, Wren reflected — watching central Oregon disappear beneath a squall line that had read a different forecast entirely — was entitled to its opinion.

"Redmond ATIS just flipped," Theo said. "Heavy snow, visibility half a mile, vertical visibility six hundred. Braking action medcode — no, there it goes — braking action *poor* reported by a Caravan, and—" a pause while the new METAR spooled in, and she could hear him check the timestamp, because the house religion had rules about datalink and history "—this is four minutes old, so it's real. And field ops just NOTAM'd a closure window. Snow removal. Forty-five minutes, starting now."

In the back, Adaeze Okafor and Chidinma had gone quiet in the way of passengers who can feel a flight deck thinking. They were twenty minutes out from a winter lodge viewing that had — until roughly ninety seconds ago — involved mulled wine.

Seattle Center made it formal: "Meridian Three-Five, Redmond's closed for snow removal, expect holding at DESCH, maintain one-five-thousand. Expect further clearance one-niner-three-zero Zulu."

Theo read it back, spun the hold into the box, and looked at her. "Forty minutes of EFC. Fuel ledger's live." The sheet was already ruled — it was always already ruled — and his pen walked the columns while she slowed the airplane toward holding speed, three minutes early, per the book, per the sermon, per their whole shared liturgy. "Here's the arithmetic. Holding burn at this weight: we can hold to the EFC and still be fat. The question isn't *can we wait*. The question is what we're waiting *for*. So — captain's decision brief, and I'll play the board. What does waiting buy us?"

"A plowed runway," Wren said, "under a squall that's still snowing on it. Vertical visibility six hundred with the approach minimums at — pull them — call it comparable, on a surface reporting poor and trending worse, at a field with one runway and no tower, in a valley, at night in about two hours. And if we wait the full EFC and shoot it and miss, we've spent forty minutes of gas to arrive at the *same* divert decision, closer to the terrain, further from the alternates, in the dark."

"And diverting now buys us?"

"Pendleton. High desert, north of the squall's spine — say the numbers."

"Pendleton's calling few clouds at four thousand, ten miles, wind three-twenty at twelve. Boring on purpose." He tapped the ledger. "Fuel to Pendleton from the hold: fifty minutes' flying, and we land with an hour forty in the tanks. No part of that sentence contains the words *minimum fuel*."

"Which is the entire point," Wren said, and keyed the cabin mic, because the client deserved the truth at the same speed as the crew. "Ms. Okafor — weather brief. Redmond's under a heavy snow squall and closed for plowing at least forty-five minutes; conditions there are at the bottom of what the approach permits and getting worse, on a slick runway, with darkness coming. We have ample fuel to wait, and I've decided not to spend it that way. We're diverting to Pendleton — clear skies, dry-ish runway, about fifty minutes. The lodge will still be there tomorrow. This is me being the pilot you pay for."

A pause. Then: "Captain, I have sat on boards for four decades, and the rarest thing I ever saw was an executive who could tell the difference between *we can* and *we should* while the option was still cheap." The speaker warmed. "Divert us, dear. Chidinma, look — she didn't even wait to be low on anything. That's the whole secret. Decide while you're rich."

Theo was already talking to Center. Wren rolled the King Air out of the hold's first turn — one lap, entered and exited like a professional courtesy — and pointed the nose northeast, and the squall fell away behind them like an argument they'd declined to have.

"For the record," Theo said, off-frequency, "that's the lecture I used to give students with a whiteboard and a fatality report. *The divert decision is cheapest at the moment it first looks reasonable, and the price only goes up.* You just did it in ninety seconds with a client aboard."

"I've paid the old price," Wren said. "Once was educational."

---

Pendleton delivered exactly the boredom advertised — a visual approach in crystalline cold air, the Blue Mountains white to the south, the runway dry where it mattered. The FBO consisted of one recliner, one popcorn machine, and an elderly golden retriever the line guy introduced as Chandelle, and Theo assessed the establishment with the gravity of a Michelin inspector while Okafor's cars were arranged.

"Popcorn's fresh, dog comes to you, line guy called the airplane 'she' with real feeling," he reported. "Five stars. Would emergency-divert again."

"You rate FBOs."

"I rate everything, Wren. It's a rich inner life." He accepted a respectful amount of retriever onto his shoe. "For the record, the one in Santa Barbara lost a star for cologne contamination."

Then the storm, with the whole night to work with, began spreading north like spilled ink. By the time the borrowed crew car reached town, snow was falling on Pendleton too, fat and unhurried, a done deal.

The inn was a hundred-year-old brick thing on Main Street with a lobby fireplace and, it emerged, exactly three rooms left ahead of a storm that had chased everyone indoors.

"Three," Okafor repeated, at the desk, with the sorrow of a woman doing easy math. "Well. Chidinma and I will take two — I snore, she reads until three, we've negotiated this treaty before. Which leaves—" and here Adaeze Okafor, empress of matchmakers, engineer of galas, had the grace to look *almost* neutral "—one. For the crew. Who are, of course—"

"A couple," said the desk clerk helpfully, reading the reservation Okafor's assistant had already made.

"—a couple," Okafor agreed, radiant, and swept toward the elevator with her goddaughter, pausing only to murmur, at a volume engineered for exactly two sets of ears: "The storm is not my doing, darlings. But I *will* be taking credit."

Dinner happened first, because the inn's dining room was the only thing open in Pendleton by then and the storm had herded everyone into it: the crew, the client, the goddaughter, two stranded wheat brokers, and a high school wrestling coach who kept buying rounds for "the pilots who beat the snow."

It was, Wren would think later, the last easy hour of the old arrangement — chowder and firelight and Okafor holding the room the way she held every room. It was Chidinma who wound her up: "Auntie. Tell them how Uncle proposed. It's a weather story; they'll like it."

"It is *the* weather story." Okafor set down her spoon. "Nairobi, 1981. We had been — circling one another, your uncle and I, for three years by then. Different cities, careful letters, two careers like two flight schedules that never quite aligned. And that March I was to fly out for a conference, and he drove me to the airport, very quiet, very correct — and the rains came. Not polite rain. Biblical, grounding, roads-becoming-rivers rain. Every flight canceled. The whole terminal groaning. And your uncle looked out at it for a long moment, and then he took my suitcase back off the trolley, and he said—" she laid her hand flat on the table; Wren was coming to know the gesture, the setting down of the words like cards "—*'Adaeze. I have done the arithmetic. In good weather you are always leaving. I would like to marry you before the sky clears.'*"

The wrestling coach put his hand over his heart. Chidinma mouthed the last line along with her, a woman who had grown up on it.

"We were married eleven days later, in borrowed shoes, and the sun came out during the vows, which he claimed until his last year of life to have arranged personally." Okafor picked her spoon back up, entirely satisfied with the state of the table. "I have told you all before, children: everything that matters starts with weather. Weather is just the world asking whether you meant it." Her eyes, lamplit, rested a moment on the two pilots wedged shoulder to shoulder on the bench across from her — the seating chart having been, like so much of the season, arranged. "Some of us require more asking than others."

Wren looked at her chowder with enormous professionalism.

Under the table, unhurried, Theo's knee came to rest against hers — deniable, plausible, bench-geometry — and this time, instead of prosecuting the case, she left it there and finished her dinner, one careful spoonful at a time, while the storm asked its question against the windows.

---

The room was small and warm and had one bed, a radiator with opinions, and a window over Main Street where the snow came down through the streetlight like static. They stood inside the door with their flight bags, two people who had flown through ice and interference and each other's histories, undone at last by hotel inventory.

"I'll take the chair," Theo said, at the same moment Wren said, "Don't take the chair."

The radiator ticked. Snow filled the streetlight.

"Wren."

"Here's my position," she said, and her voice did what it did on final — went quieter, and surer, both at once. "Clause Two says no performance without an audience. There's no audience, Theo. There hasn't been an audience for weeks and we both know it. Whatever this is, it hasn't been the act since—" the hangar; the coaster; the arc; she made a small gesture that included the entire fall "—inventory. And I'm done logging it as *not loggable*." She set her flight bag down, deliberate, a gear handle moved on schedule. "You told me once you'd had the inbound course set for weeks and you were waiting on the clearance." She looked up at him, snowlight and lamplight, and gave the transmission plainly, no doorway, no shoulder, dead ahead: "Meridian Three-Five, cleared for the approach."

Theo Marsh crossed the room the way he'd walked through rain the first morning — unhurried, unbothered, like the weather was a colleague — and took her face in both hands and kissed her, and this time nobody filed anything.

It was not like the hangar. The hangar had been adrenaline finding a door; this was a decision made in motion, briefed for weeks, flown with both hands. Her fingers found his collar, then the buttons of it; his hands traced the line of her spine like a man reading an approach plate he'd memorized and wanted anyway; and when she laughed against his mouth — helpless, disbelieving, *fifth documented occurrence* — he pulled back an inch and said, hoarse, "Stabilized?"

"Configured, on speed, needles centered," said Wren Hale, and pulled him toward the bed by the shirt-front, and switched off the lamp.

The snow fell on Main Street all night, through the streetlight, over the crew car and the courthouse and the whole silver-quiet town, and the radiator ticked like a metronome nobody was flying to, and the room kept its own counsel — save to note that at some point, in the dark, laughing, breathless, one of them murmured *"positive rate,"* and the other one, without a heartbeat's hesitation, answered *"gear up,"* and after that there were no further radio calls for a long, long time.

---

Morning came in white and dazzling, the storm blown through, Main Street a postcard being aggressively shoveled. Wren woke where she had never once in three years allowed herself to wake — in the middle of things, warm, unarmored, someone's arm heavy across her — and waited for the panic to check in.

It didn't. The needle sat centered. No flags.

Theo was awake, she discovered, watching the snowlight on the ceiling with the expression of a man doing math he liked. His hand moved absently along her shoulder, wing-walking, proprietary in a way that should have set off every alarm on her panel and instead read as *systems normal*.

"So," he said. "Amendment procedures."

"Mm?"

"The agreement." He turned his head; his eyes at close range and full daylight were, unhelpfully, wonderful. "Legal pad's in my flight bag. As of last night there are — irregularities. Clause Two's been violated so thoroughly it should get back pay. I'd like the record clean, Captain. Propose your amendments."

And that was the thing about him, Wren thought — the thing she'd been circling at fifteen miles for months: he'd learned exactly which of her rituals were armor and which were load-bearing, and he never once asked her to shed the load-bearing ones. He asked her to *amend*. In writing. In order.

So they did it right there, her chin on his chest, the legal pad retrieved and propped against his knees, and the winter sun climbing over Pendleton:

*Amendment One: Clause Two is repealed. There is no act. There never really was.* (Her wording. He initialed it with a flourish.)

*Amendment Two: the arrangement is real, effective — * they argued the effective date for five entire minutes; she held out for the hangar, he lobbied, absurdly, for the first briefing — *effective retroactively, date disputed, both parties satisfied.*

*Amendment Three: nobody else's copy gets updated yet.* This one was hers too, and she watched him read it and take care with it. "Not a secret because I'm ashamed," she said. "A secret because it's *mine*. For a few weeks I want one thing in my life that isn't briefed to a client, an owner, or a check airman. The gala's in ten days. There'll be a hundred people and a toast and Okafor holding court, and after — after, we update everybody's copy. Deal?"

"Deal," Theo said. "One condition. Clause Four stands. Both directions, always, no expiry. Anybody can go missed, no questions."

"Clause Four stands," Wren agreed, and something in her — the part that had once held for a man until the tanks ran dry — noted that this one, this one insisted on *her* exits even in the middle of his own arrival, and marked the observation *retain permanently*.

"Then it's ratified." He set the pad aside and pulled her back down. "Also, for the minutes: last night, at approximately — "

"The minutes don't need a timeline, Marsh."

"—*positive rate*," he said, insufferable, delighted, and she smothered the sixth documented occurrence against his shoulder, and breakfast was very nearly late.

---

They ran the debrief over waffles in the inn's dining room, because Clause Three had survived all amendments, and because Okafor and Chidinma had taken a triumphant window table from which to radiate satisfaction and pretend to read menus.

"Divert doctrine," Wren said, pouring syrup with captain's precision. "Go."

"Decide while you're rich," Theo said. "The divert is cheapest the first moment it looks reasonable — every minute after, the price climbs: fuel, daylight, distance to the good options, and decision quality, which degrades exactly when you need it most. Holding fuel answers *can we wait*; only the destination answers *is the wait worth buying* — and a runway being plowed under an active squall, trending worse into darkness, is a bad purchase at any fuel state. Update the picture with real timestamps — a four-minute-old METAR is truth, a twenty-minute-old mosaic is history. Tell the client the plan in the same breath you tell ATC, in plain sentences, uncertainty included. Never let pride carry you to *minimum fuel* — the words work, advisory then emergency, but the best fuel declaration is the divert that made them unnecessary. And log the EFC even when you leave the hold early." He looked up from his coffee, and let the lightness fall away for the last item, and under the table his ankle found hers, steady, deliberate, filed. "And when the weather finally breaks — amend in writing. Effective retroactively. Date disputed."

"Both parties satisfied," said Wren.

At the window table, Adaeze Okafor, watching two pilots pretend not to hold hands under a table stacked with waffles, said something quiet in Igbo to her goddaughter, and lifted her teacup to the snow-bright street like a woman toasting a landing only she had seen coming the whole way down.

*End of Chapter Thirteen.*
