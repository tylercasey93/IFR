# Chapter Five — Squawks

*Wren*

The morning it happened, Wren was in the left seat at six-forty with the checklist on her knee, doing the part of instrument flying that never makes it into anyone's daydreams: sitting still on the ground, interrogating a panel.

The instrument flight deck check was her favorite ritual at Meridian, which said something about either the ritual or Meridian. Battery on. Watch the electronic displays boot and self-test, annunciators doing their roll call. Set the altimeter to the field's current setting and check it against Boeing Field's published elevation — within seventy-five feet or the instrument was lying, and a lying altimeter was a fire-your-realtor problem, not a fix-it-later problem. Cross-check the standby. Note the vacuum-free hum of a glass airplane and then give the standby attitude indicator — the little mechanical one, the last honest gyro, the one that would someday matter more than everything else on the panel combined — time to erect and settle level, no more than a few degrees off, no drunken lean.

It leaned.

It leaned, and it kept leaning, five degrees, eight, wandering like a man looking for his keys, and Wren watched it with the specific sinking calm of a captain whose day has just changed shape.

"Morning, sunshine." Theo swung up the airstair with two coffees and his headset bag. "Okafor's car's at eight-thirty. Portland, then the coast — hey." He read her face, then followed it to the panel. Watched the standby horizon stagger. "Well," he said, setting the coffees down with great care, "that's not personality. That's a failure."

"It's been slow to erect for a week. I wrote it up as *monitor*." She heard her own flat tone and let it stand. "It's done monitoring. And there's more — flip Nav Two up."

He flipped it. The number two nav radio, the old-faithful backup to all the GPS glass, powered on and displayed a frequency and did absolutely nothing else. No flag movement, no ident audio, dead as a museum piece.

"So," Theo said. "Client leg at eight-thirty, and the airplane just handed us two squawks. One dead standby attitude indicator. One dead nav radio." He looked at her sideways, and she recognized the look: not worry. *Curiosity.* The man was constitutionally incapable of encountering a malfunction without hearing a lesson plan assemble itself. "Walk it, Captain. I'll play the skeptic."

"Then get Manny on the phone first, because whichever way this walks, it ends at a mechanic."

---

They walked it in the flight deck, engines quiet, rain doing its Seattle murmur on the windshield, and Wren laid it out the way she'd have laid it out alone — except not alone, which was still novel enough to notice.

"Start with the frame," she said. "Ninety-one two-oh-five is the floor for what this airplane must carry. Day VFR list, night adds its items, and IFR — paragraph delta — adds the instrument suite. The memory crutch is GRABCARD: Generator or alternator. Radios — two-way comm and nav equipment *suitable for the route*. Attitude indicator. Ball — the slip-skid. Clock, with a sweep second hand or digital, installed. Altimeter, sensitive and adjustable. Rate of turn indicator. Directional gyro. That's the reg floor for IFR, on top of everything day and night VFR wanted."

"Skeptic says: the airplane has three attitude indicators. Two big glass ones and the little standby. The standby's sick, the primaries are fine. GRABCARD says 'attitude indicator,' singular. We've got two working. Why isn't this a shrug?"

"Because the floor isn't the ceiling, and this airplane doesn't get to shop at the floor. It's a pressurized turboprop flying Part one-thirty-five, certified with an equipment list, operated under an approved **MEL** — minimum equipment list. The moment an operator has an MEL, the MEL *is* the law for inoperative equipment. Not vibes, not GRABCARD, not 'we've got two more.' You look the item up. And I already know what it says, because I helped Griff proof the thing: standby attitude indicator, *required for dispatch under IFR*. No relief. It's the instrument we keep for the day the glass lies or the busses drop, and the certification logic won't let us launch into cloud with the last-ditch gyro drunk. So the airplane is grounded for IFR until it's fixed. That one's not a judgment call — that's just reading."

"And Nav Two?"

"Different answer, same book. MEL gives relief on the second nav radio — may be inoperative for day operations provided remaining navigation equipment is operational, placard it, defer it, note it, fly. We've got dual GPS and Nav One. Nav Two can ride to the shop's convenience." She pulled the deferral placards from the binder — INOPERATIVE, orange, the honest little flags of a well-run operation — and stuck one on the dead radio's display. "So this morning's lesson is that two squawks walked in together and left by different doors."

"Skeptic's follow-up, for the ghost of my Skyhawk students: no MEL. Sam's Bonanza breaks something. What's the walk?"

"Ninety-one two-thirteen, paragraph delta — the no-MEL flow, and it's a beautiful little decision tree. Is the busted thing required by the day's ninety-one two-oh-five list? By the airplane's certification equipment list? By an AD? By the POH's required equipment? If yes to any — no-go until fixed. If no to all — then either *remove* it and log that, or *deactivate* it and placard it INOPERATIVE, and a pilot or mechanic determines it's not a hazard to the aircraft. Then you may fly. The Bonanza's dead second nav comes off that tree in about ninety seconds. The Bonanza's dead attitude indicator, day VMC — trick question, it can pass the tree for *VFR*, and then the last gate still applies: is it a hazard for *this* flight? The tree ends at judgment on purpose."

"And the paperwork gospel, since we're here." He was grinning now, feeding her the lines, and she was letting him, and somewhere in the back of her mind a small internal auditor noted that this — being quizzed *by* him, for the pleasure of the recital — had become one of the good parts of the day. "Documents aboard, inspections current."

"ARROW: Airworthiness certificate, Registration, Radio license if we cross the border, Operating limitations — POH, placards — Weight and balance. Aboard, or the airplane's naked. Inspections, the AV1ATE stack: Annual, twelve months. VOR check, thirty days *if* you're navigating by VOR under IFR. One-hundred-hour if we're carrying for hire, which we are, sequenced with the annual. Altimeter and static system, twenty-four months, for IFR. Transponder, twenty-four months. ELT, twelve months, battery rules on the side. And the modern add-ons: ADS-B out required in the rule airspace, and databases — the GPS's data current, or verified, or we don't fly the procedures that lean on it. Current isn't just the pilot, Marsh. The airplane runs on calendars too."

"Which is the sentence I'm putting on your headstone."

"I'm being cremated so nobody can misquote me." She unclipped her harness. "Go tell Griff his eight-thirty just became a one-thirty. I'll call Manny."

He didn't move. "We'll tell Griff together," Theo said, mildly, like it was procedure — like it was already written somewhere that no one carried a no-go into the owner's office alone. It was such a small thing. Wren sat with her hand on the door latch a half-second longer than the latch required, updating instruments she didn't have names for.

At Cascade, three years ago, a man she'd trusted had stood in a doorway exactly like the one coming, and when the schedule got loud, he'd taken one professional step sideways out of the blast radius and let her be *difficult* all by herself.

"Together," she said. "Fine. Bring your skeptic."

---

Griff took it about the way a man takes a bill he knew was coming: badly, briefly, then arithmetic. He stood at the whiteboard with OKAFOR — ASTORIA 0830 written on it and did not say any of the things owners at worse companies said. He said them *around*, which was human.

"Manny can source a standby AI from Spokane by eleven. Install and ops check by one, one-thirty." He rubbed his jaw. "She's never late. She's going to be standing in that lobby at eight-fifteen with an itinerary."

"Then I'll tell her at eight-fifteen," Wren said.

"You could tell her it's weather," Griff said, not quite a suggestion, hating it as it left him. "There's an AIRMET up. Nobody would—"

"Clause One," said Theo, pleasantly, from the door.

Griff blinked. "What?"

"House rule," Wren said. "We don't lie to anyone wearing a headset, and we don't lie *about* the airplane to anyone riding in it. The truth is the airplane's standby instrument failed its preflight and her crew wouldn't fly her behind it. Griff — that's not the confession. That's the *brochure*."

There was a silence while the owner of Meridian Air Charter looked at his two pilots standing accidentally shoulder to shoulder in his doorway, and whatever he saw made him snort and cap the marker. "One-thirty," he said. "Go manage your client, *couple*."

Theo went ahead to intercept the lobby. Griff caught Wren's sleeve at the door.

"Hey. The thing I said — telling her it's weather." He looked, suddenly, every one of his fifty-eight years. "You know I know better. I flew freight for twenty-two years; I've grounded airplanes on Christmas Eve with the box trucks idling. I just—" he gestured at the whiteboard, the schedule, the whole leaning enterprise "—Ellen's money is in this, Wren. All of it. When she got sick we sold the house, and after — after, the insurance came, and everyone said be sensible, index funds, Marcus. And I bought a King Air, because the only version of being alive I've ever understood has a ramp attached." He let go of the sleeve, embarrassed. "So when a client's standing in my lobby deciding whether we exist — sometimes the freight dog panics. That's the whole confession. It won't happen again."

Wren looked at him — this soft-hearted, math-impaired, entirely decent man who had hired her when Cascade's version of her story was the only one in circulation, who had asked exactly zero questions about it, who paid Manny's kid's dental out of *miscellaneous* — and did something she almost never did with her own accord: she stepped in and hugged him, one firm freight-dog thump of a hug, brief as a touch-and-go.

"Ellen's money is in an airplane that just refused to fly with a broken instrument," she said. "That's the safest place it's ever been. Now go apologize to the whiteboard; you've been erasing it with your thumb again."

---

And Okafor — standing in the lobby at eight-fifteen with an itinerary, exactly as forecast — listened to Wren's plain-language account of a drunk gyroscope, looked from captain to first officer and back with her bright unhurried eyes, and said: "Let me make sure I have this. The airplane has three of these horizons. Two are perfect. The spare — the spare's spare — is dizzy. And you will not fly me until the *spare's spare* is sober."

"That's exactly it, ma'am."

"My last operator," Okafor said, "once flew me with an engine indication they described as *probably the gauge*." She put on her glasses, produced a phone, and began serenely rescheduling the Astoria lodge. "You'll find, Captain, that I am never angry at the truth before nine a.m. It's the ones who make me angry at it after dinner I fire. One-thirty. Theo, dear, there's a diner across the field. Take her to breakfast. That's not a request; you look like people who forget meals when machines misbehave."

Which is how, at nine in the morning on a grounded day, with no audience that mattered and Clause Two hanging unenforced in the air between them, Wren Hale ended up in a window booth at a ramp-side diner watching Theo Marsh order pancakes with the gravity of a man filing a flight plan.

Wren ordered the logger's special, a side of hash browns, and a cinnamon roll "for the table," and met his raised eyebrow with the flat stare of a woman prepared to defend her airspace.

"I said nothing."

"You inventoried. I heard you inventory." She handed the menu back to the waitress. "I ran freight in Alaska, Marsh. You eat when the food is in front of you, because the next chance is a weather product, not a promise. It's not an appetite. It's *doctrine*."

"For the record, I find the doctrine outstanding," Theo said. "I'm just saying the cinnamon roll isn't for the table. The table and I both know it."

"The table," said Wren, "can file a report," and the cinnamon roll, when it arrived, was not for the table.

---

They talked shop for the first twenty minutes because shop was the safe frequency. The VOR check, mostly — since Nav One's thirty days expired that week and they'd need it legal for the coastal work where the GPS NOTAMs had been ugly lately.

"Ways to do it," Theo said, building a syllabus out of syrup and cutlery. "Best: a VOT — the test signal, on the field at the ones that have it. Tune it, and regardless of where you're parked it reads three-sixty FROM or one-eighty TO — the old *Cessna one-eighty* memory hook — allowable error plus-or-minus four degrees. Next: a ground checkpoint — painted circle on the ramp with a published radial, four degrees. Airborne checkpoint, six. No checkpoint anywhere? Make your own over a charted landmark on a Victor airway centerline, more than twenty miles out — six degrees. And the one nobody remembers is legal: dual VOR cross-check — tune both receivers to the same station, and if the bearings agree within *four degrees of each other*, both pass. Then the part everyone actually flunks: *log it.* Date, place, bearing error, signature. The check didn't happen unless the ink happened."

"DPBS," Wren said. "Every student I've ever met says 'D-P-B-S' and then writes it nowhere."

"I make mine sing it. There's a jingle."

"There is not a jingle."

"There's a jingle, and you will never hear it, because you'd grade it." He surrendered the last pancake to her side of the table in the manner of a man paying a toll, and then the conversation, unsupervised, wandered off the airway.

It was the booth's fault, she decided later. Booths have no checklist.

He told her — without her asking, which she was learning was how he did his own shoebox — about the airline class date. Two years on a regional's list, the email finally landing, class starting in Charlotte on a Monday. And on the Thursday before it, his father face-down between the apples and the onions at a Safeway in Des Moines, and the phrase *left middle cerebral artery* entering the family vocabulary, and Theo on the phone that Sunday night with a recruiter who was kind about it and clear about it: defer and lose the seniority number, or come to class.

"I keep waiting to regret it," he said, turning his coffee cup a quarter-turn, a man checking trim. "Everyone promises I will. *You gave up the career track.* But I sat in that hospital doing the math and it wasn't close, Wren. Seniority numbers come out every year. There's one of him." He shrugged, palms up, the same gesture he used for weather. "Some decisions don't have a brief. You just fly the airplane you're in."

Wren looked at him across the wreckage of breakfast — this man who walked in rain, who logged his own deficiencies in neat remarks columns, who'd stood in Griff's doorway and said *Clause One* like it was load-bearing steel — and felt something in her chest do exactly what the standby gyro had done that morning.

Lean. Wander off level.

She re-erected it immediately, because unlike the gyro she had discipline, and filed the entire event under *low blood sugar*, and ate the last of his pancake as evidence.

"Your dad," she said, instead of anything on the actual squawk list. "When he's flying the Bonanza again. Tell him I want a ride."

Theo looked up, surprised open — and there it was again, the approach-lights thing, except this time she was the one doing it, and knowing you're doing it, it turns out, does not help you stop.

"He'll say yes," Theo said. "Fair warning: he'll quiz you on the pre-buy he did in 1998. It has a *narrative arc*."

"I'd expect nothing less from your family."

---

Manny's part arrived at eleven-oh-five; the install ran long the way installs do; and at one-forty, N135MA lifted off runway one-four-left with a sober standby horizon, a placarded Nav Two, a relieved owner shrinking on the ramp, and a client who spent the taxi-out reading the new INOPERATIVE placard aloud to her assistant in the tone of a woman reading a favorable review.

Level at one-seven-thousand, coasting toward the Astoria arrival with the evening building gold over the Pacific, Wren keyed the intercom for Clause Three.

"Debrief. Short form."

"Two squawks, two doors," Theo said. "The MEL is the law where an MEL exists — look it up, don't vibe it: standby AI, no relief, grounded; Nav Two, relief with conditions, placard and defer. No MEL, it's the ninety-one two-thirteen tree: required by two-oh-five, cert list, AD, or POH? Fix it or leave it. Otherwise remove or deactivate, placard, and someone competent calls it no-hazard — and the tree ends at judgment on purpose. GRABCARD is the IFR floor, ARROW rides in the seat pocket, AV1ATE runs the calendars — annual, VOR thirty days, hundred-hour for hire, altimeter-static and transponder each twenty-four months, ELT twelve — plus ADS-B and current databases, because the airplane has currency requirements too. VOR check: VOT three-sixty FROM, four degrees; ground four, airborne six, airway centerline six, dual cross-check four *between* receivers — and DPBS in the book or it never happened."

"And the other thing."

He glanced over. "Which other thing, Captain?"

"The doorway." She kept her eyes on the panel, because some transmissions only go out on that heading. "At Cascade, when I made calls like that, I made them alone, and the room made sure I felt it. Today it was — not that." The gold light moved across the glareshield. "Noted for the record. That's all."

Theo was quiet for a moment. Then, in his checklist voice, the one she now knew he also used for things that weren't on any checklist: "Standby's required equipment, Wren. No relief on that item. It's in the book."

She did not ask which book, on the grounds that the answer might require a response, and she was flying. The airplane descended toward the river and the sea, all its instruments erect and agreeing, and the two pilots of Meridian Three-Five flew the arrival in a perfectly professional silence that was, by Wren's own honest instrument reading, running about a half-dot off center — a deviation she noted, declined to correct, and did not report to anyone.

*End of Chapter Five.*
