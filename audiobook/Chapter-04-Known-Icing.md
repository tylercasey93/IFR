# Chapter Four — Known Icing

*Theo*

Theo was on the ladder with a flashlight at six in the morning, running a bare thumb along the leading edge of the left wing, when he decided that the King Air 350 was the most honest piece of equipment at Meridian, himself included.

The airplane didn't pretend. Its deice boots were right there on the leading edges, black and matte against the polished wing, and either the rubber was sound or it wasn't; either the little stall-strip areas were clean or they weren't. You couldn't fake a preflight to a wing. The wing kept its own logbook.

"Talk to me, Marsh." Wren's voice floated up from beneath the nose, where she was doing her own walk-around of his walk-around, which four days ago he'd have called micromanagement and now understood as liturgy. "You're inspecting like a man composing a lecture."

"Occupational disease." He came down the ladder into the ramp lights and the six-degree drizzle. Boeing Field sat under its standard-issue October lid, but today the lid had ambition: the Zulu AIRMET called for moderate rime between six and fourteen thousand from the Cascades east, freezing level around five, tops near flight level one-eight-zero. Between Seattle and Sun Valley, Idaho, where Adaeze Okafor intended to inspect a lodge and, Theo suspected, their body language, stood a hundred and fifty miles of exactly that forecast. "You want the lecture or the checklist?"

"I want the lecture *as* the checklist. If you're going to be a check airman someday, brief me like one."

*Someday* did something warm and complicated in his chest, which he set aside for later filing. "Okay. Ground rule one, the two words everybody swaps at parties: **anti-ice** keeps ice from forming — it's preventive, you turn it on *before* you need it. Pitot heat. Prop heat. Windshield heat. Engine anti-ice. **Deice** removes ice that's already formed — it's corrective. The boots. You let the wing pick up a measurable layer, then you break it off. Different philosophies, different switches, and the accident reports are full of people who ran one on the other's schedule."

"Walk the airplane. Point."

He pointed. "Boots — wings, horizontal stab. Engine bleed air inflates them in a cycle, rubber cracks the ice, slipstream carries it off. Preflight: no cuts, no delamination, no chunks of yesterday still stuck to them. Prop boots — electric heating elements on the blade roots, and in the run-up we watch the ammeter cycle, because a prop shedding ice unevenly will shake the fillings out of your teeth and the client's champagne off its shelf. Pitot masts, heated — the burn test on preflight, carefully, because they will brand you. Static ports clear. Stall warning vane, heated. Windshield heat, left side hot for the approach. Fuel vents, heated, which nobody remembers until the day the tanks start breathing wrong. And these—" he crouched under the intake "—inertial separators. Icing's sneakiest door into a turboprop isn't the wing, it's the engine's throat. Separator deflects the airflow so ice and slush can't make the turn into the compressor; the air bends, the ice doesn't. Costs a little performance when deployed. Cheap at the price."

"And what makes this airplane different from the one you instructed in?"

"Certification. This airplane is approved for flight into known icing — FIKI. It was *demonstrated* into icing, the whole system is redundant and de-iced in a pattern the certification believed in. The Skyhawk had a heated pitot tube and my sincere apologies. Which is the real point of ground rule two: FIKI is not a force field. It's permission to *transit* icing while executing a plan to leave it. Known icing certification never made ice fall in love with anybody. Severe icing — the kind in a SIGMET, the kind with supercooled large droplets, freezing rain, freezing drizzle — is outside everybody's certification, ours included. SLD paints ice *behind* the boots where nothing can reach it, and the tell is moisture hitting the side windows aft of where the heat lives. You see that, you don't manage it. You leave. Climb, descend, one-eighty — the escape is the procedure."

Wren stood in the drizzle, hands in her jacket pockets, and he watched her do the thing she did — audit the answer against some private ledger — and then she said, "Ice types. For the record."

"Rime: small droplets, freeze on contact, milky and brittle, builds forward into the airstream like coral. Rough but relatively light. Clear: big droplets that splash and run back before freezing — glassy, heavy, hard to see, follows the contour and sneaks aft. Mixed: both, because the atmosphere contains multitudes. Clear is the one that lies to you, rime is the one that ruins the airfoil's manners, and all three of them are stealing lift and adding drag the entire time. The airplane stalls earlier and uglier with ice on it. Which is why—"

"—the numbers move," Wren finished. "Approach speeds come up with residual ice. The POH has a whole section and we will be flying the airplane the POH describes, not the one in the brochure." She turned toward the FBO, where a black SUV was delivering their client to the coffee bar. "Good lecture. One correction."

"Hit me."

"You said the escape is the procedure. In this crew, the escape is *briefed before the entry*. Before we go into that layer today, you and I will say out loud what the way out is — the altitude that's warm, the altitude that's clear, and who's asking Center for it. Alaska taught me that you don't shop for exits from inside the building." She said it evenly, but something moved under it, a current below the surface analysis, and Theo — who had been collecting Wren Hale weather for two weeks now — logged the PIREP and didn't chase it.

Not yet.

---

They launched at eight with Okafor and her assistant aboard, ginger tea secured, and Boeing Tower handed them to Departure in the base of the overcast at eleven hundred feet.

The world went white and stayed white.

"Pitot heat's been on since before takeoff," Theo said, running the flow, "windshield heat on, prop heat on, separators deployed. Boots armed and waiting for a reason." The outside air temperature ticked down through plus two as they climbed. At five thousand three hundred it kissed zero, and the first of it appeared — a thin white line of rime building on the wiper arm bolt, the airplane's cheapest and most reliable ice detector.

"Airframe's picking it up," Wren said. "Wiper bolt, and the boot's starting to frost. Light rime."

"Boots?"

"Cycle them." She was flying by hand, which he noted; the autopilot could fly this climb perfectly, and that was exactly why she wasn't letting it. "And say the reasoning, because Ms. Okafor's assistant asked me at the counter if the wings ever get icy and I told her you give a wonderful seminar."

He keyed the boots and watched the leading edge swell, crack the white line, shed it into the slipstream. "The reasoning: run the boots as soon as you're accreting, and keep them cycling. There's a ghost story that gets handed down — *wait for half an inch or the ice will bridge over the boot and inflate uselessly underneath a shell*. Ice bridging. For modern boots it's essentially a myth — the guidance for years has been don't wait, activate at the first indication. The half-inch patience play is how people turned light icing into an emergency out of respect for their grandfather's airplane. The other reasoning: the *autopilot*. George is a wonderful pilot and a pathological liar in ice. He'll hold altitude with creeping nose-up trim while the wing degrades, masking the whole slide, and then hand you the airplane at the stall with the trim set for a different aircraft than the one you now have. In sustained icing you either hand-fly or you watch the trim and airspeed like it owes you money. Airspeed's the confession: level flight, same power, five knots slow, then eight — the ice is telling you what it's costing."

"Which is why I'm hand-flying."

"Which is why you're hand-flying," he agreed, "and why I'm watching the trend line so you don't have to love it alone."

The exchange sat there, doing more work than it was assigned. Wren's eyes stayed on the panel. "Exit brief," she said. "Before we're deeper. Tops reported one-seven-five by the Horizon flight twenty minutes ago — that's our up. Warm air is five thousand and below west of the crest — that's our down, terrain permitting, which east of the crest it won't be, so the *down* option expires at the mountains. If it turns moderate-plus or anything touches the side windows, we take the up, immediately, and I'll trade Ms. Okafor's smooth ride for wing ice every day of the week. Ask for one-nine-zero now, actually — let's not negotiate from inside the layer."

He asked. Center gave them flight level one-nine-zero without discussion, the way Center does when the word *icing* is in the request, and the King Air went up through the last thousand feet of cloud shedding rime in fingernail-sized flakes, and broke out into a blue so hard and bright it felt like impertinence.

Behind them, the cabin speaker chimed. "*What*," Okafor inquired, "was that delightful crunching? Like a horse walking on gravel."

Theo took it, because teaching for the client had become his instrument of the act. "Ice, ma'am, letting go of the wing. The leading edges wear inflatable rubber — we let a thin layer of ice form and then break its grip, and the wind does the rest. You'll hear it in cycles when we're in cold cloud. It's designed, it's inspected, and Captain Hale watches it like it's a student pilot."

"And is there," the voice was delighted, "*horse-gravel* on my side of the airplane?"

"Not anymore. We climbed into the sunshine to spend less time collecting it. Costs a bit of bumpy climb, buys a clean airplane. That's the whole art of it, honestly — ice management is mostly the humility to change altitude early."

"Marvelous. Wren, darling, he explains things the way my husband used to argue: until it's romantic. I hope you never get used to it."

Wren keyed the cabin mic. "I'm building up a tolerance, ma'am."

"See that you don't."

The speaker clicked off. The flight deck held a careful, sun-flooded silence, at altitude, on top, two crew members not getting used to anything at a hundred and eighty knots.

---

Over the Columbia basin, in the smooth air where the overcast had shredded into scattered cotton, Wren gave the airplane to the autopilot, flexed her hands, and told him an Alaska story. He hadn't asked. That was, he suspected, the load-bearing condition.

"Cascade freight, before Meridian — before that, Alaska," she said. "Chieftains. Piston twin, boots, prop heat, no FIKI paperwork worth anything, and a company culture where the schedule was the weather brief. My second winter, a captain I flew with — good hands, terrible judgment, the combination that kills — took the Ketchikan run into a Zulu that had SLD written all over it. Freezing drizzle at the surface. He'd flown it a hundred times. Ice behind the boots, ice on the spinner, windshield heat fighting for a hole the size of a playing card. He made it. Landed twenty knots fast on a wet runway with the stall horn wired to a wing that had quit being the wing in the manual, and taxied in like a hero, and the company bought the fuel and sent the next guy into the same system an hour later."

"What happened to the next guy?"

"She turned around at the first side-window splash," Wren said. "The next guy was me." She watched the scattered deck slide under the nose. "The company called it costing them a client. I called it the day I started writing my own minimums card. Everyone in that hangar was *current*, Marsh. Currency was never the problem. The problem was a room full of people who thought the airplane's certification was a personality trait." Her thumb moved on the yoke, absent, like a pilot feeling for trim. "So when you briefed the exits before the entry today without me making you — that's the whole hiring decision, right there. That's the thing I can't teach in six months if it isn't installed."

Theo looked at her profile against the Idaho-bound sky and understood, with the clarity of an annunciator light, two things at once: that Wren Hale had just handed him a page from the shoebox — he didn't know about the shoebox yet, but he could feel the shape of an archive behind the story — and that Clause Two, *no audience, no performance*, had a defect neither of them had briefed.

There was no audience at flight level one-nine-zero.

And whatever this was, it was not a performance.

---

Sun Valley did what mountain strips do to flatland assumptions: Hailey's runway sat in a slot between browning-gold hills, the approach was a study in terrain respect, and the density altitude was mercifully asleep in October. They came down the valley with the boots quiet — the layer had stayed west of the crest, as forecast, for once — and rolled out into cold sunshine that smelled like sage and money.

At the FBO, Okafor's lodge people had sent a Suburban and a photographer — *a photographer* — because the lodge's marketing had heard its owner was arriving by charter and wanted ramp shots for the winter brochure.

"Oh, this is perfect," Okafor announced, sweeping down the airstair. "The crew too. The *couple*. Stand by the nose. No, together. Theo, take her hand, you're not at a deposition."

There followed three seconds of crew resource management conducted entirely by eyebrow. *Clause Two,* said Wren's. *Audience present. Performance authorized.* And Theo took her hand — cold fingers, competent calluses, a grip that started formal and then, when the photographer said *lean in a little*, adjusted itself into something that fit — and he stood by the nose of N135MA in the Idaho light while a stranger with a lens told a lie into permanence.

Here was the thing about the hand.

He'd flown with her for two weeks. He'd watched those hands run boots and brief exits, seen them steady on the yoke in the white nothing at eleven thousand feet with ice crunching off the leading edges. He knew already — knew it the way you know the airplane's sounds before you can name them — that he trusted those hands with the physical fact of his life.

Holding one of them was somehow entirely different information.

"Got it," said the photographer. "One more. Captain, smile like he just said something funny."

"He never says anything funny," Wren said, and smiled — the real one, the approach-lights one — and the shutter went and Theo stood there in the sage-smelling cold experiencing what he would describe to his father that Sunday, under oath of family, as *a compressor stall of the heart*.

Sam would laugh his lopsided laugh and say: *Son. That's not a malfunction.*

---

The lodge people insisted on feeding the crew before the return leg, and that was how Theo and Wren ended up at a long table of reclaimed timber under a wall of elk antlers, with Adaeze Okafor holding court over squash soup and telling, because the photographer's work had put her in the mood, the story of how she was married.

"Lagos, 1978," she began, in the voice of a woman unwrapping something kept in tissue. "I was twenty-three, ferocious, and booked on a flight to London for a job interview at a hotel company that would eventually be foolish enough to teach me their entire business. And at the airport — chaos. Harmattan haze, the whole coast socked in, everything grounded. You know the harmattan?" She aimed the question at Theo, examiner-style.

"Dust off the Sahara," he said. "Dry season. It can take visibility to nothing for days."

"For *days*," Okafor agreed, with relish. "And in that terminal, in that sea of furious important men shouting at airline clerks about their very important schedules, there was one man — one — sitting calmly on his suitcase, reading a paperback western, with his tie already loosened. And I asked him, because I was furious too and wanted company in it, *'Aren't you angry? We could be here for days.'* And he looked out at the haze, and looked at me, and said—" she laid her hand flat on the timber, setting the words down like cards "—*'Madam, the weather is not asking our opinion. It is only asking our plans. I plan to read this book, and then I plan to buy you dinner.'*"

Chidinma-to-be generations of the family had clearly grown up on this story; the assistant was mouthing the last line along with her.

"Forty-one years," Okafor said. "Three continents. Forty hotels. And I will tell you the thing I have told every young person at every one of my tables since: I did not fall in love with his face, though it was a good face. I fell in love with a man who did not argue with weather. Who looked at a closed sky and adjusted his *plans* instead of his *character*." Her eyes moved, leisurely, entirely without innocence, from Theo to Wren and back. "It is the rarest quality. Most people, you understand, are only ever one canceled flight away from showing you exactly who they are."

"And the interview?" Wren asked. "London?"

"I took a later flight and got the job anyway." She waved it off as trivia. "The interview was never the important departure that week." She lifted her teacup — ginger, no ice, even here — and delivered the benediction that Theo would hear again, months later, on a staircase in the same valley, without understanding until much too late that it had been aimed the first time too: "Everything that matters in my life, children, started with weather. Weather is just the world asking whether you meant it."

Walking back to the airplane through the cold sage light, Wren was quiet for most of the ramp. Then: "She's telling us something."

"She's telling us a nice story about her husband."

"She's a hotelier who reads rooms for a living, Marsh, and she aimed that moral like a localizer." Wren pulled her preflight gloves on, one sharp tug each. "*One canceled flight away from showing you who you are.* She's watching for the canceled flight."

"Then we'd better hope I'm delightful in a crisis."

"You briefed the exits before the entry today without being told," Wren said, to the cowling, in the tone of a woman reading an oil quantity. "So the preliminary data's not a disaster." She started up the airstair, then paused, two steps up, without turning around. "Don't put that in your file."

"Too late. Framing it."

"It was a *preliminary* finding—"

"Getting it engraved. 'Not a disaster.' — W. Hale," Theo told the empty ramp happily, and followed his captain up the stairs.

---

They flew home empty that evening, repositioning under a high cold clear sky, the layer over the Cascades collapsed to broken shreds with the sun going down through them like a rescue. Somewhere over the Columbia, Wren looked over.

"Debrief. Clause Three. Ice edition."

"Anti-ice prevents — on early: pitot, props, windshield, engine, separators. Deice removes — boots, cycled at *first* accretion; bridging is folklore for modern boots and patience is how you turn light ice into an event. FIKI is permission to transit with a plan, not a force field; severe ice and SLD are outside everyone's certification, and moisture on the side windows means leave now — climb, descend, or turn around, decided *before* entry, not shopped for inside. Autopilot masks the bleed — hand-fly or watch trim and airspeed, because airspeed is the ice confessing. Ice moves the numbers: earlier stall, uglier stall, faster approach with residue. And the whole art—" he glanced at her "—is the humility to change altitude early."

"And the Alaska rule?"

"Certification isn't a personality trait." He said it back quietly, the way you handle borrowed things. "Currency was never the problem."

Wren nodded, eyes forward, mountains going blue-black under them. The photograph was already in both their phones — Okafor had forwarded it from the car with a caption of one single knowing emoji — and neither of them had mentioned it, and the unmentioned thing rode home in the flight deck like a third crew member.

"For the record," she said, over the top of descent, in her checklist voice, which he was learning was the voice she kept for things that weren't checklist items at all, "you're allowed to be funny. Occasionally. Off-camera."

"Copy," Theo said. "Occasionally funny. Off-camera."

"Don't read it back like a clearance."

"You issued it like one."

And Wren Hale laughed — second documented occurrence, witnessed, at eighteen thousand feet with the boots quiet and the sun gone — and Theo flew the descent checklist and privately amended his personal minimums to include a line no card had a column for.

*End of Chapter Four.*
