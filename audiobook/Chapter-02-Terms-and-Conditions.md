# Chapter Two — Terms and Conditions

*Theo*

Theo Marsh had learned most of what he knew about love from weather briefings, though it would take him the better part of a season to figure that out.

The morning after the Okafor trip, he sat at his father's kitchen table in Des Moines — the Washington one, the little bluff-top town south of the airport that nobody east of the Rockies believed existed — watching Sam Marsh butter toast with his left hand because the right one still argued about fine motor work. The kitchen window looked at gray. The gray looked back.

"So let me get this straight," Sam said. "You've been at this outfit four days."

"Three and a half."

"And you have a girlfriend."

"I have a *fake* girlfriend," Theo said. "Which is a different rating. Separate checkride."

Sam laughed, which came out lopsided these days and was still the best sound Theo knew. Eighteen months ago the stroke had dropped him in the produce aisle of the Safeway between the apples and the onions, and for a while the doctors had used words like *significant* and *guarded*, and Theo had walked out of a regional airline class date he'd waited two years for, and neither of them had ever once discussed whether that had been the right call. It had been the only call. Some decisions don't have a brief.

"The captain," Sam said. "The one you told me about on the phone. The one who quizzed you on safety pilot requirements like it was a custody hearing."

"Wren Hale."

"You like her."

"I respect her."

"Son," Sam said, "I was married forty years. I know what respect sounds like, and it doesn't sound like a man saying a woman's name the way you just said that one." He pointed the butter knife. "Eat something. And when you go in there today, whatever they're asking you to do — get it in writing."

---

Meridian's office at ten a.m. held the specific silence of a small company waiting to hear how it dies. Priya was at dispatch pretending to type. Griff was in the doorway of his office holding two cups of coffee like collateral. And Wren was already in the briefing room with the big monitor lit — surface analysis on the left half, the graphical forecast tool on the right — because of course she was. Theo was beginning to understand that Wren Hale arrived early to everything the way other people locked doors: habit, armor, and a message to anyone checking.

"Marsh." She didn't look up. "Sit. Ceiling's coming down tomorrow and Griff has something to say that he's been not saying for ten minutes."

Griff set the coffees down, sat, and clasped his hands like a man about to describe a mid-air. "Okafor's people called this morning. She wants the season on contract — every week through spring, guaranteed hours, rates we haven't seen since fuel was cheap. It saves us. That's not a figure of speech. It is the difference between Meridian existing in June and me selling that airplane at a loss." He took a breath. "The contract language her assistant sent over specifies — and I want you to know I did not suggest this — *the current crew*. Both of you. By name."

"And the rest of it," Wren said, flat.

"There's no clause that says you have to be a couple," Griff said carefully. "There is a client who believes you are, who chose us because of it, and whose assistant asked me this morning, quote, how long you two have been together, because Ms. Okafor was curious." He looked at his hands. "I panicked and said it was new."

"You said it was *new*."

"I said you were very private people."

Theo watched Wren's jaw do something geological. He decided, with a confidence he did not fully possess, to jump in front of it.

"Okay," Theo said. "Before anybody sets a course — can I say what everybody's thinking? If we tell her the truth today, she's embarrassed, the contract's gone, and it isn't the lie that kills it. It's that we let her believe it for a whole flight and through a phone call. That window's closed. Which means—" he tapped the table "—the truth costs the same whether we pay now or in April. And in April, this company's alive."

"That's a rationalization," Wren said.

"It's a *good* rationalization," Theo said. "Also, in April, Ms. Okafor has had a season of flights she loved, and if she ever finds out, the story is two employees who didn't want to humiliate a client and kept a struggling shop's lights on. I can live in that story. Can you?"

Wren looked at him for a long moment across the briefing table. On the monitor behind her, the West Coast sat under a broad green smear of forecast IFR, a low spinning off the Gulf of Alaska with its fronts trailing like untied laces.

"Terms," she said. "Written terms, or no."

Griff exhaled a decade. Theo thought: *Dad's going to be insufferable about this.*

"Then I'll leave you two to—" Griff gestured vaguely, already retreating "—negotiate," and shut the door on them, and the weather.

---

Here is how you build a fake relationship, if you're the two of them:

You do it during a weather briefing, because there's a trip tomorrow — Seattle to Portland to Santa Barbara, Okafor's first contract leg — and the planning has to happen anyway, and neither of you can sit in silence with the other kind of conversation. So it gets braided. A clause, a chart. A term, a TAF. Later, Theo would be unable to remember the agreement without smelling laminate and dry-erase marker, unable to hear a forecast without wanting to make Wren Hale a promise.

Wren pulled a legal pad over, wrote OPERATING AGREEMENT at the top, underlined it twice, and said, "Standard brief first. Big picture. Go."

Theo took the screen. "All right. Sources, since you're going to grade me anyway: everything we're about to look at is the government stuff — aviationweather-dot-gov, the same data a Flight Service briefer reads to you if you call, and if we want it narrated by a human with legal standing, we call or we hit the online briefing and it's logged either way. The EFB pulls the identical products; the app is a viewer, not an oracle. If it's a machine-made forecast, the machine got it from the same models. Nobody's got secret weather."

"Why does the logging matter?"

"Because ninety-one one-oh-three says preflight action means *all available information* for an IFR flight — weather reports *and* forecasts, fuel, alternatives if the flight can't be completed as planned, known delays. If anyone ever asks whether we briefed, the record answers before I have to." He clicked the surface analysis. "Big picture: low off Vancouver Island, occluding. Cold front trails southwest, crosses the coast tomorrow midday. Behind it, showers and a freezing level that comes down to about six thousand. Ahead of it, warm sector — that's Portland in the morning, low stratus, drizzle, classic."

Wren wrote on the legal pad: *Clause One. No lying to anyone wearing a headset. ATC, dispatch, Voss, logbooks — the lie exists for exactly one audience: the client.*

She spun the pad to face him. Theo read it, took the pen, and wrote *agreed — T.M.* like signing a release. "Terminal forecasts," he said, sliding the pad back. "Boeing Field first."

She pulled the TAF. "Read it like I'm a student."

"Cruel. Fine." He leaned in. "KBFI, issued this morning, valid twenty-four hours. Through tonight: wind south-southeast ten knots, visibility better than six, ceiling broken fifteen hundred. From oh-six hundred Zulu — remember TAFs live in Zulu, and Zulu doesn't observe your feelings or daylight time — becoming ovc niner hundred, minus-RA. Light rain, nine hundred overcast for our departure. Then a TEMPO group midday: temporarily, ceilings five hundred, visibility three in rain. TEMPO means less than an hour at a whack, on and off, within that window — it is not the base forecast, but you plan fuel like you'll meet it, because you personally will meet every TEMPO ever issued."

"And a PROB30 group, if there were one?"

"Thirty percent probability — briefing-room translation: *the forecaster flinched.* You don't get to use a PROB30 to make a plan legal; you get to use it to make a plan smart."

"Portland."

"KPDX: morning, ovc four hundred, two miles in drizzle and mist — warm sector special. Improving after the front to broken twelve hundred. Santa Barbara's the pretty one — marine layer burns off by eighteen Zulu, then clear, then the layer slides back in around sunset like it never left. Coastal California is a tide chart with an ATIS."

Wren almost smiled — he saw the approach lights of it, right at minimums — and wrote: *Clause Two. Performance is for the audience. No audience, no performance. In the flight deck we are captain and first officer, full stop.*

Theo read it twice. Something in Clause Two stung in a way he elected not to examine, so he initialed it and said, "AIRMETs. You want them by name?"

"You're the mnemonic guy."

"AIRMETs come in three flavors, and the old-timers still call them Sierra, Tango, Zulu, which conveniently is also the order in which they ruin your day. **Sierra** — IFR conditions and mountain obscuration. S for *socked in*. **Tango** — turbulence, T for *turbulence*, the FAA got tired that day. Moderate turb, sustained surface winds over thirty. **Zulu** — icing and freezing level, Z because *zero degrees* is where the trouble starts. They're issued for wide areas — the graphical versions live in the GFA now — and they're for everybody, not just the big iron. Tomorrow we've got Sierra over the coastal valleys through midday and Zulu for moderate ice, freezing level six to eight thousand, which for a King Air with boots is a planning item, and for the Skyhawks I used to teach in is a wall."

"SIGMETs."

"Bigger, meaner, rarer. Plain SIGMET — severe ice, severe turb, dust, ash: the stuff that's hazardous to *every* aircraft. Convective SIGMET — thunderstorm business: embedded storms, lines, big areas, and it implies everything a thunderstorm brings, so it doesn't bother itemizing. If a convective SIGMET and my route ever intersect, the route was wrong."

Wren wrote: *Clause Three. Debrief daily. Five minutes minimum, honest, both directions. What worked, what didn't. This clause covers the arrangement AND the flying.* She hesitated, pen down, then added: *Nobody saves up grievances for April.*

"That one's going to be load-bearing," Theo said.

"Everything on this pad is load-bearing. PIREPs — why do I care more about one PIREP than three forecasts?"

"Because a forecast is a promise and a PIREP is a fact. Some poor soul in a Caravan at seven thousand over the Cascades saying *light rime, tops niner-five* is worth more than any model run — it's ground truth, it's fresh, and it tells me tops, bases, ride, and whether the Zulu is honest. And we *give* them, not just take them — urgent ones especially: severe ice, severe turb, low-level wind shear. The system runs on pilots telling the truth out loud." He shrugged. "It's a whole philosophy, if you think about it."

"Don't get poetic before noon, Marsh."

"FIS-B, then, since we've got it on the panel and Ms. Okafor will ask why the pretty picture disagrees with the window. Datalink radar is *history*, not weather — the mosaic can be five, ten, fifteen minutes old by the time it paints, and a cell can move miles and grow teeth in that time. Strategic tool: route around the red from far away. Never a tactical tool: never thread a gap by the picture. Same for the datalink METARs — check the timestamp like it owes you money."

Wren nodded slowly, and then did the thing he was learning meant the real conversation had arrived: she squared the legal pad, and looked at him with the whole instrument panel of her attention.

"Clause Four," she said. "Either one of us can end this. Any time, any reason, or no reason. You say the words *going missed* and it's over — the act, the arrangement, all of it — and the other one doesn't ask why. Not that day, not ever. We tell Griff together, we tell the client whatever kind version is true enough, and nobody—" the pen stopped for a half-second "—nobody makes anybody stay."

The room was quiet. Down the hall Priya's keyboard had stopped pretending.

Theo took the pad. Wrote: *agreed — T.M.* And because the silence had weight, and because he was who he was, he added out loud: "For the record, that's just a missed approach clause. Every good procedure has one. It's not pessimism — you brief it *because* you intend the approach to work. You know the stats: the miss you briefed is the one you fly well."

"Is that another mnemonic?"

"No, that one's just true."

Wren tore the sheet off, folded it in half, and — this part he'd remember — didn't file it in the trip folder. She put it in the left chest pocket of her flight jacket, the one with the zipper, the pocket where he'd watched her stow the fuel receipt and her father's old E6-B and nothing else.

"Personal minimums," she said, standing, all business again. "Last item, then you're buying lunch, because apparently we're dating and I have standards."

"You want mine?"

"I want to tell you the company's, and then I want yours, because tomorrow morning that TEMPO group turns Boeing Field to five hundred and three, and legal isn't the question — the airplane and I can fly a five-hundred-foot ceiling all day. The question is where *your* line is, four days into a new airplane, so I know it before we're at the hold short line instead of finding out at decision altitude." She pulled her own kneeboard card and laid it on the table, and it was exactly what he'd have bet: a printed grid, personal weather minimums by role and recency, reviewed and initialed, dated the first of every month. "Regulations are the floor," she said. "This is the house I actually live in."

Theo looked at the card. At the neat initials, twelve months of them, no gaps. It was the single most Wren Hale document in existence, and he was seized by the sudden, inconvenient, entirely real desire to know what the rest of the house looked like — what she kept in the other pockets, what the sentimental clutter of her would be if she ever let anyone in past the entryway.

"My line," he said, instead of any of that. "This week: I don't fly us into a TEMPO at minimums as pilot flying. Next month, ask me again. The line's supposed to move — that's what makes it a training program instead of a fence."

Wren picked up her card, and this time the smile actually landed, brief and real, and worse than any weather.

"Look at that," she said. "He briefs *and* he learns. Buy me a sandwich, sweetheart. We're on the clock."

She was out the door before he could decide whether the endearment had been for practice, for Priya's benefit at the dispatch desk, or — this was the theory he shelved fastest — a test flight of something with no audience at all.

---

The trouble with Adaeze Okafor, Theo decided the next morning at altitude, was that she conducted romance the way check airmen conducted orals: warmly, patiently, and with follow-up questions.

They'd briefed her at the airplane — Wren's crisp thirty seconds: ceilings low in Portland, smooth ride on top at flight level two-one-oh, forty minutes gate to gate — and Okafor had listened with her eyes closed like it was chamber music, then buckled in and waited for the seatbelt chime like a woman waiting for intermission to end. The moment the climb checklist was done and the speaker came alive, she began.

"So. Theo. How did you first know?"

Theo, who had spent the entire climb rehearsing weather answers, went blank on the only subject he was contractually required to know. In the left seat, Wren's mouth twitched with what he chose to interpret as support.

"The briefing," he said, finally, honestly. "First morning. She quizzed me on my own currency before she'd let me touch her airplane — chapter and verse, six approaches in six months, holding, the whole catechism — and I remember thinking, this is a person who will never once lie to me about anything that matters." He was aware, as he said it, of the legal pad folded in a jacket pocket eighteen inches to his left, and of the exact shape of what he was and wasn't saying. "Everything else was details."

There was a silence from the cabin speaker. Then, delighted and suspiciously damp: "Marcus said you were private people. That is the least private thing I've ever heard. Captain, your turn."

"I'm flying, ma'am."

"You're on autopilot, dear, I can hear it."

Wren shot him a look — *this is your fault* — and keyed the cabin mic. "He walked to the airplane in the rain," she said. "First day. Didn't run, didn't hunch, didn't complain. Just — walked, like the weather was information instead of an insult." A beat. "I've hired a lot of pilots who run."

Okafor sighed happily. Theo looked out the right window at the tops sliding by, at Mount Rainier standing in the southeast like a witness, and informed himself, in his firmest instructor voice, that everything anyone had said in the last three minutes had been for the audience.

Clause Two existed for a reason. No audience, no performance.

He just couldn't remember, entirely, which parts had been the performance.

---

They put the airplane to bed in Santa Barbara gold — marine layer gone offshore, waiting for sunset like a courteous predator — and walked to the FBO with the day's paperwork. At the counter, Wren stopped, squared her shoulders like a woman honoring a signature, and said: "Clause Three. Debrief. Go."

"Now?"

"Every day, Marsh. That was the deal. Five minutes. What'd we learn?"

And so the first daily debrief of the arrangement was conducted standing at a rental counter in California, and it went to weather, because the flying was the part they could say out loud:

"TAFs are Zulu," Theo said. "TEMPO is a threat, not a base forecast. PROB30 can't make a plan legal, only smart. AIRMETs: Sierra socked-in, Tango turbulence, Zulu ice at the zero line — wide areas, everybody's problem. SIGMETs for the severe stuff; convective SIGMETs mean the route was wrong. A PIREP beats a forecast because it's a fact, and we owe the system our own. Datalink radar is history — strategic, never tactical. And the briefing counts when it's logged, because ninety-one one-oh-three wants *all available information*, and the record should answer before I have to."

"Personal minimums?"

"The floor is legal. The house is where you live. Review it monthly so the line moves on purpose instead of by accident." He paused. "And yours lives in your left jacket pocket."

"That's the agreement in the left pocket," Wren said, signing the fuel slip. "Minimums are on the kneeboard. Keep your systems straight, first officer."

But her hand — he noticed, because noticing was becoming a problem — went to the zipper anyway, checking, the way pilots touch things they don't intend to lose.

*End of Chapter Two.*
