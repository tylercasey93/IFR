# Chapter Six — Void Time

*Theo*

The trip that nearly broke Clause Two started as the easiest line on the whiteboard: fly empty to Friday Harbor, collect Adaeze Okafor and two lodge investors from some resort summit at Roche Harbor, bring them home. An hour of flying wrapped around an afternoon of waiting on the prettiest ramp in the San Juan Islands.

"Friday Harbor," Sam said on the phone that morning, with the reverence other men saved for ballparks. "You're going to *Friday Harbor* and I'm going to physical therapy."

"I'll eat something unhealthy in your honor."

"Eat it at the counter at the cafe by the marina. Window seat. That's the one." A pause, and then, too casually: "The Bonanza's annual's done, you know. Manny signed it off Tuesday. She's sitting in the hangar, legal as a church."

"And her owner?"

"Her owner sees the doc about the BasicMed physical next month." Another pause, the sound of a man buttering toast one-handed and rebuilding a life the same way. "Tuesday breakfast in Friday Harbor, son. It's coming. Fly good today."

Fly good. Two thousand hours of instructing grammar surrendered to that phrase every time.

---

The morning leg up was Wren's, and it was the kind of flying that made the whole profession feel like getting away with something — off Boeing Field into a broken deck at three thousand, on top by four, the Sound laid out silver and the islands scattered ahead like a green fleet at anchor. Short flight, but IFR the whole way on principle; the deck over the islands was forecast to fill back in by afternoon, and neither of them was in the mood to scud-run a fake relationship *and* a real airplane on the same day.

"Friday Harbor's non-towered," Wren said over the top of descent, in her check-airman voice, which he had learned meant *earn your keep*. "No tower, no approach control on the field. So who do we talk to, and how does this end?"

"Whidbey Approach works us down. If we break out and have the field VMC — which today, morning-wise, we will — best practice at an uncontrolled field: cancel IFR in the air, once we can stay visual. Frees the airspace behind us, because here's the thing people forget — at a non-towered field, IFR arrivals are one-in, one-out. The system protects the whole terminal area for exactly one instrument operation at a time. As long as we're inbound IFR and haven't cancelled, nobody else gets an IFR release off that field and nobody else gets an approach clearance into it. Cancel when it's safe, or land and cancel fast — by radio if we can reach the remote outlet, by phone if we can't — because somewhere out there is a guy holding for release who's *begging* us to remember he exists."

"And if we can't cancel in the air because the field's IFR?"

"Then we fly the approach, land, and the clock's still ours until we close it — and if we forget, the system starts wondering if we're in the trees, and search and rescue is a phone call that starts embarrassing and gets expensive. Close your IFR at uncontrolled fields. Every time. It's the flight-plan equivalent of Clause Three."

"Adequate," Wren said, and began the descent, and did not smile, and had absolutely been smiling somewhere behind the sunglasses.

They broke out at three and a half over the channel — ferries dragging white wakes, the harbor opening up green and improbable — and Wren keyed up: "Whidbey Approach, Meridian Three-Five, field in sight, cancel IFR." And that was that: back to being a VFR airplane in a VFR sky, cleared of obligation by one sentence, the machinery of the system releasing them as smoothly as it had held them.

The landing was Wren's usual — an arrival so undramatic it felt notarized — and by eleven they were parked on the Friday Harbor ramp with six hours to kill and a cafe by the marina with a counter and a window seat.

He took her there. Obviously he took her there.

---

Here is what Theo learned in six hours on San Juan Island, cataloged honestly:

That Wren Hale, released from schedule and audience both, walked slower. That she read the specials board all the way through like a NOTAM package. That she ordered the fried oysters at the counter's window seat without knowing she was sitting in Sam Marsh's imaginary chair, and that telling her — *my dad's been planning breakfast at this exact counter since his stroke; it's the whole reason he's fighting for the medical* — made her go still and thoughtful in a way that fancy restaurants full of audiences never had.

"Tuesday breakfast," she said, looking out at the marina. "That's the mission driving the whole recovery?"

"Flight surgeon, physical therapist, and a breakfast counter. He's a simple aircraft with excellent avionics."

"When it happens," Wren said, "when he flies here — somebody should be at this window to see the landing." She said it lightly. She said it like logistics, like coverage planning, like anything other than what it was, which was Wren Hale volunteering, months in advance, for a seat at his family's finish line. Theo looked at his coffee and had a brief, vivid argument with himself about clauses.

The afternoon went to walking it off. Harbor, bookshop, the little whale museum where Wren read every placard with the thoroughness she brought to MELs. The deck over the islands thickened on schedule, gray sliding over the green, and by five-fifteen, when Okafor's people texted that the cars were leaving Roche Harbor, the field had gone properly IFR — eight hundred overcast, the channel fading to pewter, the kind of evening where VFR was a rumor.

Which meant the departure was about to become the day's syllabus.

"No tower," Theo said, back at the airplane, running the numbers with the phone already in his hand. "Field's IFR, so no cancelling our way around it — we need a clearance *before* we launch, into that." He nodded at the ceiling. "And a clearance off an uncontrolled field in IMC comes with homework."

He dialed the clearance line — the phone number straight into the facility, the modern replacement for shouting at a scratchy remote outlet — put it on speaker on the glareshield, and pulled his pen. Wren, boarding checklist done, passengers still ten minutes out, leaned into the frame of the airstair door to watch him work, and he'd have been lying if he said the audience didn't improve his handwriting.

"Meridian Three-Five, clearance available, advise ready to copy."

"Meridian Three-Five, ready to copy."

And there it came, the oldest song in instrument flying, five verses, always in order — **CRAFT** — and his pen walked down the shorthand column he'd taught to a hundred students:

"Meridian Three-Five is *cleared to* Boeing Field—" **C**, clearance limit, almost always the destination and you notice hard when it isn't— "via *upon entering controlled airspace*, direct Lopez, Victor two-ninety-nine, then radar vectors—" **R**, route, with that lovely lawyer's phrase acknowledging that the first few hundred feet off an uncontrolled field are his own problem in Class G— "*climb and maintain* four thousand, *expect* eight thousand one-zero minutes after departure—" **A**, altitudes, the immediate one and the promised one, and the promise matters because if the radios die, the expected altitude is part of the lost-comm contract— "departure *frequency* one-two-zero-point-niner-five—" **F**— "*transponder* four-six-five-two." **T**.

Then the fine print, the part unique to fields without towers, delivered in the bored cadence of a controller who has ruined many evenings with it:

"Clearance void if not off by zero-one-four-five Zulu. If not off by zero-one-four-five, advise flight service or approach not later than zero-two-one-five of intentions. Time now, zero-one-two-niner."

Theo read it all back, got his "readback correct," ended the call, and looked at the time.

"Void time," he said. "Sixteen minutes. The system just carved us a private hole in the sky — one-in, one-out, remember, and right now we're the *one*. Nobody else gets in or out of this field IFR until we're gone or the window slams. If we're not airborne by four-five, the clearance evaporates — void — and launching after it is a pilot deviation with our name on it. And the second time matters just as much: if we blow the void time, we've got until zero-two-one-five to *tell* someone, because if we go silent, the system's next assumption isn't that we got shy. It's that we crashed on departure. That half hour is the tripwire for search and rescue."

"And her cars," Wren said, looking at the empty ramp gate, "are ten minutes out."

They did the math at the same speed and arrived at the same place. Ten minutes to arrival, three to board and brief, two to start, taxi — that put wheels-up at void-time-minus-one if every single thing cooperated, and the first rule of uncontrolled-field void times is that nothing cooperates.

"Call them back," Wren said. "Push it."

"Or." Theo held up the phone. "Better tool. When I ask for the clearance again I ask for a *hold for release* instead — let them hold us until the pax are aboard, then we call ready and they cut us a fresh release with a tight window we can actually make. You don't stretch a void time. You re-cut it. A void time is a promise, and we—" he was dialing already "—are extremely into keeping those, contractually."

The redial got a new voice, an easy amendment — "call when ready, we'll have a release for you" — and the evening exhaled. Which left, suddenly, nothing to do: an empty ramp, a gray ceiling, a boarding door open to the smell of rain and kelp, and the two of them standing closer than the task required in the golden-gray island light, adrenaline with nowhere to file itself.

"Sixteen-minute hole in the sky," Wren said. "Just for us. That's the most romantic thing this fake relationship has ever produced."

"I'll have it engraved. *One-in, one-out.*"

"Don't put that on jewelry." But she was laughing — third documented occurrence, and the first with nobody anywhere, no client, no Griff, no photographer, Clause Two nowhere in force because there was no performance to authorize — and she had rain in her hair and the ferry horn sounded somewhere out in the pewter and Theo became aware, with total instrument certainty, that he was about to do something with no checklist, and that she knew it, and that she had not moved.

The phone rang.

They both looked at it. It rang again, glareshield-loud, the clearance line calling back — and the moment folded itself neatly along its seams and filed itself away, and Wren stepped back into the airplane saying something about seatbelt cards in a voice one notch too even, and Theo answered the phone like a man who had recently survived a lightning strike.

"Meridian Three-Five, your release—"

*Yeah,* he thought, pen moving, heart idiotic. *That's the problem exactly.*

---

The leg home was nine-tenths uneventful, which in aviation as in romance means the one-tenth got their attention.

They were level at eight thousand in the black between layers, Okafor and her investors murmuring about cap rates behind them, when Seattle Center acquired a second, similar voice on frequency: *Cascadia Five-Three-Five*, some turboprop repositioning south. Meridian Three-Five and Cascadia Five-Three-Five, sharing air on a scratchy sector at the tired end of the day — the exact setup Theo used to build into simulator scenarios back when this stuff was theoretical.

"Cascadia Five-Three-Five, descend and maintain six thousand."

Silence. Then Center, again, slightly frayed. And Theo's readback instinct had a hand hovering when the next one came:

"Meridian Three-Five, descend and maintain six thousand."

Wren's hand went to the altitude selector. Stopped. And both of them heard it at the same time — that call sign had gone to *them*, but the descent, two transmissions ago, had gone to the other guy. Somewhere in a dark sector, a controller's strips had shuffled.

"Confirm for Meridian Three-Five," Wren transmitted, even, unhurried, the radio voice she could have taught surgery in. "We were level eight thousand; verify the descent to six is for Meridian Three-Five and not Cascadia Five-Three-Five."

A two-second pause with a shift change inside it. "Meridian Three-Five... good catch. Maintain eight thousand. Cascadia Five-Three-Five, descend and maintain six thousand." And the other crew, sheepish, took their altitude, and the sector went back to its evening.

"That," Theo said quietly, "is the whole religion. Readback-hearback. The system's got three layers of protection and two of them are us: hear it, read it back, and *listen to whether the world agrees with what you read back*. Similar call signs are the classic hole in the cheese — the reg says the magic word: if you're in doubt about a clearance, ninety-one one-twenty-three's answer is *ask*. Immediately. 'Confirm' is not a confession of weakness. It's the cheapest safety equipment on the airplane."

"And if Center had insisted, and it was wrong — say, descent into terrain we could see on the display?"

"Then the other magic word: *unable*. A complete sentence. ATC owns separation; we own the airplane. And underneath it all, ninety-one point three — the PIC's emergency authority: in an actual emergency, deviate from any rule in the book to the extent required, then be ready to explain it on paper if they ask. It's not a loophole. It's the system admitting, in writing, that the last line of defense is the person with their hand on the yoke." He glanced left. "You knew all that."

"I like hearing the catechism," Wren said. "Besides. Cascadia." Her jaw was set at a familiar angle, the Alaska angle, the Cascade Air Group angle. "Similar call signs have burned me before. Different company. Same lesson: when two things sound almost alike, the cost of *confirming which one is real* is thirty seconds." She was quiet a moment, islands of light sliding beneath the cloud gaps. "Applies broadly."

Theo flew and did not touch that with a ten-foot pole, and touched it internally with every pole he owned.

---

Clause Three happened on the darkened ramp at Boeing Field after the client was carred away, the two of them folding into the paperwork under the wing light.

"Debrief," Wren said. "Go."

"Uncontrolled fields: one-in, one-out — the airspace holds one IFR operation at a time, so cancel in the air when VMC allows or close up fast on the ground, because someone's always holding for the hole you're standing in. Clearance off an uncontrolled field in IMC: CRAFT — cleared-to, route, altitude with the *expect* that doubles as lost-comm insurance, frequency, transponder — plus the fine print: void time, hard wall, don't launch after it; and the notify-by time, half an hour, because past it the system assumes the worst and starts spending real money looking for you. Don't stretch a void time — re-cut it; hold for release exists for exactly that. En route: readback-hearback, similar call signs are the classic trap, 'confirm' costs nothing, ninety-one one-twenty-three says when in doubt, ask — and 'unable' is a complete sentence. Bottom of the stack: ninety-one point three. Emergency authority. The last line of defense has a pulse."

"And the other item," Wren said, not looking up from the fuel slip.

The ramp light hummed. Somewhere across the field a jet spooled and thought better of it.

"Ramp at Friday Harbor," Theo said carefully. "Sixteen-minute window. We were interrupted by a phone call."

"We were saved by a phone call," Wren said — but she said it the way a pilot says *field in sight* when the runway's been in sight for miles, and she clipped the fuel slip to the board, and handed him the pen, and her fingers were not entirely careful about where his were. "Clause Two's getting hard to administer, Marsh. Log it as a discrepancy."

"Monitor?" he said. "Or ground the fleet?"

"Monitor," said Wren Hale, and walked off toward dispatch through the rain she never ran in either, leaving the fleet — both of them — very much airworthy, and very much on watch.

*End of Chapter Six.*
