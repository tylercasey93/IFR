## COLE BISHOP — Captain, 36

### Timeline
- **1990** — born outside Walla Walla, Washington, the middle child of five. Father: **Ray Bishop**, wheat farmer who dusts his own fields and half the county's from a patched-together Ag Cat. Mother: **Nadine Bishop**, who runs the farm's books, the church potluck, and, by general agreement, the family.
- **1990–2008** — a childhood of harvest crews, hand-me-down everything, and a Super Cub on a dirt strip between the wheat and the windbreak. Learns weather from a man whose income depends on reading it off the Blues by eye. Solos at sixteen; the family's love language is teasing, chores, and showing up.
- **2007 (age 17)** — comes out. Nadine's response takes four seconds. Ray goes quiet for two seasons — two — and then one flat June dawn stands by the Cub and says, *"Get in. You're not leaving this farm without knowing spins."* The apology is a syllabus; Cole flies it and cries where the slipstream hides it. (Canon-to-be: the series' template for how Bishop men say the big things — badly, sideways, and completely.)
- **2008–2012** — ratings through a Tri-Cities flight school while driving its fuel truck; instructs two years; discovers he's good at making scared people feel capable — and that he'd rather be liked than known, a fact he won't diagnose for another decade.
- **2012–2016** — Part 135 freight in Caravans out of Idaho backcountry strips — Salmon River country, real weather, real decisions made alone. Where "current isn't proficient" stops being a poster and becomes his operating system; writes his first personal-minimums card, reviewed monthly, initialed.
- **2016 (age 26)** — hired at **Cascade Air Group** (Boeing Field; 135 charter, Navajos → King Airs). A year later, a magnetic senior captain named **Danny Ruiz** runs his King Air checkout and starts calling him "farm team" like it's a compliment. It sounds like one.
- **2018–2020** — ⚠ Ruiz and Cole are quietly together for two years — quiet on Ruiz's terms: *discretion, professionalism, optics.* Cole, out since seventeen, folds himself back into a half-closet for love and hates the shape of himself in there. He will later understand the discretion wasn't shame; it was cover — for everything.
- **2020–2021 (age 30–31)** — the fall. Cole discovers Ruiz has been pencil-whipping training records and duty-time logs across the operation — including sim sessions that never happened, initialed with a forgery of Cole's own hand. He confronts; Ruiz's answer is the coldest sentence anyone has ever said to him: *"Nobody checks. That's what the system is for."* Cole reports internally; internal dies quietly; he goes to the FSDO. Cascade needs its golden captain, and the story gets rewritten with Ruiz's help: *the bitter ex, the grudge, the personal thing dressed up as a safety report.* Colleagues who know better — Jax Calloway among them — say nothing. Cole is "let go for cause." Ruiz gets a tail-number transfer and a promotion arc.
- **2021–2023** — the wilderness: ferry contracts, a winter instructing in Arizona, two years of watching the industry's version of him be the only version in circulation. He never once shops his side of the story. (This fact is in a file he doesn't know exists.)
- **2023 (age 33)** — **Dale Voss**, administering a 135 check for an operator Cole is contracting for, watches him fly, reads the Cascade file *sideways*, and makes one phone call. **Marcus Griffin** hires him without asking a single question about Cascade. Cole has been Meridian's captain — and its entire standards department — ever since.
- **October 2024** — interviews a too-polished applicant named Jack Marsh at the base of a ladder in the rain, decides within ten minutes that the polish is load-bearing, and hires what's under it.
- **2025** — when Sam Marsh strokes out in a Safeway produce aisle, it's Cole who drives Jack to Harborview and sits the corridor with him. Neither of them has ever mentioned it since. Neither of them has forgotten a second of it.
- **2025–2026** — the Elias arrangement: **Elias Ward, 38**, a marine structural engineer in Ballard. Dinner when the season allows, a night, no questions, no futures — intimacy scheduled, contained, and unable to cost him anything. It dissolves mid-Book 1 under the mission's secrecy; Cole ends it in person, decently, explaining nothing; Elias's four-word eulogy — *"Figured. Good luck, Captain."* — closes it.
- **Fall 2026 (age 36)** — Book 1 opens: three years of rebuilt reputation, the safest small flight department in the Pacific Northwest, and a best friend in the right seat he has a rule about.

### The wound
Two-layered. Layer one is Ruiz: *love used my integrity as a resource, and the institution sided with the liar.* Layer two is quieter and his alone: *he made himself smaller once, for love, and the love was counterfeit* — two years in a half-closet for a man who was compiling leverage. Both layers produce the same behavior: total transparency as armor (he will never again be anyone's secret — which a job made of secrets is about to test), and warmth as perimeter defense. Cole is the most approachable man on any ramp in Seattle, and it is precisely how nobody gets in: be everyone's friend and you never have to be anyone's confidant.

### Psychology
- **Wants:** a flawless operation; a company that survives the winter; to never again be a story someone else gets to tell.
- **Needs:** to let one person inside the perimeter — to learn that being liked and being known are different currencies, and he's been paying himself in the wrong one (the explicit series thesis for him).
- **Defenses:** hospitality as deflection (feed people, fix their problems, learn their kids' names — anything but be asked a question); standards as emotion-containment; the smile that stays on while the eyes go to the numbers.
- **Contradictions that make him real:** radically honest about facts, a vault about himself; the guy who remembers everyone's birthday and has never once mentioned his own; physically brave, emotionally risk-averse; hates lying — and is about to sign the manifest.
- **The manifest problem (Book 1 core):** the mission requires the pilot-in-command to sign falsified paperwork — Ruiz's sin, in Cole's hand, with Cole's real name. His resolution: he does it with open eyes, having interrogated Okafor about the difference until he believes it (*Ruiz lied to protect himself; this protects someone else, and I sign my own name*). But it costs him sleep, and he never lets the crew see the bill. ⚠ In Book 3, that signature is exactly what makes him mole-plausible on paper — the file reads "has falsified official documents under pressure," and the man who authored his first smear knows it.

### Voice & mannerisms
Warm baritone; easy, specific praise ("that was a nice catch on the fuel numbers — say it louder next time"); asks about your mother by name. The hard no is quiet and total: *"We're not doing that."* The tell that a decision is coming: he rolls his sleeves. Under real load the warmth doesn't vanish — it compresses: shorter sentences, same kindness, zero give. Writes his minimums card monthly and initials it like a legal instrument. Coffee black from a farm-auction thermos older than Jack. Whistles exactly one bar of something when the weather turns, then stops himself. Says the big things badly, sideways, and completely — his father's syllabus.

### Appearance
6'2", broad through the shoulders, farmer's forearms; sandy-brown hair going its own way by end of duty day; a squint line from twenty summers of glare off wheat and water. Handsome in the open, uncomplicated way of a man who has no idea what to do with the information and has therefore discarded it. Flight-department polo on duty; ballcap and flannel off. Wears his grandfather's wristwatch, wound daily.

### What the Agency file says ⚠
Voss's original assessment memo, quoted in Book 2: *"BISHOP: the rarest profile — integrity with a paper trail. Reported his own captain, took the industry hit, never once shopped the story to save himself. Sat out two years rather than sign someone else's version. If he agrees to carry something, it arrives. Weakness: he will burn himself for the crew without telling anyone he's on fire. Handle honestly or not at all."* The file gets one thing wrong: it lists him as "no significant attachments." The file was written before anyone watched him watch Jack Marsh.

### Series arc
- **B1:** the manifest decision; learns his integrity can serve something bigger than his record; ends Elias; watches his best friend rehearse being in love with someone else and discovers the perimeter has been breached for two years; the ramp kiss; the night — then refuses, with catastrophic professionalism, to discuss it.
- **B2:** learns to carry a cover without becoming it; the jealousy he has no standing for; teaches Mara crosswind landings the way Ray taught him spins; the words, at last, end of book — and he says them first, badly, sideways, and completely.
- **B3:** the suspicion inverts Cascade — this time an institution he *chose* doubts him, and the man who wrote the first lie is writing the second; the growth is that he doesn't armor up alone: he lets the crew fight for him. Ends: engaged, perimeter open, and one long-promised flight over the Blues to a wheat farm, introducing his fiancé to the Ag Cat.

### Canon anchors (new canon — survive all rewrites)
Walla Walla wheat; Ray's two seasons and the spins lesson; the Super Cub; personal-minimums card initialed monthly; "current isn't proficient" as his cockpit doctrine; "we're not doing that"; the rolled sleeves; the thermos; Cascade/Ruiz — reported his own captain and partner, burned for it; hired via Voss's one call and Griff's no-questions; interviewed Jack at the base of a ladder in the rain; sat the Harborview corridor; rule one: *never with someone on my crew.*

## JACK MARSH — First Officer, 31

### Timeline
- **1995** — born in Des Moines, Washington. Father: **Sam Marsh**, Boeing 727 flight engineer, later a Part 91 corporate operation's dispatcher-of-all-trades. Mother: **Ellen Marsh**, wildlife photographer (birds), part-time school photographer for money.
- **1995–2013** — grows up bilingual in Aviation and Ellen. Sam's world: checklists, systems, the Truck (the V35B Bonanza, bought 1998, hail dimples and all). Ellen's world: patience, watching, the two-knock doorframe ritual every time anyone left the house. The pattern that will define him shows early: whatever Jack picks up, Jack masters — state chess final at fifteen, a golf swing his instructors called unfair, piano by ear — and then sets down, restless, looking for the thing that will finally be difficult. Flying is the first thing that never stops being difficult. He never sets it down.
- **2013–2017** — ratings through a busy Puget Sound flight school while working its front desk; instructs part-time before he can rent a car; teaches himself conversational Japanese off freight-radio boredom and language tapes because a line pilot bet him he couldn't. (The bet costs that pilot forty dollars and, nine years later, pays for itself in a Sapporo hotel lobby.)
- **2017–2022 (ages 22–27)** — freight: a Navajo for a scrappy outfit running Puget Sound and the coast. Five years of real IMC, real ice, real single-pilot discipline. On a regional's hiring list from 2023.
- **2020 (age 25)** — Ellen dies in spring: eighteen months of illness she narrated with a photographer's calm. The doorframe knock migrates to the glareshield. Sam goes quiet for a year; Jack moves back across the water, keeps flying, holds the family together — and the easy, sunny kid finishes disappearing into the finished, polished man. People who meet him now describe him the same way twice: *impressive*, then, quieter, *hard to read.*
- **2022–2024** — full-time instructing (a CFII with a waiting list; Bergman the ER doctor is his; the "you built this" text is canon).
- **October 2024 (age 29)** — hired at Meridian by a captain who interviewed him at the base of a ladder in the rain and was, unnervingly, neither charmed nor intimidated. Jack has been trying to figure out which annoys him more ever since. Two years, one cockpit, best friend he's ever had.
- **Early 2025** — the regional class date finally lands — and days later Sam drops in the Safeway produce aisle. Left MCA stroke. Jack declines the class from a hospital corridor (*"some decisions don't have a brief"*) with Cole sitting beside him, and never re-litigates it.
- **Dating history** — Jack is bisexual, out to his family since he was nineteen (Sam's complete reaction, canon: *"Okay. Does he like airplanes?"*). A college boyfriend who taught him about jazz; a freight-years girlfriend who taught him about endings; and since 2025, **Aaron Brandt, 33**, a Harborview ER charge nurse — an easy, kind, no-questions arrangement built on incompatible schedules and mutual emotional unavailability. Aaron ends it gently mid-Book 1, when the couple legend makes Jack unexplainable, with the observation Jack isn't ready to hear: *"I'm not competing with a girlfriend you can't explain. And honestly? It wouldn't be her I was losing to anyway. I've heard you talk about your captain."*
- **Fall 2026 (age 31)** — Book 1 opens on the Okafor account's strangest week.

### The wound
Ellen's death taught him that the worst forecasts verify — so he treats every day like VFR-on-top: enjoy the sun, know the tops, never trust it to last. The polish is real *and* it is weather-proofing: if the performance is flawless, nobody looks behind it, nobody leaves, nobody asks how Jack is. Second layer: the deferred-dream ledger — he doesn't regret choosing Sam, but he carries a quiet fear that his life is a series of graceful accommodations rather than choices, and that being good at everything is what you do instead of wanting one thing badly enough to be bad at it. Which is why the mission — and what he finds in himself over the fake dating — hits so hard: they're the first things since the hospital corridor that he *chooses* at full throttle, with no exit briefed.

### Psychology
- **Wants:** everyone he loves safe, solvent, and within twenty minutes' drive — arranged so seamlessly they never notice him doing it.
- **Needs:** to want something visibly — to put a thing he could actually lose on the table where people can watch. ⚠ The tradecraft arc sharpens this: Book 2 forces him to face that he *enjoys* deception, is arguably built for it, and to decide what kind of man wields that safely. His answer — Ellen's answer — is: one who never lies at home.
- **Defenses:** competence as moat (be the best in the room and the room keeps its distance); the pause before answering that makes people re-hear their own question; charm deployed at precise, chosen wattage — he can warm a room or chill it, and both are decisions.
- **Contradictions:** an open book about facts, a vault about grief (the glareshield tap went unexplained for two months — canon); intimidating to strangers, endless with students and the elderly; the most honest liar in the series; drives a blue Subaru with a mismatched fender he will never fix — the one unpolished thing he owns, and the tell that the polish is a coat.

### Voice & mannerisms
Low, unhurried, precise — he doesn't fill silences, he lets them work. Wit like a scalpel handed to you handle-first: you're laughing before you notice you've been cut. Names adversaries (Gary the jammer — canon) in a tone that suggests Gary should be worried. Two knocks on the glareshield before every takeoff (canon: Ellen). The one-shoulder small shrug for true things. Goes *quiet and precise* when genuinely frightened — the tell only Cole can read, because the polish never cracks anywhere else. Everyone — FBO staff, customs officers, new clients — assumes Jack is the captain; his correction is instant and oddly fierce: *"He's the captain."* Never runs in rain.

### Appearance
6'1", lean, dark blond, jaw like a decision; dresses a full pay-grade above the job — tailored where the field is fleece — and the effect is less vanity than uniform. The habitual expression is polite, attentive, and faintly evaluating, which is why ramp rumor at three FBOs has him as either ex-military, ex-federal, or a hitman, and why the truth (chess club, wine lists, hotel-bar piano, a father he calls every Sunday) keeps surprising people. The half-smile, when it actually arrives, is the whole show.

### What the Agency file says ⚠
Okafor's own assessment, Book 1: *"MARSH: natural elicitor, improvisational legend-builder, zero training. Subjects volunteer information to him and thank him afterward. Aptitude ceiling not yet observed. Motivation profile unusual: not money, not thrill, not flag — attachment. He will do dangerous things excellently for the people inside his fence. Recommend: put the mission inside the fence."* What the file gets wrong: it flags his reserve as an operational asset with no cost. The cost is that the one thing he wants most has been sitting three feet to his left for two years, unbriefed.

### Series arc
- **B1:** the audition he doesn't know he's passing → the mission he chooses with open eyes; assigned the couple legend and discovers, gesture by manufactured gesture, that he's performing from memory — every manufactured intimacy a copy of something he already does, unthinking, for Cole; loses Aaron to a truth he won't say out loud; the ramp kiss; the night — followed by a morning-after in which two fluent communicators say nothing at all.
- **B2:** formal tradecraft; the "I'm good at lying" reckoning; Sam tension (first time in his life he can't tell his father the truth — about the work or the man); discovers he minds — enormously — that the arrangement has no name; wins the words by never once pushing — end of book.
- **B3:** the overheard debrief detonates his exact wound (*was I an accommodation? was I an assignment?*); eleven days of silence; the crucible; learns to ask the question instead of armoring in polish. Ends: on one knee in Friday Harbor with an unread speech.

### Canon anchors
The glareshield double-tap and Ellen's doorframe origin; "some decisions don't have a brief"; Bergman and the "you built this" text; the archived airline email; walks unhurried through rain; the mismatched fender; the coaster ("I'M NOT GOING ANYWHERE" — now a Jack→Cole beat); teaches through mnemonics; sixty-six-HIT; Sam's "Does he like airplanes?"; "He's the captain."

## WREN HALE — 34. BRIDGEBELL contract aviator & cover officer. *(Re-slotted, v5)*

### Timeline
- **1992** — born in Ketchikan, Alaska. Father: **Gus Hale**, salmon-boat owner-operator (F/V *Marta June*). Mother: **Diane Hale**, elementary-school teacher — still in the house above the harbor, still teaching third grade.
- **2009 (age 17)** — the envelope. A bad season, and Gus still puts a summer's cash on the table: *"One of us should get to look down at the water for a change."* Adds a used aluminum E6-B bought off a retiring bush pilot, paint worn off the wind side. She solos before her eighteenth birthday.
- **2010–2016** — ratings in Ketchikan and Anchorage; floatplane tours; then Anchorage freight in Piper Chieftains — ice, darkness, fish smell that never leaves the charts. **2016:** Gus dies (engine-room accident; boats are honest about their risks). He never flew with her. The shoebox of expired charts begins here.
- **2017–2018** — the Zulu winter: turns around at the first side-window splash of SLD ice and is told she "cost the company a client." Leaves Alaska within the year — Diane stays; it's Wren who can't live in a town made of Gus-shaped absences.
- **2018–2022** — the odd years: Pacific Rim contract flying — ferry deliveries, survey work, discreet charters for clients with excellent paperwork and no small talk. An increasing fraction of the work, she will later learn, was Okafor's network auditioning her.
- **2022 (age 30)** — Dale Voss grades a 135 check she flies for a client operator: "adequate." Three weeks later a woman with magnificent posture buys her dinner in Honolulu and offers her the only honest job description of her career: *"I will lie to you about one category of thing, tell you which category, and never anything else."* Wren, who has been lied to by better-dressed people about worse, signs.
- **2022–2026** — BRIDGEBELL contract aviator and cover officer: six legends run, none broken. She has been a widowed lodge heiress, a fisheries lobbyist, and half of three engaged couples, all without once changing her expression.
- **Fall 2026** — assigned to WINTER HARBOR as the investor legend's female half, with a fiancé-shaped hole in the paperwork that Okafor has already decided a certain first officer fills.

### The person
Everything true in the old file stays true: flat declaratives; threats delivered like fuel loads; "adequate" as maximum praise; peanut M&Ms eaten in ritual fours from a double freezer bag ("ballast"); the E6-B in the zippered left chest pocket; squares paperwork when buying time; hums Patsy Cline doing weight-and-balance and denies it. Straight, single by design, and keeps whatever arrangements she has in a jurisdiction the reader never visits. 5'7", built like someone who has thrown cargo in the dark; gray eyes; her father's watch, band replaced twice.

### Series function
- **B1:** the professional who teaches Jack fake-dating tradecraft straight-faced (backstory drills, ring logistics, how couples stand at a check-in desk) — and clocks him inside a week: *"I've done six of these. You're the first one who keeps checking whether someone else is watching."* Her deadpan runs the device; her decency ends it kindly. Exit line at the debrief: *"Best fake fiancé I've had. Tell him, or I will."*
- **B2–B3:** the crew's standing third pilot and driest ally; teaches Mara straight-and-level in cruise, hands on the yoke, the way Gus never got to; one Book 3 landing at Ketchikan — Diane's kitchen, the harbor, twenty minutes on the page that pay for the whole subplot. Holds the counter seat at Friday Harbor because someone asked her to be at the window to see the landing.

### Canon anchors
Ketchikan; the envelope and Gus's line; the E6-B; the shoebox; the M&Ms; Diane in the house above the harbor; "adequate"; six legends, none broken.

## ADAEZE OKAFOR — 71. Hotelier. Other things.

### Timeline
- **1955** — born in Lagos, third daughter of a customs clerk and a market trader; grows up multilingual (Igbo, Yoruba, English, market-stall French) and observant — the family business is reading people at a counter.
- **1977** — degree in accountancy, University of Lagos; hired by an international hotel group as a junior auditor. Discovers she can walk a property once and know which manager is stealing.
- **1978 (age 23)** — the harmattan airport, the calm man on the suitcase: **Bernard Okafor** — Nigerian-American, officially a hotel-development consultant, actually a CIA officer under non-official cover. ⚠ *The meeting was a bump*: Adaeze had been spotted as access-agent material. What Bernard's file never predicted: he fell for the recruit.
- **1981 (Nairobi)** — the grounded flight. Before the proposal, Bernard commits the sin of his career: he shows her the file — the bump, the assessment memo, all of it — and *then* says the line about the arithmetic. She marries him eleven days later, eyes fully open. This is the root of her lifelong method: **the truth is the strongest cover** — and of her rule that she never lies to the people she recruits about anything except what she must.
- **1981–2015** — the double life as one life: forty hotels across three continents, each one real, profitable, and quietly useful. Naturalizes American 1988; formally an officer (NOC) 1990. No children — a grief reassigned into goddaughters, scholarships, and staff who become family (Chidinma's mother was her first hire in London).
- **2015–2022** — Bernard's diagnosis; seven years of being only married ("the profitable years"). **2022:** Bernard dies (41 years — canon). Within six months she is bored, furious at being bored, and back on contract: **BRIDGEBELL**. First acquisition: a Gulfstream in a trust. First problem: a crew she can trust with it.
- **2025–2026** — finds Meridian (via Voss); watches its two pilots for eighteen months with a connoisseur's patience. Book 1: the Sorokina crisis forces her hand early.

### The wound
Bernard's death removed the only person on earth cleared for all of her. Her charm, her matchmaking, her "entertainments" are not manipulation — they are a widow's raging appetite for *true things happening in front of her*, and the truest thing on her current horizon is two pilots who finish each other's checklists and look away from each other at exactly the same moments. The series' quiet tragedy: she can give them the life she had, or the work she had, and she knows better than anyone that carrying both nearly requires a miracle. She recruits them anyway. ⚠ Book 3 makes her answer for that.

### Psychology & voice
Truth-forward tradecraft; tests disguised as generosity; power worn as warmth; never raises her voice — the executive appears only in stillness (canon: "I made no decisions before tea. After tea I made several"). Grand, unhurried, theatrical; lays words down "like cards"; ginger tea, no ice, at top of climb; addresses people as "children," "my aviators," "dears"; proverbs that turn out to be doctrine ("everything that matters starts with weather" — Bernard's line first).

### Series arc
- **B1:** the unmasking as necessity; carries the moral weight of asking Cole for the manifest — and the private delight of assigning Jack the couple legend, knowing exactly which two people it will actually educate.
- **B2:** mentor and program chief; the reveal of Bernard's file trick becomes her recruiting sermon.
- **B3:** her program is the one under suspicion; she must choose between protecting BRIDGEBELL and protecting the crew, and — because she is who she is — finds the line where both are true, at real cost. Final scene: at the Friday Harbor breakfast, one table over, having arranged nothing for once.

### Canon anchors
Harmattan story; Nairobi proposal; 41 years; forty hotels; ginger tea; Chidinma; "I am never angry at the truth before nine a.m."; "nobody quotes my aviators out of context twice."

## DR. YELENA SOROKINA — 44. The asset.

### Timeline
- **1982** — born in Zhukovsky, outside Moscow — an aviation-city childhood; her father tested navigation systems, her mother taught mathematics. English learned from smuggled aviation manuals and Jeppesen charts (canon hook: textbook-perfect phraseology, charmingly formal).
- **2004–2012** — radiophysics degrees; recruited into a GRU-adjacent research institute working satellite-navigation vulnerabilities. Believes, sincerely, that she is building *shields*.
- **2010** — marries a flight-test engineer; **Mara** born 2011. Widowed 2019 (test-flight accident, the institute's own program; a medal, a pension, never an honest report — the first crack).
- **2020–2024** — her portfolio is folded into an offensive targeting directorate under **Col. Ruslan Verin**. She watches her shields become spears — including applications against civil aviation she cannot unknow. Begins, 2024, passing carefully chosen material through a contact cultivated in the restaurant of an Okafor hotel in Helsinki.
- **2026** — a counterintelligence sweep closes in; she runs from a Singapore conference with Mara and two suitcases; goes to ground in staff quarters of the Okafor hotel in Sapporo. Book 1 begins nine days later.

### Psychology & voice
Brilliant, exact, allergic to handling ("Do not manage me. Brief me."); grief-dry humor; treats the flight deck as the one place on earth where the rules are honest. Her defection is not ideology but *refusal of complicity* — which makes her Cole's mirror (both ended their careers over falsified purposes; the first real conversation they have is about paperwork, and it goes four layers deeper than either intends) and the natural second teacher of the series: from the jump seat, the woman who builds jammers explains why the crew's green-needle discipline is the only thing her old directorate genuinely fears.

### Series function
- **B1:** the package who becomes a person; her competence turns the exfil into a three-pilot problem-solving story rather than a rescue of a victim.
- **B2:** consulting voice from the safehouse; the GPS-interference exercises are hers; her intelligence starts the Pacific Corporate thread.
- **B3:** ⚠ Verin's pursuit of her is the engine; her warning ("he will not jam your radios; he will jam your trust") names the book.

## MARA SOROKINA — 15.

Sharp-tongued, terrified, pretending to be neither; learned English from cartoons and her mother's manuals, so she sounds like a sitcom teenager quoting a POH. Book 1: her passport problem is the pickup's complication; she bonds with Jack over naming things (he lets her name the Gulfstream's autopilot, and her verdict on him — *"you are like a spy from a movie, except sad"* — is the most accurate assessment in the series) and with Wren over silence — Wren teaches her straight-and-level in cruise, hands on the yoke, the way Gus never got to. Books 2–3: American high school as deep cover comedy; Cole teaches her crosswind landings (Ray's syllabus, third generation); one Book 3 beat where the crew's protection of her is what breaks the case open. Function: raises stakes, humanizes the mission, and gives the series its future-generation echo (the epilogue teases her first solo).

## COL. RUSLAN VERIN — 52. The adversary.

GRU signals/navigation-warfare officer; Sorokina's former patron — he pulled her into the directorate, praised her, promoted her, and genuinely believed she shared his cold arithmetic (deterrence requires spears). Her defection is therefore personal twice over: professional catastrophe and something close to apostasy. Methodical, unshowy, contemptuous of thugs; his weapon is process — manifests, cameras, patterns, patience. Book 1: a search grid and a voice on other people's phones; one near-miss at a Sapporo FBO he never knows was a near-miss. Book 2: identifies the *airplane* from fuel-uplift records. Book 3: on-stage antagonist; author of the sabotage; undone not by force but by the one variable his process can't model — a crew that tells each other the truth. Guardrail: never cartoonish; he is what Sorokina would have become if she'd stopped asking questions.

## THE HOME CAST

### Sam Marsh — 68. Retired 727 flight engineer.
Boeing panel 1981–2004; then twenty years as a corporate flight department's dispatcher/mother-hen. Married Ellen 1985 — she flew home from the 1998 Bonanza pre-buy photographing clouds and named the airplane the Truck (canon). Widowed 2020; stroke early 2025; BasicMed comeback 2026 (canon arc). The series' hearth: bad coffee, porch light, "fly good today." Adores Cole with the uncomplicated enthusiasm of a man who has watched his son orbit something for two years ("Does he like airplanes?" was answered years ago; the follow-up — *"Does he like* you*?"* — is Sam's one Book 2 intervention, delivered over engine parts). ⚠ Book 3 beat: the old flight engineer recognizes agency handwriting a generation early — he flew "special" charters out of Clark in the seventies and knows exactly what a too-clean trust looks like; he says nothing until saying something saves everyone. Never fully witting until the epilogue; his dignity about it is the point.

### Chidinma Adeyemi — 26. Barrister. Vetting officer.
Okafor's goddaughter — her mother ran Adaeze's first London property; the "family firm" raised her on discretion. Commercial-fraud barrister (real, excellent) whose chambers work doubles as BRIDGEBELL's legal/vetting arm — contractor, not Agency staff, which lets her say *"I don't work for anyone's government; I work for my aunt, who is worse."* Book 1: builds the couple legend and its paperwork (the ring is her selection; her memo on it is four paragraphs). Book 2: handler-of-record. Book 3: the one who refuses to let the mole file swallow Cole — she cross-examines the misdirection to death, in a conference room, magnificently. Voice: cut-glass precision, verdicts as kindness.

### Marcus "Griff" Griffin — 58. Owner. Never witting.
Twenty-two years of freight; his late wife **Ellen Griffin's** insurance money is in Meridian (canon; two Ellens is intentional texture: both households built on a beloved ghost). Bought the company because "the only version of being alive I've ever understood has a ramp attached" (canon). Series function: the innocent the whole cast protects; hired both pilots on gut and one phone call apiece and considers them the best decisions of his life, a judgment he announces at bad moments. His arc is dignity: Book 3 hands him one moment where being a good boss *is* operational security.

### Priya Chandra — 29. Scheduler/dispatcher. The radar.
Four years at the desk; putting her brother Dev through Green River (canon); studying nights for her aircraft-dispatcher certificate. Notices everything — including, years before anyone says it, the thing between the pilots (her private log of near-miss moments is a running gag the reader gets in glimpses). Book 2's manifests don't add up and she knows it; proposed (per bible): she assembles the truth herself in Book 3 and handles it so professionally that Okafor offers her a job, horrifying Cole and delighting everyone else. Voice: teasing, fiercely loyal, reads averted eyes like weather depictions (canon).

### Dale Voss — 58. Check airman. Retired Air Branch.
Army rotary → fixed-wing → fifteen years flying "government charters" nobody names (Air Branch, 1990s–2000s) → DPE/check airman with a sideline: for twenty years he has quietly graded pilots for Adaeze. "Adequate" was always a recruitment mark (canon retrofit — it's how both Cole and Wren entered the file). The pen clicks are real Morse — dit-dit: *good* (Book 2 reveal, played once, perfectly). Function: the standards conscience of both worlds; trains the crew's emergency work; delivers the series' driest wisdom ("Radio failures don't heal on silence" — canon).

### Manny Ortiz — 45. Mechanic.
IA with a father's pride in the airplane; kid with braces on the company plan (canon). Book 3: the teardown that proves sabotage — his testimony, delivered with a torque wrench in hand, is unimpeachable precisely because he's the least political man alive.

### The ghosts
- **Gus Hale** — the envelope, the E6-B, "one of us should get to look down at the water." Never flew with Wren. Everything she does for Mara is his unfinished flight.
- **Ellen Marsh** — the doorframe knock, the hundred and forty frames of clouds, "now we have a truck that sees over the weather." Jack's compass for what honest love looks like: patient, observant, never performed.
- **Bernard Okafor** — the file shown before the proposal; the proof the series keeps offering that truth and tradecraft can share a house — at a price someone always pays.
- **Danny Ruiz** — see full dossier below. Through Books 1–2 he remains exactly this: weather, off-stage, the reason for Cole's rule one. ⚠ Book 3 promotes him to the flesh.

### Jax Calloway — 47. The useful idiot.
Pacific Corporate fleet captain; was in the Cascade crew room when Cole burned and said nothing (canon — the sin of the bystander, not the villain). Vain, competent, transactional; recruits for his outfit like it's a lifestyle brand. ⚠ Books 2–3: his charter arm's new owners use him precisely because he repeats things at parties; his gala gossip blows an operational detail he never understood he had. Endgame: not punished by the plot so much as *seen* — Okafor's freeze-out plus the dawning, career-ending knowledge of what he almost caused. A Clancy world needs one man whose only ideology is his own reflection.

## ⚠ THE BOOK 3 TRAITOR (approved)

**Elliot Kang — 49. BRIDGEBELL logistics coordinator (introduced mid-Book 2 as a friendly).** Twenty years Coast Guard (C-130 loadmaster, then logistics chief, Kodiak and Sacramento); recruited by Okafor 2023 to run ground support — fuel contracts, customs brokers, FBO relationships. Warm, rumpled, competent; the guy who always has the crew car warmed up. The lever: his younger brother **Daniel**, a merchant-marine engineer, arrested on manufactured charges when his ship called at Vladivostok in 2025. The offer came through a lawyer in Seoul: schedules and tail numbers — never lives, they promised — for Daniel's treatment, then his release. Elliot feeds "harmless" logistics data for a year, telling himself it's parking information… until the sabotage, which he never agreed to and cannot undo. His unmasking is Book 3's saddest scene, not its most hateful — the book's thesis in reverse: *he lied to protect someone he loved, one compromise at a time — the road Cole refused.* Resolution: he turns himself in through the crew, testifies, takes his consequences; Daniel comes home in the epilogue's background.

**His handler is Danny Ruiz.**

## ⚠ DANNY RUIZ — 46. Cascade's golden captain. The mole-runner. (Book 3 antagonist — approved)

### Timeline
- **2016–2021** — Cascade Air Group's celebrated captain; charming upward, corrosive downward; pencil-whips training and duty records because rules, in his cosmology, are for people without talent. Takes a warm, gifted young captain-track pilot named Cole Bishop under his wing, then — quietly, on his own terms — into his life. The discretion he demands ("optics, farm team") is sold as professionalism; it is actually camouflage, and not only for the relationship. When Cole finds the forged records, Ruiz burns him rather than face the FSDO — and burns him with precision: the *bitter ex* narrative is Ruiz's authorship, deployed exactly once, exactly where it would end the questions. He made their two years visible only when visibility was a weapon.
- **2021–2024** — the slide he can't feel happening: a tail-number transfer dressed as promotion, then a Gulf-adjacent operator, then a Part 91 gig flying a leased Gulfstream for shell-company owners who ask no questions and pay in wires. Each rung down confirms his belief that the system is rigged — never that he is the rigging.
- **2025** — the shell companies turn out to have owners of their own. The network that quietly bought Pacific Corporate's charter arm identifies him in an afternoon: a disgraced American charter captain with industry access, expensive habits, a grievance narrative, and practice at falsifying exactly the kinds of records they need falsified. His recruitment is barely a seduction; they describe the job as consulting and pay him what he believes he was always worth.
- **2026–Book 3** — courier and spotter work first; then, when Verin's directorate needs a local hand for the Kang channel, Ruiz becomes the cut-out: the "lawyer's" successor, the voice that collects Elliot's schedules and pays in hope about Daniel. He never learns Verin's name. He is a contractor to the end — which is the point of him.

### Function in Book 3
- **He runs the mole:** Kang's handler — meets, taskings, payments; the tradecraft is Verin's, the face is Ruiz's.
- **He authors the misdirection:** when BRIDGEBELL's mole-hunt begins, the network feeds it Cole — and the feed is expert, because Ruiz wrote the original Cascade lie and knows precisely which entries in Cole's history read two ways ("has falsified official documents under pressure" — the Book 1 manifest — is his suggestion). The institution's file on Cole was always half his authorship; in Book 3 he gets to finish the job, and nearly does.
- **He informs the sabotage:** the network's plan against the G550 is built on his professional knowledge of 135 maintenance culture — where a contactor lives, what a busy shop defers, what a crew trusts.
- **The confrontation:** not a gunfight — an interview. Endgame gives Cole fifteen minutes across a table from him (federal building, counsel present) and the discovery that he is *small*: no ideology, no plan, just grievance compounding at interest. Cole walks out unburdened, because the man who defined five years of his life turns out never to have understood a single thing about him. Ruiz goes to prison believing, to the last, that everyone else was doing it too.

### Guardrails
He and Verin never meet on the page; the professional despises the contractor he uses. Ruiz gets no redemption beat and no cartoon menace — his danger is exactly the banal competence of a man who knows how the industry works and owes it nothing. The old relationship is handled as tradecraft, never spectacle: the books never play Cole's history as a scandal, only as the seam Ruiz mined. The reader's satisfaction is Cole's: not vengeance — *diagnosis*.

## Relationship geometry (quick reference)
