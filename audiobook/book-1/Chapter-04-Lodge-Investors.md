# Chapter Four — Lodge Investors

*Jack*

You can learn everything worth knowing about money by watching it board an airplane.

Old money boards like it's walking into its own kitchen. New money stops on the airstairs for a photograph. Institutional money reads the safety card, which I respect. And the particular money that boarded our airplane on a gray Thursday in late October — six passengers, billed to the Okafor account, listed on Priya's manifest as *lodge investment associates* — boarded like none of those things. They boarded like people who had been *told how*. Up the stairs at an even pace, a nod to me at the door, coats handed off without ceremony, and every one of them found their seat on the first try, which nobody does.

I noticed it the way you notice a good golf swing. Somebody built that, I thought, and then the coffee maker needed attention and I thought about something else.

I want it on the record that I noticed. It's the noticing-what-it-meant I was behind on, and I was behind on it all day.

---

The day had started at four forty in the crew room, because a trip to Southeast Alaska in late October starts the night before and finishes filing at four forty, and because Cole had flown the previous evening's Portland turn and I'd told him I'd build this one. He audits everything I build anyway. I build it knowing he'll audit it, which makes me build it better, and neither of us has ever acknowledged this arrangement, which is how our best arrangements work.

Sitka, for the record, is a wonderful place to take a Gulfstream if you enjoy honesty. The airport sits on a little island stitched to a bigger island, sea level, water on three sides, mountains standing over it going *what*. Weather comes off the Gulf of Alaska with no land to slow it down, and the town gets a hundred inches of rain a year and doesn't mention it.

The forecast that morning had Sitka at fifteen hundred broken with showers around our arrival, wind southeast gusting twenty-five. Flyable. Ordinary, for there. But here's the arithmetic that matters, and I'll teach it the way I taught it for years at a school with a leaky roof, because it's the piece of instrument flying that catches more certificated pilots than any other: you don't plan an IFR flight to an airport. You plan it to an airport *and a question*, and the question is — one hour either side of when you think you'll arrive, is the forecast at least a two-thousand-foot ceiling and three miles' visibility?

One, two, three. One hour, two thousand, three miles.

If yes — if the destination is forecast that good, that whole window — the regulation lets you file without an alternate. It's betting weather, and the government agrees the bet's safe. If no — and fifteen hundred broken is no — you owe the flight plan a second airport. A real answer to the question *and if not there, then where?* — chosen before you're in the air, fueled for before you're heavy, while you're still a person at a desk with coffee instead of a person at flight level four one zero with opinions coming at you sideways.

And you can't just write down the nearest field with a nice restaurant. The alternate has its own math. At planning time — this is planning arithmetic, not what you'll need if you actually divert — the forecast at your alternate has to clear alternate minimums: if it's got a precision approach, six hundred and two miles; non-precision, eight hundred and two. Unless the airport publishes its own numbers, which the hard ones always do, because the ones you want in bad weather are the ones bad weather visits.

Six hundred and two. Eight hundred and two. It sounds like a hymn book. It's actually a margin — the system quietly demanding your Plan B be *better* weather than your Plan A, because if you've arrived at Plan B, something has already gone wrong once.

So: Sitka needs an alternate. In Southeast Alaska your honest choices are Juneau and Ketchikan, and I sat there at four fifty with both TAFs up, doing what the regulation actually wants, which isn't compliance. It's comparison shopping with your life.

Ketchikan I liked. Ketchikan I always like — long runway on its own island, approaches off the water. And then I did the thing you do last that should be first, which is read the NOTAMs. All of them. Not the first screen. There were forty-one for my little kingdom that morning — tower hours, a crane near Juneau, taxiway lights, a berry-picking notice I swear on my mother — and buried at number thirty-three: Ketchikan's glideslope, out of service through the end of the month.

Which changes nothing about flying there and everything about *filing* there. No glideslope, no precision approach; no precision approach and my planning number jumps from six-hundred-and-two to eight-hundred-and-two, and Ketchikan's forecast for the afternoon was nine hundred broken and three. Legal by a hundred feet of forecast, which is legal the way a bridge is safe by one truck.

I've had students want to argue that one. *It clears, doesn't it?* It clears. And a TAF is a professional's best guess with a six-hour shelf life, and if the guess drifts a hundred feet the wrong way while I'm over Chatham Strait with a cabin full of other people's futures, my legal alternate is an anecdote. Somebody dies in the margin between *legal* and *comfortable* every couple of years, and the NTSB writes it up, and the write-up always reads like the NOTAM had their name on it the whole time.

Juneau's forecast was, for once, the good one — the flow that was throwing showers at Sitka was leaving the channel alone. So I filed Juneau, and then I did the Cole Bishop thing, which is now also the Jack Marsh thing because two years in one cockpit will do that: I put on fuel for Sitka, the approach, the miss, Juneau — *and then* Ketchikan, *and then* the legal forty-five minutes at normal cruise burn on top of everything.

That forty-five, by the way, is the floor the regulation sets, the absolute bottom of the tank you're allowed to plan for — destination, alternate, and three-quarters of an hour of nothing going wrong. It is not a target. It's the line under the target. Fuel is the only problem in aviation you can solve completely while you're still on the ground, and the G550's great gift is that she carries answers by the ton.

Cole came in at five thirty, wet, carrying nothing, and read my file the way he reads everything, which is completely, and with his coffee refusing to defend itself.

"You filed Juneau."

"I filed Juneau."

"And fueled for Ketchikan."

"And fueled for Ketchikan."

"Even though the glideslope's out."

"Because if I ever actually need Ketchikan, I'll take it at eight hundred feet over needing it and not having gas to reach it. Filing is a promise about paperwork. Fuel is a promise about arriving."

Cole looked at the fuel number a moment longer, and then he did the millimeter at the corner of his mouth.

"That's a nice sentence," he said. "You practice it?"

"Four drafts," I said. "You taught me that."

"Mm." He handed the file back. "Your leg up. Mine home."

---

I went down the hallway at six to liberate the good clipboard from the front office, and Griff's door was open the width of a hand.

He was on the phone. I heard it the way you hear things at six in the morning through four inches of door, which is in pieces.

"— no, I understand what you're offering. I understood it the first time." A chair creaked. "What I'd like to know is how you know what our note looks like. That's — no. That's not a number we publish, so either my lender's talking, or —" A pause, long. "We're not for sale. We weren't for sale in August when your associate called, and we're — then he's *also* my age, and he can *also* call me himself." Another creak, softer, a man sitting back instead of forward. "Don't call the bank again. Call me. I'm in the book."

The phone went down. Not hard. Gently, which was somehow worse.

I stood in that hallway for exactly as long as it takes to decide a thing is none of your business, which for me, that morning, was about four seconds, and then I took the clipboard off its nail and walked back to the airplane. Griff came out to the ramp at six forty and watched the catering go on and told a joke about the salmon, and his color was one shade off, and I decided a man's finances were his own weather. You don't fly through another pilot's storm just because you can see it.

I filed it, is what I'm saying. I file everything. Filing it is not the same as reading it, and I know the difference now.

---

We were wheels up at eight into a two-thousand-foot deck, on top by nine, and northbound up the coast with the Inside Passage unrolling under broken cloud like something a railroad would put on a poster.

The associates rode quietly. Money in transit. Two of them worked; one slept; a silver-haired woman read a novel in French and drank tea Okafor had somehow caused to exist for her. And across the aisle from her, facing backward like a man who has spent his life in seats other people arranged, sat a smiling, blazered, beautifully barbered fellow of about forty-five named Teddy Vance, who had shaken my hand at the door with both of his and told me the airplane was *marvelous* in an accent that had been to school in three countries.

I made the cabin rounds at altitude, because on the Okafor account we fly like a two-crew operation and host like a five-star one, and that's when she did it.

Adaeze Okafor was in her seat, four-across with a folder of prospectus paper she wasn't reading, and she caught me with the ginger tea question — the tea was fine, the tea is always fine, the tea is a drawbridge — and then, as I turned:

"Jack. Settle a dispute."

"Ma'am."

"Adaeze."

"Settle a dispute, Adaeze."

"Kimiko" — the silver-haired novel reader, who did not look up — "maintains that no one can truly learn a language as an adult. Past twenty-five, she says, you are only ever renting. I have told her I know a man who taught himself Japanese out of an airplane for the price of a bet, and she does not believe you exist."

Now.

I tell that story. It's mine and it's a good one and I have told it at three FBOs and one wedding: the freight years, the Navajo, the overnight in Yakima, a line pilot named Kowalski who bet me forty dollars I couldn't order dinner in Japanese by Christmas, and the language tapes I wore through so badly the library made me buy them. I had never told it to Adaeze Okafor.

I told it to her then, obviously — you can't leave a woman standing on *he does not believe you exist* — and she received it delightedly, and Kimiko put down the novel and said something rapid and unfriendly-fast in Japanese with a smile at the corner of it, testing, and I answered — a little slow, a little formal, the accent of a man taught by a cassette — and got the laugh, and the cabin was warm and pleased with itself.

And walking back up to the flight deck, some cold junior clerk in the back of my head filed the sequence. She hadn't asked *do you speak Japanese*. Nobody had asked me anything. She'd built a little machine with a dispute in it and a doubting witness and a bet, and the machine's only output was Jack Marsh demonstrating, at conversational length, in front of an audience, exactly how much Japanese he had — and how he sounds under a native speaker's pressure — while feeling, the entire time, like the most charming man on the airplane.

I recognized the *shape* of it. It's my shape. I've used that exact machine on customs officers and a chief pilot and one memorable state trooper: never ask the question — build the room where the man wants to answer it.

*She'd have run a great restaurant,* I thought. *Forty hotels. That's forty rooms full of people telling her things.* And I sat back down in the right seat feeling flattered, which — I want this noted with a timestamp — is the exact feeling the technique is designed to produce.

"Good crowd?" Cole said.

"Great crowd. I did the Kowalski story."

"You love the Kowalski story."

"It's a good story."

"You're a performing seal for anyone who says the word *bet*," he said comfortably, and asked Center for lower.

---

Sitka gave us what the forecast promised, which is all you can ask of a forecast: showers on the field, ceiling ragged around fifteen hundred, wind off the sea with its shoulder down. I flew the RNAV to runway one one with the needles centered and the speed nailed, broke out at twelve hundred with the runway sitting exactly where the math said it would be — that's the entire religion, by the way; the whole faith is *the runway will be where the math says* — and Cole called the gusts like a man reading his kid a familiar book, and the G550 put her wheels on the wet asphalt of Baranof Island like she was apologizing for the weight.

The Juneau file rode along behind us the whole way, unopened. That's a successful alternate. Its whole career is to be fat, boring, and unused.

Deplaning, Teddy Vance stopped at the flight deck door, took in the panel, and said, "Now *that* is a proper office," and then, to Cole, with the both-hands handshake: "Captain. That landing. I've paid for smoother and never received it."

"Airplane did it," Cole said. "I'm mostly furniture."

"Ha!" said Teddy Vance, and pointed at him like Cole had scored, and went down the stairs into the rain without putting his hood up, which told me he'd been raised somewhere wet and rich enough not to care.

---

The lodge was forty minutes by covered boat, up a channel the color of gunmetal, to a dock and a long cedar building with smoke standing out of two chimneys, and I will say this about Adaeze Okafor's investment properties: whatever else was true about that day — and a great deal else was true about that day — the salmon was magnificent.

We were invited. That's not standard. Standard is the crew stays with the airplane, eats something in cellophane, and guards the coffee. But Okafor had said, "My aviators do not lunch in a hangar while I lunch in a lodge; it makes the food disloyal," and Cole had looked at me and I had looked at the fuel truck's ETA, and we'd gone.

Lunch was long and shrewd. The associates walked the property between courses with the lodge's manager, and there was a photographer — unremarkable, efficient, a woman who shot the group on the dock, the group at the rail, the group examining the smokehouse — *for the prospectus*, someone said. I watched Okafor arrange those photographs without appearing to arrange anything: a hand on an elbow, a laugh redirected, *Kimiko, stand where the light forgives us all* — and every grouping composed itself around her like iron filings being modest. It occurred to me that she'd have been a monstrous wedding planner. It occurred to me the woman could place people the way Cole places fuel loads. It did not occur to me to ask what the pictures were *for*, beyond the obvious, because the obvious was standing right there being plausible, the way it had been standing there for eleven months.

And Teddy Vance spent the entire dock hour on my captain.

I want to be fair to Teddy, because fairness is cheap now and I can afford it: he was good. He was genuinely funny; he knew a little about airplanes, the flattering amount, enough to ask questions that let a man talk; and he'd made Cole for what Cole is inside of a minute — not the furniture line, the thing under it — and gone straight at it. He asked about the Gulfstream's legs the way you'd ask a rancher about his best horse. He asked about weather flying with his whole face. He laughed at things that were, by my count from eleven feet away, at best forty-percent funny, and he touched Cole's forearm when he did it — four times; the dock had boards, the boards did the counting — and somewhere between the second touch and the third he mentioned, in the smooth patter of a man laying planks over a stream one at a time, that his fund kept an apartment in Singapore that was a crime to leave empty, and that if the captain ever found himself with days off on that side of the Pacific —

"— though I imagine you're never off," said Teddy Vance, giving him the full high-beams. "Men like you."

Here is what a professional observer, which is what I am, observed, from eleven feet, over a plate of salmon:

My captain being flawless with a client. That's all. That's what it was, and I watched it the way an off-duty dealer watches another table — professionally, appreciating craft. The warmth up full and the door quietly shut behind it. The laugh — the real-sounding one he can produce at will, which four hundred people on our field would swear is the whole man. The forearm suffered to be touched, because the account was worth more than the forearm. And the escape, when it came, executed so gently that Teddy never felt the airflow change: "Days off I spend on a wheat farm fixing my dad's fence, and you'd revoke the invitation if you saw my luggage" — self-deprecating, geographically fatal, completely closed, and delivered with a smile that sent the man away *pleased* about it.

Textbook. I'd have graded it a hundred. I have watched Cole do the equivalent forty times — with a fuel-truck driver in Bozeman, with drunk charter guests, with Jax Calloway, God knows — and enjoyed every performance.

I would like, therefore, for someone to explain the salmon.

Because the salmon, which had been magnificent, stopped being anything at all somewhere around forearm-touch three, and I set down my fork, and I found I had opinions about a stranger's blazer, of all the mirthless things, and about the phrase *men like you* in the mouth of a man who had known him for two hours. I ran a full instrument scan on the feeling and got nothing usable off any of it. Long day. Early show. Salmon's rich. And nobody enjoys watching craft wasted on a customer who thinks he's buying the craftsman — that's a union feeling; any tradesman would have it.

I filed it under *lunch*, is where I filed it.

The clerk in the back of my head, who by that point in the day was developing a caseload, noted for the record that when Teddy Vance had spent ninety seconds at the flight deck door being charming at *me* that morning, I had felt nothing whatsoever except mild approval of his tailor.

File closed. Next item. I'm told hindsight is a flight instructor. Everything she says starts with *you had all the information.*

---

Okafor found me at the end of the dock while the coffee was going around, on the weathered bench there, and sat down beside me with her cup, and for a while said nothing, which from her is not absence — it's an appointment.

The channel did its channel things. Rain walked around on the water, politely, over there.

"January," she said, eventually, to the water. "Was it? The airline class you did not take."

I looked at her.

"February," she said, correcting herself, "no — January, because Marcus tells the story with the roads icy, and Marcus's stories are decorated but never moved." She turned the cup in her hands, that slow quarter-turn. "A man waits eleven years for a seniority number, and declines it from a hospital corridor, and goes back in to sit with his father. And then does not ever mention it again, which is the part I find" — she chose the word like a chocolate — "*expensive*."

"Griff talks," I said.

"Marcus adores you both and is made entirely of doors," she agreed. "I never even knock."

"It wasn't a hard call," I said, which is what I always say, because it's true. "Some decisions don't have a brief. You just fly them."

"Mm," said Adaeze Okafor, exactly the way she'd said it on a ramp in the rain three weeks earlier, and I felt the sound go into a ledger somewhere, and sat there on a bench having been, I now know, weighed for the second time before coffee finished going around. "And do you regret it? On the bad Tuesdays. Be truthful; the water is listening and cares nothing for either of us."

I gave that the four seconds it deserved, because she'd asked it the way you'd ask a real question, and on the far end of the dock Cole was laughing at something the lodge manager said, with the laugh that isn't the client one — bigger, dumber, the one with the hiccup in the middle of it that embarrasses him — and Teddy Vance was watching my captain laugh with an expression I declined to have an opinion about.

"No," I said. "I keep the email. I never re-read it."

"You keep it."

"Archived. Not deleted." I shrugged the one-shoulder shrug. "You keep the chart after the trip. Doesn't mean you're flying back."

Adaeze Okafor looked at me for a long second, and something happened at the very back of her eyes that I took — filed, wrote down, initialed — as an old woman being fond of a young man's sentence.

"No," she said. "It means you know what the trip cost." She rose, shook rain off nothing, and patted my shoulder once. "Come, we are leaving; if Teddy buys anything else today it will be the airplane."

---

Cole flew us home into the last of the light, and the associates slept the sleep of people whose money had had a productive day, and somewhere over the Dixon Entrance, with the flight deck dark and the strobes doing their slow heartbeat against the cloud below us, I ran the day's paperwork in my head, because that's what I do instead of thinking.

An owner we'd never met. A client who could take a man's whole biography without asking a question. A phone call at six in the morning from somebody who knew numbers Griff doesn't publish. A photographer who shot the group like evidence. A charter that made money for everyone and sense for no one.

I lined them all up, and then I did the thing I'd been doing all day, the thing I am — I want this understood — *professionally excellent* at, which is finding for each item the nearest plausible shelf and putting it there. Rich people are eccentric. Hoteliers read guests. Vultures circle small operators every winter. Prospectuses need photos. Shelf, shelf, shelf, shelf. The room looked clean when I was done. The room always looked clean.

"You're quiet," Cole said, over the top of the descent checklist.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're never quiet. You're *smooth*. It's different, and this is neither." He reached up and dimmed the panel a shade against the coming dark, eyes still outside. "The overheard thing?"

I hadn't told him about Griff's phone call. I hadn't decided not to, either; the day just hadn't offered the door. And what I heard myself say, in the tone I use for true things when I'd rather they were smaller, was:

"Somebody knows our numbers, Cole. Whoever called Griff this morning — they knew the note on the hangar. That's not gossip. Gossip gets the size wrong. That's someone who's *seen paper*."

Cole didn't answer right away. Below us the deck was breaking; Seattle put a long orange smear on the underside of the sky, and the two of us sat in front of it, descending, in the kind of cockpit quiet that has a whole conversation in it about who tells Griff, and when, and how you guard a company that doesn't know it needs guarding.

"Monday," he said finally. "I'll take him to breakfast. You take the trash-can lid off gently or the raccoon runs."

"You just called Griff a raccoon."

"I called the *problem* a raccoon."

"He'd honestly take it as a compliment."

"He would," Cole said, and keyed up and got us lower, and the day filed itself down toward the pattern, and the runway, as always, was exactly where the math said.

Two knocks had gotten us off the ground that morning; habit doesn't take the return leg off. But I'll tell you the actual last entry in the actual log of that Thursday, the one nobody signs. It was the walk across the dark ramp afterward, Cole ahead of me with both flight bags because he'd picked mine up without asking, like always, telling me over his shoulder — wet, again; unhurried, again — what his mother would have done to Teddy Vance's blazer, and me laughing, and the whole warm ordinary machinery of us running just as designed.

I had watched technique all day and filed all of it.

Nobody had less excuse than I did. That's the joke. I *invented* the shelf.
