# Chapter Two — The Routing Request

*Cole*

My father can tell you what the afternoon is going to do by looking at the Blue Mountains over breakfast. He has never in his life read a forecast discussion. He dusts wheat for a living out of a patched-up Ag Cat with a radio that works when it feels like it, and if you ask him how he knows, he'll say the mountains told him, which is not mysticism, it's forty years of looking at the same ridge every morning and remembering.

I couldn't do that. I left. So I do it the long way, with charts, in the dark, in a crew room that smells like carpet glue and other people's coffee, and it takes me about forty minutes where it takes him a glance out a window over a plate of eggs. But it's the same job. You are trying to find out what the air intends to do to you, and you are trying to find it out early enough that you still have choices.

Thursday morning I was doing it at four fifteen because Priya had put something on my desk on Wednesday night that I didn't understand.

It was a trip request from our client, and it read like this. Depart Boeing Field Friday. Fly to Anchorage. Overnight. Return Saturday. Zero passengers both ways.

Zero.

I read it three times looking for the part I'd missed, which is a habit and also, that week, a mistake, because there wasn't one. Adaeze Okafor wanted us to take a fifty-million-dollar airplane fourteen hundred miles up the coast with nobody in the back, park it overnight at a field in Alaska, and bring it home empty. At full charter rate. Which she had already wired.

At the bottom, in a note Priya had copied out of the email:

*Please have Captain Bishop fly it as though I were aboard. And I should like a short written report afterward — not the invoice, the decisions. What you chose, and why.*

Griff, when I showed him, held the paper at arm's length like it might be contagious.

"She's paying us to fly an empty airplane to Anchorage."

"Yes."

"And back."

"Yes."

"Cole." He set it down. "Do you know what our number looks like this month? Because I do, and this fixes about a third of it, and I want you to know I am prepared to not ask a single question."

"I'm going to ask a couple."

"I know you are," Griff said. "That's why I said it out loud, so I'd have said something."

He's not a coward, Griff. He is a man who has been standing on a ramp since he was twenty-two watching money decide things, and he has made his peace with the parts of it he can't change. He also, for the record, would have backed me. He always has. That's not nothing, and in this business it's close to rare.

I called the client's assistant and got the answer inside a minute, which is the part that stayed with me. No hesitation, no improvising. The owner's trust wanted the airframe positioned north for a possible charter the following week; if it didn't materialize, the flight was a deadhead, and Ms. Okafor considered a well-run deadhead a fine use of money.

Every word of that was plausible. Owners do reposition airplanes. Trusts do speculate on trips. And Rainier Aviation Holdings had been telling us where to put this airplane for eleven months from an address in Delaware and a lawyer who answered his email on Fridays.

I said thank you and hung up, and I sat in the crew room with the request in front of me, and here's what I actually thought, in order: *this is strange; this is legal; this pays the shop.*

Then I went and built the weather, because whatever else it was, it was a trip to Alaska in October, and Alaska in October does not care whose money it is.

---

I do it the same way every time, in the same order, and I do it before I look at anything anybody has decided for me — before the dispatch release, before the flight plan Priya's already roughed in, before Jack's routing. Not because they're wrong. Because if I look at their answer first, I'll spend the morning agreeing with it.

Big to small. That's the whole method.

I start with what the whole sky is doing — the surface analysis and the prog charts, where the lows are, where the fronts are, which way the whole machine is turning. In October the Gulf of Alaska is a factory. It builds lows and fires them southeast down the coast one after another like somebody feeding boards through a saw, and the only real questions are how fast the next one is coming and whether we're in front of it or behind it.

That morning there was a good one. Nine seventy-eight millibars, sitting southwest of Kodiak, turning like it meant it.

Then medium: the graphical forecasts, the icing and turbulence panels, the freezing levels. Then small: the actual airports, the actual hours, the actual numbers.

Anchorage's forecast, when I got to it, was a piece of writing I want to walk through, because everything I decided that week came out of about nine lines of teletype.

The first group was clean. Wind one four zero at twelve, ten miles, broken at four thousand. That's the forecaster's main story — the thing he'll defend. Then a FM group Friday afternoon, from twenty-two hundred Zulu: wind swinging around and freshening, visibility down to four in light snow, ceiling down to a thousand overcast. FM means *from* — a real change, a new regime, the forecaster telling you the weather has moved house.

Then the two lines people get wrong.

TEMPO, twenty-three Zulu to zero three Zulu: two miles, moderate snow, ceiling six hundred.

And underneath it, PROB30 zero two to zero six Zulu: half a mile, snow, ceiling two hundred.

Most pilots read those and hear *maybe*. They're not maybe. They're two different sentences and they mean two different things.

TEMPO is the forecaster saying: this will happen, in bursts, and it will not last more than an hour at a stretch, and it'll be there less than half the time. It is not a possibility. It is a promise of intermittency. You plan for it. You fly assuming you will get it, because in a six-hour window you will.

PROB30 is different. PROB30 is the forecaster saying: something is arguing with me. Three times in ten, the thing in that group is what you'll get, and I am not confident enough to write it as the story, but I am worried enough that I refuse to leave it out. A PROB30 line in a TAF is not noise. It's a professional telling you where he's uneasy. When a forecaster admits he might be wrong, you should not round that down to zero. You should go find out what he's afraid of.

What he was afraid of was the low southwest of Kodiak.

So I went and looked at the airmen's meteorological advisories. AIRMET Sierra for IFR conditions and mountain obscuration through most of Southcentral, which in October is practically a matter of geography. AIRMET Tango for moderate turbulence below eighteen thousand along the coast — that's the low, throwing its weight around. AIRMET Zulu for moderate icing between four thousand and eighteen, freezing level right down on the deck at Anchorage by Friday night.

Sierra, Tango, Zulu. IFR, turbulence, icing. Three letters and you know what kind of day it is.

Then the SIGMET, which was the one I sat up for. A SIGMET is not an advisory; it's a warning, and it applies to everybody, and the fact that it exists at all means somebody looked at the situation and decided that ordinary language wasn't sufficient. This one was for severe icing over the northern Gulf, valid a window that just clipped the back end of our return leg.

And then, last, because it's last for a reason, I read the pilot reports.

Everything above the PIREPs is a forecast. A forecast is an argument. It is a very good, very disciplined, extremely well-informed argument made by a professional who is not in the airplane. A PIREP is the only piece of paper in the whole stack that is a *fact*: somebody was up there, in it, at a time and a place and an altitude, and this is what it did to them. My father would put it less politely. He'd say the mountains don't file forecasts.

There were four out of the Gulf that morning. A 737 at two four zero reporting continuous light chop and negative ice. A Caravan at eight thousand out of Yakutat reporting moderate mixed, which is a Caravan pilot's way of saying *I left*. And two more that told me the same story: whatever the low was going to do, it was doing it below about twelve thousand, and it was doing it wet.

I sat with all of that for a while.

Here's what the weather said, once I'd finished arguing with it. Friday's flight up was fine — ahead of it, easy, clean arrival with the front still offshore. Friday night in Anchorage would be genuinely lousy and would not matter, because we would be in a hotel. And Saturday's flight home would depend entirely on whether that nine-seventy-eight low had done what lows do, which is arrive earlier than advertised.

Which meant the trip was flyable, and the decision wasn't Friday's. It was Saturday's, and it would be made Saturday, with Saturday's information, and my job on Thursday was to make sure Saturday had options in it.

So I built options. A second alternate with real minimums instead of a legal one. Fuel to hold and then go somewhere with a longer runway and a hotel. A departure window Saturday morning that got us south of Yakutat before the SIGMET's window opened, and a decision gate at the top of climb where we'd either continue or turn around and buy Griff another night's crew hotel, which is the cheapest thing in aviation and the hardest to talk people into.

I wrote all of it down. She'd asked for the decisions, and she was going to get them.

---

Jack came in at six with a paper bag and two coffees and read my whole file standing up, in about ninety seconds, which is a thing he can do and I have stopped being impressed by out loud.

"She's paying us to deadhead to Anchorage."

"She is."

"For no reason."

"For a stated reason."

"Cole." He put the coffee down. "That's the same sentence twice."

"I know what it is." I took the coffee. "Tell me what you see on Saturday."

He looked at the TAF for maybe four seconds.

"I see a PROB30 that's going to be the whole trip," he said. "And I see you already built a second alternate, which means you think so too, and I see that you put us wheels-up at fifteen Zulu instead of eighteen, which is going to make everybody at that FBO hate us." He glanced up. "Do we care?"

"No."

"Good. I don't either." He pulled the other chair around backward and sat on it. "What's her real reason?"

"I don't know."

"You don't like not knowing."

"I don't." I closed the folder. "But I've been not-liking it for eleven months and the checks have cleared and nobody's asked me to do anything I wouldn't do. There's a difference between a client who's strange and a client who's a problem. Strange is most of them."

Jack didn't say anything for a second. He gets very still when he's actually thinking, as opposed to when he's talking, which is most of the time, and the two things look nothing alike once you know him.

"You'll tell me," he said. "If it moves from one to the other."

"Yeah."

"Okay," he said, and got up, and stole half my breakfast on the way out the door, which he has done approximately four hundred times and has never once acknowledged.

---

Priya caught me in the hallway around ten.

"A guy called for you yesterday afternoon. Twice, actually — once before you got back from the run, once after." She checked a sticky note. "Said he was a writer. Working on something about safety culture at small charter operators. Wanted to talk to captains who'd 'been through things.'"

"Did he leave a number?"

"No. That's why I wrote it down." She looked at me a beat longer than she needed to. Priya Chandra is twenty-nine and misses nothing and has the good manners to pretend otherwise about eighty percent of the time. "He knew you'd been at Cascade."

"Okay."

"Cole—"

"It's okay," I said. "Thanks for telling me. If he calls again, take a number and don't give him anything, including whether I still work here."

She went back to her desk, and I stood in the hallway for a minute doing what I do, which is nothing.

Five years ago I worked for an operator called Cascade Air Group, out of this same field, four hangars down. I found something in the training records that wasn't true. I did what you're supposed to do — I went inside first, and inside is where it died, and then I went to the FSDO, and after that the company needed a story that wasn't *we falsified records*, and it found one. The story was about me. It was a good story. It was built by somebody who knew exactly which parts of my life would make it land, and it landed, and I did not fly for money for two years.

I have never told it to anybody in this building. Griff hired me without asking. Voss made one phone call and never said what was in it. And I have spent three years being the most transparent man on this airfield about absolutely everything except that, which I am aware is a strange way to be honest, and which works fine, and which I intend to keep doing.

Somebody was asking. Somebody had been asking for about a week, going by what I'd already heard from a friend at another shop.

I decided it was a reporter, because that was the reasonable thing to decide, and because if you decide that a stranger asking about you means something worse, you will spend your whole life indoors.

---

I had dinner with Elias that night in Ballard.

Elias Ward is a structural engineer who does marine work — piers, ferry terminals, the unglamorous concrete that keeps a coastal city attached to itself — and he is a genuinely good man with a dry sense of humor and forearms from a sport I never asked about. We have been doing this for about a year. Dinner when the season allows. No questions past the ones you'd ask a colleague. It suits us both, or it suits me and he has never complained, which I have chosen to believe is the same thing.

He asked how work was. I told him about the empty airplane going to Anchorage, because it was funny and because it was true and because it cost me nothing.

"That's the whole trip?" he said. "Nobody in it?"

"Nobody in it."

"So it's just you and — Jack, right?" He was cutting something. He didn't look up. "The one you're always quoting."

"I don't quote him."

"Cole," Elias said, mildly, "you have quoted him twice tonight and we've been here forty minutes."

I said something about how you get that way flying with a person, which is true, and he let it go, which is what he does, and we talked about a ferry terminal in Bremerton for a while, and it was a nice evening.

He asked, later, at the door, whether I ever thought about doing something on a Saturday that wasn't dinner.

There is a rule I have, and it isn't about Elias.

The rule is that you do not, ever, get involved with somebody on your own crew. Not a first officer, not a mechanic, not a scheduler. Two people in a flight deck have to be able to disagree at four hundred knots without one of them managing the other's feelings, and the day that stops being true, the airplane has an extra system in it that nobody designed and nobody inspects. I've watched it. I know precisely what it costs, better than most, and I don't discuss why.

None of which has anything to do with a Saturday in Ballard.

"I'm in Anchorage Saturday," I said.

"Right," said Elias. "Sure."

---

We flew it Friday.

Empty airplanes fly like a completely different machine, light on the wheels and eager, and there is something faintly ridiculous about a G550 with fourteen empty seats and two guys up front eating gas station sandwiches over British Columbia. We ran it like a trip anyway. Full brief, full callouts, everything by the book, because she'd asked us to fly it as though she were aboard and because that's how I'd have flown it regardless, and somewhere over the Coast Mountains Jack said, without looking over:

"You know she's going to read that report."

"That's why I'm writing it."

"No — I mean she's going to *read* it. Line by line. Like a person who knows what it says." He watched the ridges go under for a while. "Clients don't ask for decisions. Clients ask for prices."

"You're the one who thinks everybody's up to something."

"I'm the one who's usually right," Jack said comfortably, and put his feet on the footwell rail, and the low southwest of Kodiak spun another notch, fourteen hundred miles ahead of us, in no particular hurry at all.
