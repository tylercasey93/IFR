# Chapter Three — Changi

*Wren*

I have a rule about passengers. I don't learn their names.

Not the real ones, not the ones on the manifest, not the ones they give you at the top of the airstairs with their hand out. I load them, I fly them, I unload them, and if you asked me a week later I could tell you their weight to the nearest ten pounds and nothing else. It is not coldness. It is a load sheet. A hundred and sixty pounds in seat two goes in the arithmetic the same whether it's a fisheries lawyer or a woman running for her life, and the arithmetic is the part that keeps her alive.

I have held that rule for four years, through six of these.

I broke it that Thursday, at thirty-nine thousand feet over the Philippine Sea, because a fifteen-year-old asked me what the airplane was called.

---

The tasking came at 1410 Singapore time while I was in a hangar at Seletar eating peanut M&Ms out of a doubled freezer bag, four at a time, which is how I eat them and which nobody has ever gotten an explanation for.

Chidinma, on a line that clicked twice before it opened.

"How long do you need."

Not *how are you*. I like her enormously.

"For what."

"Two passengers, Changi Business Aviation Centre, wheels up tonight. Destination New Chitose. The aeroplane is the Challenger, it is fuelled, and the crew is you and a copilot named Farid whom I am told you have flown with twice."

"Three times." I put the bag down. "Who are they?"

"A woman and her daughter," Chidinma said. "That is genuinely all you need, and it is also all I am going to enjoy saying, so let us both move on. Wren — this is a real one."

She didn't have to tell me that. You can hear a real one in the gaps. In the practice legs, the courier runs, the milk runs with a sealed case in the baggage bay, there's a rhythm to how the office talks to you: unhurried, faintly bored, everything in the passive voice. This was somebody reading me a time.

"What's the clock?"

"Their conference finishes its afternoon session at sixteen hundred. There is a coach to the hotel at sixteen fifteen. They will not be on it." A pause I didn't like. "And a delegation liaison will notice by dinner. Perhaps sooner."

Four hours. Maybe less.

I said, "Fine," which in my case is a complete sentence, and hung up and started on the paper.

---

Here's what people think happens when you take two human beings out of a country in a private jet: you put them on, you close the door, you go.

Here's what actually happens. Before that airplane moves one foot, an entire second airplane exists on paper, and it is that one — not the one made of aluminum — that gets you out. Every window, every seam, every hour of it has to be true enough to survive somebody bored looking at it twice.

The ICAO flight plan first, because everything else hangs off it. Not the domestic form you'd file in the States — the international one, the numbered boxes, the one that gets read by eight countries' systems between takeoff and landing and has to say the same thing to all of them.

Item 7: the aircraft identification, which is the registration or the callsign, and which had better match the letters painted on the side.

Item 8: flight rules and type of flight. I for IFR. G for general aviation, which is a lovely word for what we were doing.

Item 10: equipment and surveillance capability, which is a string of letters that tells the world what this machine can do — RNAV, required navigation performance, controller-pilot datalink, the transponder's flavor. Every letter in that box is a promise. Put a letter in it you can't back up and somewhere over the South China Sea a controller will ask you for something you can't deliver, and then you are a memorable airplane, and memorable is the whole thing we are trying not to be.

Item 15: speed, level, route. Airways and waypoints, the actual thread through Vietnamese and Chinese and Korean and Japanese airspace.

Item 16: destination, estimated elapsed time, and — this is the box people skip — the alternates. Two of them, for a leg like that. A jet full of people who cannot be turned around at a border needs somewhere to go that isn't back.

Item 18, the other-information box, where the truth lives in abbreviations. REG for the registration. DOF for date of flight. PBN for the navigation specs. Operator, contact numbers, and any remarks a receiving state needs so that nobody at the other end has to guess.

And Item 19. Supplementary information. Endurance. Emergency equipment. Survival gear, dinghies, jacket color.

And POB.

Persons on board. A number, in a box, filed with the state.

I have typed that box six hundred times. It is not a philosophical instrument. It exists so that if we go into the water off Luzon, a Filipino coast guard officer at three in the morning knows how many bodies to look for and stops looking when he has them all. That's it. That's the whole purpose of the number.

I typed a four. Two crew, two passengers.

The four was true. Everything on either side of it was arranged.

Then the general declaration — the gen dec, the crew and passenger list that gets handed to the officer at each end, with names and passport numbers and dates of birth. Then the landing permit for Japan, which the office already had, dated eleven days earlier, because Chidinma does not wake up and start problems, she wakes up and closes ones she opened weeks ago. Then Changi's slot and the parking and the handling agent. Then customs and immigration, both ends.

The names on the gen dec were not the names those two women were born with. I read them once, on purpose, and then I did the thing I do, which is stop.

Farid, my copilot, is thirty-four, Malaysian, and has the best hands in a crosswind I have personally seen. He knows exactly what our operator is and does not ask, because he's paid extremely well not to and because he's got a kid in an international school in KL. He looked at the fuel figure and said, "Twenty-eight hundred nautical, that's tight-ish with the winds tonight."

"It is."

"Tankering out of here or a stop?"

"No stop."

He didn't argue. He got out the performance tables, and I got out my father's E6-B — aluminum, heavier than it needs to be, the paint gone off the wind side — and ran the winds aloft by hand while the computer ran them too, because I like knowing when they disagree and by how much. They disagreed by four minutes.

We would land in Sapporo with an hour and change over the alternate. Adequate.

---

The Changi Business Aviation Centre at six in the evening is glass and quiet and orchids in pots and a coffee machine that costs more than my first car. Everybody moving through it is somebody's principal or somebody's staff, and everybody has the specific brisk incuriosity of people who are paid not to notice each other.

Which is exactly why they came out through there instead of somewhere clever. There's a corporate flow out of a conference like that — delegates, sponsors, the ones who leave early because their airplane is waiting. You don't hide beside a river. You get in it.

I stood by the airstairs in my third stripe and my flat face and watched the car come across the apron at 1841, forty minutes behind schedule, which is the longest forty minutes I have spent standing still.

Two women got out. One about my age, maybe a decade older, in the kind of good gray coat you buy for a conference, with a laptop bag and a rolling case and the flat, scoured calm of somebody running on the far side of adrenaline. The other one was fifteen and looked it: hood up, earbuds in, a backpack she was wearing on both shoulders like a kid on a field trip, staring at the Challenger like it had personally insulted her.

The mother stopped at the bottom of the stairs and looked at me for a long moment.

"You are the captain," she said. Textbook English, every consonant paid for.

"I am."

"Not what I expected."

"I get that."

She almost smiled. She was too tired to complete it. "How long is the flight?"

"Six forty on the plan. Probably six fifty tonight."

"And you have done this before."

I have found that people ask that question for one of two reasons, and the reason determines the answer.

"I've flown that route eleven times," I said, which was true, and which was not what she'd asked, and which was what she needed.

She nodded once and went up.

The girl stopped in front of me instead. Up close her eyes were red and she'd been crying somewhere in the last hour and had done a bad job of hiding it, and she stuck her chin out at me the way you do when you have decided that you would rather be furious than whatever the other thing is.

"Do I have to sit anywhere specific," she said.

"Yes. Third seat back on the right, until I say otherwise."

"Why."

"Because that's where the weight goes."

She thought about arguing and decided not to. She went up.

The handling agent brought the paperwork out. Twenty-two, blazer, name badge, doing this for the ninth time that day. He compared the gen dec to two passports, and he looked at the photographs, and he looked at the faces through the cabin window, and it took him eleven seconds, and in the ninth second a man in a polo shirt came out of the terminal doors behind him and stood there with a phone, not using it.

I did not look at the man. I squared the paperwork on the clipboard, corner to corner, twice, which is a thing I do when I need four extra seconds and would prefer nobody know I need them.

"Everything is fine, Captain," the agent said, and gave me my copy, and went back inside.

The man in the polo shirt was still there when we started engines. He was still there when we taxied. He might have been waiting for a Gulfstream. He might have been on a break.

I put him in the report and I did not put him in my chest, which is a distinction I have spent about eleven years learning and still get wrong.

---

We were wheels up at 1927 into a sky the color of a bruise, out over the Strait with the anchored ships lit up like a spilled drawer of jewelry, and then the long left turn north.

It is a beautiful piece of the world to fly through and I have never once looked at it properly.

Vietnamese control, then Chinese, then the long stretch east of Taiwan, then Fukuoka. Position reports, level changes, a re-route around weather off Luzon that Farid negotiated in his second-best voice. The airplane did what Challengers do, which is go a long way without complaining. I ate M&Ms four at a time. Farid told me about his daughter's violin recital, which he had missed, and about which he was philosophical in a way I recognized as not philosophical at all.

Around 2300 the cabin door opened and the girl came forward and stood in the flight deck doorway with her hood down.

"My mother's asleep," she said, like an accusation.

"Good."

"She hasn't slept since Sunday."

"Then it's very good."

She stood there. Fifteen-year-olds have a particular way of occupying a doorway when they have decided you owe them something and they don't know what.

"Sit down if you're standing," I said, and pointed at the jumpseat, and she sat down on it hard, the way they do.

Out in front of us the sky had gone completely black and completely clear, and the stars over the Philippine Sea at thirty-nine thousand feet are not the stars anybody sees from the ground. They don't twinkle. There's no air left above you to make them do it. They just sit there, hard and steady, all the way down to a horizon you can pick out because that's where they stop.

She looked at it for about four minutes without saying anything, which I respected.

"What's it called," she said.

"What's what called?"

"The plane. It has to have a name. Everything has a name."

"It has a registration."

"That's not a name, that's a *license plate*." She said it with enormous scorn, and it was the first thing out of her all night that sounded like a person instead of a survivor. "Ships have names. This is like a ship."

I should have said *it's a Challenger 605*. That is the correct answer and it is also, in this line of work, the entire correct posture: give them the make and model, give them the flight time, do not give them anything with your fingerprints on it.

Instead I heard myself say: "My father had a boat called the *Marta June*."

"Was that his wife?"

"His mother."

"Was she nice?"

"I never met her. He said she was mean as a snake and could gut ninety fish an hour."

The girl laughed. It surprised both of us — a real one, cracked in the middle, too loud for the flight deck. She put her hand over her mouth immediately and looked back toward the cabin.

"She's asleep," I said. "You're allowed."

"Okay." She pulled her sleeves over her hands. "Then this one is *Marta June*. Until you get a better one."

"That's not how registration works."

"I don't care," said Mara Sorokina, and looked back out at the stars.

Which is how I learned her name, at thirty-nine thousand feet, four hours from Japan, in violation of a rule I had kept for four years and six operations.

I have thought about it since. I don't think I broke it because she was a kid, exactly. I think it was the two-shoulder backpack. I think it was that somebody, at some point in the last seventy-two hours, had told that girl to pack one bag, and she had packed it the way you pack for school.

My father put a summer's money in an envelope on a kitchen table in Ketchikan when I was seventeen so I could go learn to do this, in a year when there wasn't a summer's money, and told me that one of us should get to look down at the water for a change. He never flew with me. There was always another season, and then there wasn't.

I don't talk about that. I don't think about it much either, most days.

But there is a specific thing that happens in me when a parent has put a child in an airplane and gone to sleep, and it is not professional, and it does not appear anywhere on the load sheet.

---

We landed at New Chitose at 0612 local into low cloud and a wet runway and that particular Hokkaido gray that has no bottom edge to it.

Japanese arrival procedure is precise and unhurried and completely humorless, which I have always liked. Quarantine, immigration, customs, in that order, each one at its own counter, each one working from the paperwork I'd filed nine hours earlier from a hangar in Singapore.

An officer read the general declaration. He read the passports. He read the general declaration again. He asked the purpose of the visit and the mother said *technical consultancy* in her textbook English and offered a folder before he asked for it, which was correct, and he took it and read that too.

Twenty-six minutes, start to finish.

I have never in my life been so aware of the four in Item 19.

The car was in the freight lot, a plain gray van with a hotel's crest on the door, and a woman from Adaeze's staff who introduced herself to nobody. The mother stopped at the van and turned around and came back three steps to where I was standing with my flight bag.

"Thank you," she said.

"You're welcome."

"You have not asked me one question."

"No."

"That is unusual," said Dr. Yelena Sorokina. "In my experience, everyone asks. Even the ones who are not supposed to."

"I'm not going to know anything you'd have to think about later," I said.

She looked at me for a second with an expression I have seen exactly twice before, both times on people who had been holding something up alone for a long while and had just felt somebody put a hand under the other end of it.

"Then I will tell you one thing you did not ask," she said. "My daughter has not laughed since the fourteenth of September."

She got in the van.

Mara got in after her, and then rolled the window down two inches, and said, through the gap, "Bye, *Marta June*," and put the window back up before I could answer.

---

I sent the report at 0810 from a chair in the corner of the crew lounge with a coffee I didn't drink.

*Delivered 0612L, no incident. Documents survived scrutiny both ends. Handling agent at Changi routine. One unidentified male, thirties, polo shirt, standing airside at the BAC without evident purpose for approximately six minutes during our departure sequence — probably nothing. Filing it anyway.*

*Passengers are in good order. Mother is exhausted and holding. Daughter is fifteen.*

*On the hide: staff quarters is clean, the crest on the van is not, and there are one hundred and forty people employed at that property. Any one of them can count. I'd give it days, not weeks.*

Adaeze answered before I finished the coffee I wasn't drinking, which meant it was ten past nine at night in Seattle and she was awake and had been waiting.

*Understood. Days, not weeks — I agree, and I have already begun. Rest. You will fly again shortly and I will need you sharp.*

Then, thirty seconds later, a second message.

*The daughter. Fifteen, you said.*

*Yes.*

There was a pause of about a minute and a half. For Adaeze Okafor, that's a long time to hold a card.

*Then we will not be putting her on a commercial aeroplane,* she wrote. *We will want an aircraft nobody has any reason to look at, and a crew I would trust with my own life.*

*Do you have one?*

*I have been finding out for eleven months,* wrote Adaeze. *Go to sleep, Wren.*
