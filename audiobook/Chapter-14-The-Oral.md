# Chapter Fourteen — The Oral

*Theo*

The training center in Tukwila smelled like carpet, coffee, and consequence. Somewhere beyond the glass wall of the briefing room, the King Air simulator crouched on its hydraulic legs like a patient spider, and across the table from Theo, Dale Voss opened a folder, clicked a pen, and said the eight most frightening words in aviation:

"Relax. This is just a conversation between professionals."

Griff had arranged the day months out — a formal mock oral and sim session, the dress rehearsal for the upgrade check that would come whenever Meridian's second captain seat did. Wren had dropped Theo at the door with coffee and a kiss (Amendment Three permitting kisses in parking lots, following a brief and enjoyable regulatory review) and the parting words: *"He's going to try to make you defend something wrong. Don't."*

"Let's start easy," Voss said, in the tone of a man who had never once started easy. "You're captain now. Your new first officer flew four approaches five months ago and nothing since. Yesterday he did six approaches in the sim with a CFII. Legal to be your SIC under IFR today?"

"If the sim work covered the full sixty-one fifty-seven package — approaches, holding, intercepting and tracking — and it was in an approved device with an authorized instructor, then his six-month clock reset yesterday. Legal. Whether he's *ready* is my problem, and I'd want a leg with him before the weather does the checking for me."

"Mm. Your alternate for tonight forecasts five hundred overcast at your ETA. It's got one ILS. Legal alternate?"

"No, sir. Filing test at an alternate with a precision approach is six hundred and two. Five hundred fails it — I need a different airport on the paper. And if I actually *divert* there later and the weather's five hundred, I can still shoot the ILS to its real two-hundred-foot minimums. The filing test and the flying test are different exams."

"Why do we make you file one at all, if you can go anywhere once airborne?"

"Because the alternate requirement is fuel arithmetic wearing a legal costume. Forcing the alternate forces the ninety-one one-sixty-seven fuel: destination, alternate, forty-five minutes. The rule isn't about the airport. It's about making sure the tanks contain a plan B."

Voss made a mark. It was impossible to tell what kind. "MEL versus ninety-one two-thirteen. One sentence each."

"MEL: an approved book that *is* the law for your tail number — look up the item, apply the relief and conditions, or ground it. Two-thirteen-delta: the no-book flow — required by the day's rule, the cert basis, an AD, or the POH? Fix it or don't go; otherwise remove or deactivate, placard, and a competent someone decides it's no hazard."

"A VOR check with a dual cross-check. Allowable error."

"Four degrees between the two receivers. Not four from a known radial — four from *each other*. And it's ink or it didn't happen: date, place, bearing error, signature."

"You're holding at six thousand. What's your max speed and when did you need to be at it?"

"Two hundred knots indicated at six thousand and below; slowed within three minutes of the fix, before crossing it."

"Center clears you to a fix that isn't your destination and says nothing else. You arrive. Radios still work; the controller's just busy. What are you doing?"

"Holding at the fix, standard pattern on my inbound course, at my last assigned altitude — and asking, with increasing sincerity, for further clearance and an EFC before I get there."

"Hm." Voss turned a page. "You're twenty minutes into a hold. Your EFC was five minutes ago. Comms are fine. Do you leave?"

"No, sir. With working radios the EFC is just an old promise — I stay and I ask. The EFC only becomes self-service authority if the radios die. Lost comm is the one customer the EFC always pays out for."

"We'll do lost comm properly another day," Voss said, with the faint relish of a man saving dessert. "Last one before the box. Your company's biggest client asks you, mid-flight, over the cabin speaker, whether the weather ahead is dangerous. It is. Your owner is also on the airplane. Answer the question."

Theo blinked. "Out loud, sir? 'Ma'am, yes — there's weather ahead I'm not willing to fly through, so here's what we're doing instead,' and then the plan, in plain sentences. The dangerous version of that conversation is the one where the client learns the truth *after* the turn instead of before it."

Voss looked at him for a long moment over the folder. "That answer," he said, "is why Marcus Griffin hasn't sold that airplane. All right, Marsh. Box time. Try not to make me write anything down."

---

At the lunch break Theo took his sandwich to the parking lot for air and called his father, per treaty — Sam had demanded "telemetry at the half" like the whole day was a game he had money on.

"How's the old buzzard treating you?"

"He asked me eleven questions about alternates and made a mark I couldn't read. It's like being audited by fog."

"Good sign. When Voss thinks you're hopeless he gets *chatty*." A clink of the good mug; Sam settling in. "Your mother used to say you'd end up a captain, you know. You'd have been — nine? Ten? You made the neighbor kids do a walkaround on their bikes before the big ride to the quarry. Full checklist. Tire pressure by thumb. She took a picture of it; it's in one of the albums — six bikes in a row and you pointing at a chain." A pause, the good kind. "She'd have liked Wren. Same species. The ones who point at the chain."

"Dad."

"I'm just saying it's in the albums," Sam said, innocent as a fair-weather cumulus. "Go fly the box. Don't let him fail your engine at anything low."

"He's absolutely going to fail my engine at something low."

"Well," said Sam Marsh, "then fly the half you've got left, and call me at the whistle."

---

The simulator did what simulators do: took every comfortable assumption Theo owned and fed them, one at a time, into a wood chipper.

The first hour was almost pleasant — steep turns, an ILS, a missed approach into published holding, Voss's scenarios stacking weather on failure on distraction with a craftsman's pacing. Then, level at eight thousand in simulated night IMC, straight-and-level, autopilot off because Voss had quietly failed it forty minutes ago, the left engine let go.

Not with a bang — with a *sag*. Torque unwinding, ITT drifting, the airplane leaning into the dead engine like a tired man against a doorframe, and the yaw — the yaw was the whole exam. In the goggles-gray of instrument night there was no horizon to lean on; there was only the panel: ball sliding right, heading walking left, the attitude indicator tipping into a bank he hadn't commanded.

*Dead foot, dead engine.* His left foot was doing nothing; his right foot was standing on the floor holding the nose straight. Left engine.

"Engine failure, left," Theo said, and flew the litany he'd taught for years and never before needed with his own hands full of airplane and his own inner ear campaigning for the opposition. Rudder to the ball — *step on the good one*. Pitch for blue line — Vyse, best single-engine climb, the speed that buys altitude when altitude is the only currency left. Bank a couple of degrees into the good engine, ball half out toward the live side — zero-sideslip, the drag-minimum trim state where the airplane flies straightest through the air even though the panel ball sits slightly ajar. Then, and only then, the drill: "Mixtures, props, throttles forward — flaps up, gear up — identify: dead foot, dead right— dead *left* engine—" saying it wrong once and catching it, which was the entire reason the *verify* step existed— "verify: retarding left throttle—" no change; the sag was real; the left engine was truly dead and not lying about it— "feather left, secure per checklist."

"Feathered," Voss said, from the god-seat, setting the sim's left prop to the knife-edge drag of a real feather. "Tell me why you're alive."

"Because the prop's feathered and the airplane's clean. A windmilling propeller is a barn door — it's the single biggest drag item on the airplane, worse than the gear. Feathering it is the difference between a machine that climbs on one engine and a machine that descends with style. After that it's arithmetic: this airplane single-engine gives me maybe a couple hundred feet a minute at blue line at this weight — a number with no generosity in it. Every knot below Vyse and every item of drag hanging out is subtracted from a margin that started small."

"And Vmc? The famous red line. Why does it matter less right now than students think?"

"Because Vmc is about *control*, not performance — the speed below which full rudder can't hold the nose against full power on the good engine. Danger lives low and slow and dirty with high power: the go-around, the botched approach. Up here at blue line, I've got control margin to spare; my problem is *performance*. The discipline is never letting the speed decay toward the region where the control problem wakes up — because the Vmc departure isn't a stall, it's a snap roll with paperwork."

"Good. Fly the ILS. Single-engine. And Marsh—" the scenario weather crumpled to two hundred and a half "—the client's aboard."

The single-engine ILS was forty minutes of concentration compressed into six: power on the live engine doing double duty for speed *and* glideslope, rudder trim carrying the load, configuration held clean and late — *gear down at glideslope intercept, flaps approach only, landing flaps only with the runway made* — because on one engine, drag added early is altitude you can't buy back. "And the go-around brief changes," Theo narrated, needle-riding down the wire, "single-engine, once I'm below about four hundred feet with landing flaps out, I'm *committed* — the single-engine go-around from low and dirty is a maneuver the performance charts regard as fiction. So the brief is: below commit height, we land. Somewhere on the airport. That's not defeatism; that's arithmetic with a spine."

He broke out at minimums, landed on the live engine's patient thrust, and kept it on the centerline with rudder that his right leg would remember for a week.

The screen froze. The motion parked itself with a hydraulic sigh.

"Well," said Dale Voss.

He clicked his pen twice, which — Theo would learn from Wren later — was, from Voss, roughly a standing ovation.

"Debrief's short," Voss said. "Your engine-out flow is clean, your verify step caught your own wrong call, which is *why the step exists*, and your commit-height brief is airline-grade. Two writedowns: you let blue line wander three knots in the level turn — three knots you own, always — and your securing checklist waited a beat long because you were narrating for my benefit. Stop teaching during emergencies, Marsh. There's only one student in the airplane when it's real, and it's the airplane." The pen went into the pocket, and something in the dry old face shifted a millimeter toward warmth. "When Meridian's seat opens, my recommendation goes in the same day. You'll pass. Now — one item outside the syllabus."

Theo braced.

"Your captain," Voss said. "Hale. I gave her her one-thirty-five checks when she washed up from that Cascade mess — angriest good pilot I ever met. Flew like the airplane owed her money." He gathered his folder, unhurried. "Rode along on an Okafor leg last month, jump seat, unannounced. Watched you two run a flight deck like a twenty-year crew. Watched her *laugh*." He paused at the door. "In this business we pretend CRM's a technique. Mostly it's just whether the two people up front would go to the wall for each other. Whatever you two are calling whatever this is — " the pen reappeared, pointed, dry as the Pendleton desert " — keep it off my checkride and on your airplane, and consider me," a click, "*pre-invited* to whatever gets catered."

He was gone before Theo could assemble any response beyond opening and closing his mouth like a man rehearsing a readback for a clearance he'd never once expected to receive.

---

It was in the parking lot, of all places — waiting for Wren, watching the evening traffic stack up on the 5 like a badly run arrival — that the realization finished landing.

His phone had buzzed at some point during the sim. An email. *RE: Flight crew recruitment — we've reopened our class dates, and pilots with your file are eligible for direct-entry—* the regional, three years later, hiring again, offering him the exact seat he'd walked away from in a hospital corridor. The deferred life, callback issued, holding short and cleared into position.

Theo read it twice, standing in the cold with his flight bag on his shoulder and his leg still humming from the rudder work.

And felt — nothing. No tug. No grief. Somewhere between a briefing-room argument in October and a snowed-in radiator in Pendleton, the file marked *the life I was supposed to have* had quietly gone inactive, all its contents transferred. He thought of Voss's pen clicking twice; of Sam's folder with the certificate; of a legal pad with amendments in two hands' writing; of a captain who planned alternates for everything and had, for reasons that still astonished him nightly, stopped needing one for him.

*You just fly the airplane you're in,* he'd told her once, palms up, about the biggest decision of his life.

The airplane he was in was a twenty-two-year-old King Air with a client who cried at approach lights, an owner who couldn't do math, a check airman who ate free at weddings, and Wren Hale in the left seat.

He archived the email without replying, and the old Subaru with the mismatched fender pulled up to the curb, and the window came down on the seventh documented laugh before he'd even said anything at all.

"You survived Voss," she said. "You're smiling like an idiot. Debrief."

He got in. Kissed her — parking lot; permitted; policy — and gave her the short form, which was becoming their evening liturgy, the day handed across the pedestal one item at a time:

"Engine-out lives in the feet first: dead foot, dead engine — then *verify* with the throttle, because the litany exists to catch your own wrong call, and mine did. Blue line is the whole economy; a windmilling prop is a barn door; feather buys the climb. Zero-sideslip: a couple degrees into the good engine, ball half out — the panel looks wrong when the airplane's flying right. Vmc is a control problem that lives low, slow, and dirty — respect it by never visiting. Single-engine approach: drag late, gear at glideslope, flaps when the runway's yours, and below commit height you land — arithmetic with a spine. Voss says my flow's clean, my speed control owes him three knots, and I should—" he did the pen-click, twice, deadpan "—stop teaching during emergencies."

"And the rest?" She pulled out into traffic, eyes forward, but her hand found his on the console with the ease of standard procedure. "You had your face on. The big-decision face."

"The airline wrote back," Theo said. "They want me for a class date."

Her hand didn't move. That was the thing he'd tell Sam later — her hand didn't so much as flicker, but the whole car went as still as a hold with the EFC expired.

"And I archived it," he said, "at a red light, without pulling over. Wren. I'm not holding for that clearance anymore. I canceled the flight plan. This—" the freeway, the rain starting, the ridiculous beloved airline of two "—is the route. As filed."

Wren Hale let out a breath she had possibly been holding since October, brought his hand up, and pressed her mouth to his knuckles at sixty miles an hour in I-5 traffic, which was, she would maintain forever after, a fully stabilized maneuver.

"Cleared as filed," she said. "Fly the airplane, Marsh."

*End of Chapter Fourteen.*
