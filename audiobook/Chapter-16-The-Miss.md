# Chapter Sixteen — The Miss

*Theo*

The day of the gala gave them one last flight together first, and the flight — Theo would spend a long time afterward turning this over — tried to teach him everything he'd need by nightfall. Aviation does that. It rehearses you for things.

They spent the afternoon shuttling Okafor's out-of-town guests up from Portland, and it was on the second run, two lodge investors and a violinist in the back, that Boeing Field's pattern got interesting: a fuel truck, misreading its clearance in the winter dusk, nosed past the hold-short line as Meridian Three-Five came down final, five hundred feet and stable.

Tower saw it the same second Wren did. "Meridian Three-Five, go around. Vehicle on the runway. Fly runway heading, maintain two thousand."

And the crew of Meridian Three-Five did the thing they had briefed on every single approach of the season, the brief Theo had privately thought of as boilerplate until the moment it wasn't: Wren's hands moved — power up, pitch to the go-around attitude — while his moved with them — flaps to approach, positive rate, gear up — and the King Air climbed away from its own shadow with the unhurried competence of a maneuver that had been flown a hundred times in two imaginations before it was ever needed in the world.

"Go-around, runway heading, two thousand," Wren read back, level as a bubble. The fuel truck reversed off the pavement below them, trailing apologies on ground frequency.

"And that," Theo said, once they were downwind for another circuit, "is the whole sermon, isn't it. The one I used to give with a whiteboard."

"Give it anyway. The violinist's asleep and I'm sentimental about your whiteboard."

"The missed approach is not a failure. It's the *system working*." He ticked it off, the liturgy, one more time. "It's required — not optional, *required* — whenever you reach DA or the missed approach point without the references, whenever the references quit on you below minimums, whenever the landing can't be made normally, whenever you lose sight of the airport in a circle. And because it's required, you brief it *before* every approach like you expect to fly it — power, pitch, configuration, first altitude, first heading — because the go-around is flown with the hands you packed, not the hands you wish you'd brought. Aviate first: the airplane climbing and clean before anything else. Navigate second: published procedure if you're on your own — and the fine print matters: go missed *early*, before the MAP, and you fly the *lateral* track of the approach all the way to the MAP before turning, because the procedure's obstacle protection was surveyed from the MAP, not from wherever your nerve ran out. Climb early all you like; turn only where the chart turns. Communicate third: tell somebody — tower's instructions outrank the published miss when tower's talking, like just now, and the report is mandatory anyway. And then—" he shrugged "—you're not falling, you're flying. Full tanks of options: another approach, a hold to think, the alternate. The miss isn't the end of the approach. It's the approach *protecting you*, exactly as designed."

"The part people get wrong," Wren said, turning base, the runway clear now and lit like a promise, "isn't the procedure. It's the shame. Crews wreck airplanes salvaging approaches they'd have flown perfectly the second time, because somewhere somebody taught them a go-around was an apology." She rolled out on final, stable, two red two white. "It's not. It's the most professional sentence in aviation: *this isn't working; I'll be back in five minutes with a better setup.*"

They landed out of the second approach without a ripple. Theo wrote the fuel truck up in the squawk log, and Wren signed it, and neither of them — this was the part he'd turn over and over — neither of them heard the evening clearing its throat.

---

The Rainier Club wore its hundred and thirty years the way Okafor wore silk: structurally. Chandeliers, cedar, a string quartet warming up under portraits of dead lumber barons, and two hundred of the West Coast's better wardrobes orbiting champagne.

Theo arrived late and alone — the tuxedo had required a second opinion from Sam, who had straightened the tie one-handed and said, gruffly, *"Your mother would have taken a hundred pictures,"* which had cost them both a minute — and by the time he'd surrendered his coat, the room was full and Wren was nowhere in it.

She'd been strange since Friday. Not cold — *loaded*. He knew her instruments by now: the extra half-second before answers, the pending-folder look, a transmission drafted and held. At the hangar she'd said *adequate, plus* and almost said something else, and he'd let it go, because they had Saturday morning, and then Saturday morning had dissolved into guest shuttles and there'd been no briefing slot at all.

And then the crowd shifted near the quartet, and there she was.

The dress was the color of a night sea, and Theo Marsh — who had watched this woman de-ice a King Air at six a.m. in a parka the approximate shape of a fuel bladder, and had found *that* distracting — stood in a ballroom full of the West Coast's better wardrobes and forgot, briefly and completely, the location of his hands.

She crossed to him through the crowd with her captain's walk, took one of the glasses he'd forgotten he was holding, and said, deadpan, by way of hello: "You're gaping, Marsh. Close the cowl flaps."

"You're carrying contraband," he countered, because his powers of observation had survived even the dress: a cocktail napkin in her other hand, folded with flight-bag precision around what was unmistakably a cached supply of the little crab things from the passed trays. "The dress has no pockets, and you've established a forward operating base anyway. I've never been prouder."

"The trays only come around every eleven minutes and the toast could run long. This is fuel planning." She ate one, unrepentant, magnificent. "Destination, alternate, and forty-five minutes of canapés. Don't teach it to the students; they're not ready."

"You look—" he attempted.

"Careful."

"—*airworthy*," Theo said, with feeling, "in an unlimited category," and had the enormous satisfaction of watching Wren Hale, decorated survivor of Alaska and Cascade and every interrogation Adaeze Okafor had ever mounted, go faintly pink above the neckline of the night sea.

The quartet, which took its cues from Okafor, chose that moment to begin something slow. And because the room was watching — because the room was *always* watching, that was the job — she set down her glass and held out her hand, and said the thing she'd have said on any flight deck: "Well. We're on the schedule."

They danced. He would keep that dance a long time — the way she followed the first two bars stiffly, auditing the choreography, and then stopped auditing; the way her hand settled at his collar exactly where it had landed in a hangar in October; the ridiculous rightness of holding her in a room full of chandeliers while Okafor beamed from the head table like a woman watching her own horse win. Halfway through, Wren looked up at him with something balanced on the edge of her expression — the pending folder, opening — and said, "After the toast tonight. There's something I need to — there's a briefing I owe you. Both of us, somewhere quiet."

"Good briefing or bad briefing?"

"Necessary briefing," she said, which from her was a whole weather product, and before he could file for further clearance the song ended, and Okafor's assistant materialized to reclaim the captain for photographs, and the room swallowed her — one flash of night-sea silk between dinner jackets — and Theo went to find champagne, unreasonably happy, thinking: *after the toast.*

He would spend a long time, afterward, doing the arithmetic on that. If the photographs had run two minutes shorter. If he'd carried the glasses a different route. If the pending folder had opened on the dance floor instead of in a lamplit annex with the wrong man in it. The links of an accident chain, he'd taught a hundred students, are individually featherweight, and every one of them holds.

"The crew's other half!" An investor he half-recognized caught his elbow. "Your better half's in the library annex, I think — the Pacific Corporate fellow swept her off for shop talk. Pilots, at a party! Incorrigible."

The library annex was down a paneled corridor, door ajar, lamplight and two voices spilling out — hers, and a baritone he'd last heard wearing cologne in Santa Barbara.

Theo would ask himself, later, with the ruthlessness of a man who audited his own scan for a living, why he stopped outside the door instead of walking through it. Fixation, he'd conclude. The oldest scan error in the book. One instrument, hot and bright — *Jax Calloway, alone with her* — and the rest of the panel abandoned.

Through the gap: Wren in the lamplight, arms folded, in the dress that had ridden to the gala like a co-conspirator. Jax leaning against the reading table with a drink, all silver temples and patience.

"—chief pilot's re-sent that offer twice, Hale. He doesn't do that. West Coast fleet captain, your schedule, your base. So before you toast the family charter act out there, tell me straight—" a gesture with the glass toward the ballroom, the quartet, the whole warm world "—you and the first officer. The *couple crew*. Come on. I fly for these people; I've seen every kind of arrangement. It's a bit, isn't it? It's good business."

And Wren Hale's voice — flat, controlled, the freight-dog register she used when a thing needed to land without flaring — said the sentence Theo would hear on instruments for weeks:

"It was an act, Jax. The couple thing — it started as an act. For the client, for the contract. Griff invented it in a lobby in about four seconds, and we went along because Meridian was three months from folding. That's the truth, and you're going to keep it to yourself, because—"

*Because in about an hour I'm done pretending; because it stopped being an act in a hangar in October; because I'm telling Adaeze Okafor the whole of it Monday and she deserves to hear it from me and not from you—*

That was the rest of the sentence. Theo never heard it.

What he heard was the blood in his own ears, and Jax's low whistle, and — as he was already turning, two champagne glasses sweating in his hands, the corridor tilting like a botched entry — the follow-up, Jax pressing, delighted: "So there's nothing actually keeping you at that little operation," and Wren's voice, sharp, starting: "I didn't say—"

The quartet swallowed it. The corridor delivered him back into the ballroom, into light and laughter and a tray where he set down both glasses with terrible care, and the evening proceeded to happen to him from a distance, as if reported by ACARS.

He stood where he was supposed to stand. He smiled at what he was supposed to smile at. Adaeze Okafor rose under the chandeliers, tapped her glass, and gave the toast — *"To Meridian, and to my aviators, who taught an old romantic that the truest kind of love is the kind that briefs its exits"* — and two hundred people laughed warmly and turned to look at the couple, and Wren appeared at his side flushed and distracted from the direction of the library, her hand finding his on cue, and Theo held it and raised a glass to a room full of light with the cabin pressure dropping in his chest, thinking, in his instructor voice, the voice that never once had lied to him:

*It started as an act. For the contract. That's the truth.*

Her copy. Unamended. He'd initialed a legal pad in a snowstorm and believed the two of them had ratified something, and here was the primary source, in lamplight, briefing the competition: *an act, for the client* — and an offer, twice re-sent, and *nothing keeping her*, and the withheld transmission he'd felt loitering behind her eyes since Friday resolving now into the simplest, oldest picture in the world.

She was leaving. She'd been drafting the words all weekend. The pending folder was him.

He was wrong — catastrophically, fixation-blind wrong, one instrument mistaken for the panel — and the whole apparatus of his training stood there in a tuxedo and failed him, because there is no cross-check for a heart that has just heard its own worst weather, in her voice, corroborated.

The toast ended. The quartet resumed. Wren leaned close, under the applause, worry starting in her eyes — "Hey. You're white as a TAF. What—" — and Theo Marsh did the only honest thing left in his inventory.

He briefed his exit.

"Clause Four," he said quietly. The words came out level. Two thousand hours of teaching in actual weather will do that; the voice flies itself long after the pilot's gone missing. "Going missed."

He watched it hit — watched her whole panel light up at once, confusion, disbelief, the beginnings of a question — and then he watched *her own architecture* catch her, the clause she'd written with her own hand, no questions, not that day, not ever, and the question died on her lips because she was Wren Hale and she did not violate procedures, even the ones killing her.

"Theo—"

"You built the exit for a reason," he said. "You were right. It's a good procedure." He set his glass on the tray of a passing waiter, precise as a checklist item. "Tell Ms. Okafor I felt unwell. It'll even be true."

And Theo Marsh walked out of the Rainier Club into the December rain — not running, never running, the weather a colleague to the last — got into the blue Subaru with the mismatched fender, and drove through the streaming dark with the radio off.

Both radios. All frequencies.

He didn't go home. He drove on autopilot, and the autopilot — knowing better than the pilot, as it sometimes does — delivered him to Des Moines, to a porch light his father never turned off, and Sam Marsh opened the door at half past midnight to find his son standing in the rain in a tuxedo with his tie in his fist.

"What happened?"

"Clause Four," Theo said.

Sam looked at him for a long moment — at the rain, the tie, the face doing its mother's trick of flying level through weather it had no business in. "I don't know what the hell a Clause Four is," he said at last, stepping back from the door, "but I know a diversion when one lands in my driveway. Get in here. I'll make the bad coffee." And he asked nothing else — not that night, and Theo would understand later that his father, forty years married and eighteen months rebuilt, had simply recognized the procedure: when an airplane arrives silent, you don't demand the story on the ramp.

You keep the lights on, and you get them warm.

Somewhere behind him, in a ballroom under chandeliers, the woman he loved stood perfectly still amid two hundred people with a clause in her chest like a knife she'd sharpened herself — required to fly the lateral track, forbidden to turn toward him before the MAP, holding, with no EFC, in the oldest procedure either of them knew:

when the communications fail, and the sky goes quiet, *continue as assigned. Continue as expected. Continue as filed.*

The rain came down on Seattle. Neither of them slept.

And nobody had briefed the miss.

*End of Chapter Sixteen.*
