# Chapter Ten — Climb Gradient

*Theo*

His father beat them to the hangar.

Theo saw it from the parking lot: the side door propped, the lights on, and Sam Marsh sitting in a folding chair beside the Bonanza with a thermos and the aircraft logbooks open on a card table, like a man visiting an old friend in the hospital who had recently been discharged. The Bonanza gleamed. Manny's annual had left her better than the factory had; the V-tail threw the hangar lights around like gossip.

"You're early," Theo said.

"I'm retired. I'm early to everything; it's the entire benefit package." Sam's eyes went past him to Wren, crossing the hangar in her flight jacket with her thermos, here at six-forty for the Hailey reposition, and Theo watched his father sit up the way he used to sit up for check rides. "And this is the captain."

"Wren Hale." She shook his hand — the left one, offered smoothly, no ceremony made of it — and then did the thing that won the morning outright: she looked past him at the airplane. "V35B. Tip tanks. Is that the IO-550 conversion?"

"It is," Sam said, in the voice of a man discovering religion in his own hangar. "You know Bonanzas."

"I know a man in Ketchikan who swore his would outclimb the company Chieftain, and one day in a fit of honesty we raced. Tell me about the annual."

That was it. That was all it took. For twenty minutes Theo stood functionally orphaned while his father walked Wren Hale around the Bonanza — compressions, the new attitude indicator, the pitot-static cert stickers, the narrative arc of the 1998 pre-buy, which she received like scripture — and it ended, inevitably, at the card table, where Sam flipped a manila folder open with his good hand, trying and failing to be casual.

Inside: a physician's checklist, signed. A course completion certificate, printed, dated Tuesday.

"BasicMed," Sam said. "Exam's done — regular state-licensed doc, went through the whole FAA checklist with me, signed me off. Online medical course, done, certificate in the logbook. Which makes me—" his voice did something at the edges "—legal, Theo. PIC. Private flying, the Bonanza's six seats and her weight are under the limits, keep it under eighteen thousand and two-fifty knots, day, night, IFR if I'm equipped and current. Which I am not. Current." He looked up at his son. "Yet."

Theo had briefed himself for this moment for eighteen months and discovered the briefing did not survive contact. He was still finding his voice when Wren said, matter-of-fact, tapping the certificate:

"Flight review's next, then. And an IPC eventually, if you want the rating back in play — that's an instructor sign-off, tasks from the ACS, no examiner needed." She glanced at Theo, then back at Sam, and something passed between the two of them, some treaty concluded entirely over his head. "Your son's a CFII with a remarks column instead of a personality. I imagine something can be arranged."

"Captain Hale," Sam said solemnly, "I like you a great deal, and I am begging you to never stop doing whatever this is to my son, who has not once looked away from you since you walked in."

"*Dad.*"

"Tuesday breakfast," Sam said, unrepentant, closing the folder like a man closing a deal. "Friday Harbor. It's coming. Both of you fly good today."

---

The reposition to Hailey was Theo's leg, left seat — Griff had blessed the training arrangement with the enthusiasm of a man who saw a future captain as cheaper than a future job posting — and Wren ran it like what it was, an audition conducted by someone rooting for the actor.

"Hailey," she said, chart up, as they climbed east into a scrubbed-clean morning, the front through, the Cascades all glitter and no menace. "Sun Valley's airport lives at the bottom of a slot. Getting *in* is a terrain seminar. Getting *out* — today's syllabus — is the departure procedure. So, professor: taxonomy. What are the species of instrument departure, and who do they belong to?"

"Two species, one common ancestor: somebody at the FAA looked at every runway with an instrument approach and asked, *if a plane goes into cloud right here, what kills it?* Where the answer is 'nothing much' — climb straight ahead, standard climb, you're fine — they publish nothing special. Where terrain or towers crowd in, they build an **obstacle departure procedure**. The ODP. Plain-text or charted: *climb runway heading to such-and-such, then climbing right turn direct the VOR, cross at or above nine thousand,* whatever the rocks require. And the thing about ODPs—" he banked gently around a cell of leftover cumulus, because the airplane was his today and the sky was gorgeous "—is they belong to *us*. Pilots. At a non-towered field, part ninety-one, I can fly the ODP without ever telling ATC — it's pre-approved terrain insurance. Nobody clears you for it; it's yours, like a fire exit."

"And the other species."

"**SIDs** — standard instrument departures. Those belong to *ATC*. They're traffic plumbing: get flow off the runway and into the en route structure with minimum radio poetry. Charted, named, dogleg-rich. You need a clearance to fly one, you can refuse one — 'no SIDs' in the flight plan remarks, if you enjoy being difficult — and while a SID *also* keeps you off the rocks, its reason for existing is sequencing, not salvation. ODP: obstacle document. SID: traffic document. And the third cousin: the **diverse vector area** — at some radar airports the controller can just assign headings right off the runway, because the terrain study's been done for vectors below the usual minimum altitudes. That's the DVA: permission for ATC to steer you low, pre-surveyed."

"Numbers. The part everybody hand-waves."

"The contract," Theo said, "and it's the same contract everywhere, so it's worth engraving: unless the procedure says otherwise, instrument departure design assumes you cross the departure end of the runway at *thirty-five feet*, climb to *four hundred above field elevation* before any turn, and climb at *two hundred feet per nautical mile* minimum, forever, until reaching a safe altitude. Two hundred per mile is the default gradient. Where it's not enough — where the slot walls come in, like today — the procedure publishes a steeper number, and *that number is a performance requirement, not a suggestion.*"

"Hailey's."

He had the plate ready. "The ODP off runway three-one wants — for our filed route — a climb gradient of four hundred and five feet per nautical mile to well above the valley. So: the math nobody does until it's dark and snowing. Gradient is feet per *mile*; the panel gives feet per *minute*. The converter is groundspeed. Groundspeed in knots divided by sixty is miles per minute; multiply by the gradient. Us, departing at about a hundred and twenty knots groundspeed: two miles a minute, times four hundred and five — call it eight hundred and ten feet per minute, *minimum*, the whole way up the slot. The King Air does that on one engine having a bad day. But the discipline is doing the arithmetic *every time*, because the day it matters is the day you're heavy, hot, high, and the answer comes out uncomfortable. Gradient times miles-per-minute. Groundspeed, not airspeed — the wind is either your friend or your accomplice."

"And the fine print at the bottom of the plate."

"Low, close-in obstacles. The note that says trees or a berm or somebody's hangar penetrates the surface right off the departure end — too close to bother building the whole procedure around, so they just *tell* you: don't cross the end at thirty-five feet in the flare of overconfidence; see and avoid the listed lumps. It's the chart designer leaning out of the page going, *also, there's a tree.*"

Wren was quiet a moment, watching the Sawtooths assemble themselves out of the haze ahead. "Voss asks it meaner," she said at last. "He'll say: you break out of a hold into the missed at Hailey, one engine's coughing, and Departure says 'climb and maintain, fly heading three-one-zero.' What keeps you alive, the vector or the chart?"

"The chart until I *know* the vector's inside a DVA or above the minimum vectoring altitude. Terrain doesn't care whose idea the heading was. Ninety-one point three lives in the left seat—" he tipped his head at where she was sitting, right seat, today "—wherever the left seat happens to be parked."

"Adequate," Wren said. "Plus." And she let the *plus* sit there, unexplained, all the way through the descent into the slot, and Theo flew the valley with his ears going faintly pink and eight hundred and ten feet per minute of margin in his pocket.

---

They came home heavy with client: Okafor, Chidinma, a lodge attorney with a laptop, and half a seat of contracts. And Seattle, in the way of great cities at four p.m., was ready for them with a queue.

"Meridian Three-Five, cleared to Boeing Field via the RAINYR Four arrival, *descend via*."

Theo — pilot monitoring now, Wren's leg home — read it back and then, for the cabin speaker and the syllabus both, unpacked it. "Ms. Okafor, you just heard the two most efficient words in arrivals. A STAR — standard terminal arrival — is the SID's mirror twin: a published funnel from the en route structure down toward the airport, drawn on a chart with crossing altitudes and speeds at the fixes, like sheet music. Old style, the controller sings every note himself: descend and maintain this, cross that at ten thousand, slow to two-fifty. Exhausting for everyone. But *descend via* hands us the sheet music: fly the lateral path *and* honor every published altitude and speed window yourself. One clearance, twenty instructions."

"And the trap," Wren said, spinning the altitude selector down to the bottom altitude of the procedure, "is the fine print between 'descend via' and everything else. If they'd said *descend via except maintain one-two-thousand*, the exception wins — we honor the chart until twelve and stop. If they say a hard *descend and maintain*, the STAR's altitudes are dead; the controller took the music back. Crews bust altitudes in both directions by mixing the dialects. The readback is where you prove which language you're in."

The RAINYR brought them down over the Sound in steps — cross ANVIL at and above one-six-thousand, MOSSY between twelve and ten, the speed windows tightening like the evening — and Wren flew the profile with the VNAV doing the labor and her scan auditing every note, and Rainier went pink off the left wing, and in the back Adaeze Okafor could be heard telling her attorney that *descend via* was how she intended to run her estate: one elegant instruction, everyone trusted to fly their own altitudes.

Short final, gear down, Theo ran the before-landing flow and made the last teaching point mostly to himself, though the intercom carried it: "Mandatory reports still ride under all of it. Leaving an altitude. Missed approach. ETA change if we're non-radar. Anything that breaks — nav, comm, the five-hundred-a-minute climb we can't make. The fancy procedures don't repeal the narration."

"Narration is free," Wren said, flaring.

"Assumptions are not," said Theo, and the mains touched, and somewhere in the exchange the phrase had stopped being hers or his and become, like a joint bank account, simply theirs.

---

Sam texted while they were putting the airplane to bed: a photo of the BasicMed certificate taped inside the Bonanza's logbook cover, and beneath it, *FLIGHT REVIEW, INSTRUCTOR TBD. KNOW ANYBODY?*

Theo showed her. Wren studied it in the hangar light for a long moment, and when she looked up there was something in her face he'd only seen pieces of before — the shoebox thing, the Ketchikan thing, unarmored all the way down.

"Descend via," she said quietly.

"Say again?"

"Your father. Eighteen months ago somebody read him the worst clearance of his life, one hard instruction at a time — can't drive, can't fly, might not walk right. And this week the system handed him back the sheet music and said: *your altitudes are yours again. Fly your own profile down; we trust you.*" She handed back the phone, and her fingers stayed on it, and on his, one beat past the transfer of control. "That's the whole point of the fancy procedures, Marsh. Not efficiency. *Dignity.* Getting to fly your own descent." A breath. "Debrief. Departures and arrivals. Short form — the light's going."

He gave it with her hand still warm in his memory: "ODPs are the pilot's fire exit — obstacle documents, flyable without clearance from non-towered fields; SIDs are ATC's plumbing — traffic documents, clearance required, refusable. DVA: pre-surveyed permission to vector low. The default contract: end of runway at thirty-five feet, four hundred up before turning, two hundred feet per nautical mile — and published steeper gradients are performance *requirements*: gradient times groundspeed-in-miles-per-minute equals the feet per minute you owe the rocks. Low close-in obstacles: the designer leaning out of the page saying *also, there's a tree.* STARs are the mirror: descend via means fly the lateral *and* the published altitudes and speeds; *except maintain* rewrites the bottom line; hard altitudes take the music back — and the readback proves which dialect you're in. Mandatory reports survive underneath everything." He paused. "And BasicMed will get a man back to Friday Harbor: private flying, under eighteen thousand, under two-fifty, doctor's checklist plus the course, and a son who apparently owes somebody a flight review."

"Somebody," Wren agreed. Her hand found the hangar switch; the lights went down in sequence, familiar now, their own private sunset. "For the record — when he flies that breakfast run, I already called the window seat. I filed months in advance."

"I remember," Theo said. "Cleared as filed."

They walked out into the dusk not-holding hands with the practiced discipline of two professionals who had, without ever once saying so, both begun flying the arrival.

*End of Chapter Ten.*
