# Chapter Nineteen — Squawk Sheet

*Wren*

The airplane came home on a Tuesday, ferried back from Pasco by Wren and Manny under a special flight permit with the weather scrubbed blue behind the departed front, and the week that followed was the unglamorous, load-bearing part of aviation that no movie has ever filmed: paperwork.

Wren loved it. She had stopped apologizing for this years ago.

"Walk the students through it," she said to Theo, in the maintenance hangar Thursday, where N135MA stood open-cowled and patient with Manny elbow-deep in her electrical bay. "Voss will ask you the postflight side of the world someday, and everybody blows it, because everybody thinks the flight ends at the chocks."

"The flight ends," Theo said, "when the ink dries. Okay." He picked up the squawk log — her longhand from the Pasco ramp, wind-wrinkled, already becoming company legend — and taught to it. "It starts with the walkaround nobody does: the *postflight* inspection. After every flight, and doubly after an event — look at the airplane. Ice damage, popped static wicks, oil where oil shouldn't be, the brakes you rode, the strut that took your landing. The airplane just told you everything about the flight; the postflight is reading its statement. Then: *documentation*. Every discrepancy, in writing, specifically — not 'radio broke' but what failed, when, what it did, what we did. Ninety-one four-oh-five puts teeth in it: the operator ensures defects get entered in the record and repaired, and *known* defects between inspections don't ride along unresolved — they get fixed or they go through the MEL-slash-two-thirteen door with a placard, like our old friend Nav Two. The squawk you don't write up isn't a kindness to the schedule. It's a trap you set for the next crew."

"And then the part pilots pretend is Manny's problem."

"Return to service. Manny fixes the contactor, and the fix isn't real until *his* ink dries — the maintenance record entry: what was done, when, by whom, signature, certificate number. Big repairs need more paper. And the records themselves — ninety-one four-seventeen — the owner keeps them: maintenance, inspections, ADs, the airplane's whole medical history, retained and producible. When you buy an airplane you're buying its logbooks; the aluminum comes free." He flipped the log shut. "The philosophical point, since you're grading: the system runs on *narration*, ground edition. Everything we preach in the air — say what failed, say what you did, make the record answer before anyone has to ask — the maintenance world invented first."

"One more piece of paper," Wren said, and slid two printed forms across the workbench. "Filed mine this morning."

Theo looked. Grinned slowly. "NASA forms."

"Aviation Safety Reporting System. The two of us, narrating the Pasco event to the one federal database that exists purely to learn and not to punish. Everything goes in anonymized — the contactor, the cascade, the comm strategy, what worked, what we'd change. Some poor crew next winter hits a bus-tie fault over mountains, and the safety newsletter they half-remember reading might be *us*." She tapped the deadline box. "Ten days from the event is the good-citizenship window — file promptly. And yes, there's the famous side effect — a timely ASRS filing can waive certain penalties if the FAA ever came asking, sincerity and non-deliberateness assumed — but that's not why crews like ours file. We file because the system that kept us findable over the Cascades is made out of other people's honest paperwork. Rent's due."

From inside the electrical bay, muffled, Manny's voice: "Found your arc tracks. Twenty-two years old and it picked *that* morning. You want the dead contactor for a trophy?"

Wren and Theo looked at each other.

"Mount it," they said, together, and Manny's laugh rang around the hangar like a prop coming into pitch.

---

The contactor got its trophy ceremony Friday afternoon, because Priya decided the company needed one and Priya's decisions had the force of NOTAMs.

Manny had mounted the scorched little villain on a walnut plaque from the hobby store, with a brass plate engraved SQUAWK OF THE YEAR — N135MA — "IT PICKED THAT MORNING," and the whole population of Meridian Air Charter gathered in the hangar under the wing to unveil it: Griff, misty before anyone had said a word; Priya with grocery-store cake; Manny's kid, released from homework for the occasion; Voss, who had "happened to be on the field" in the manner of a man who had driven forty minutes to happen to be there; and Sam, who had demanded attendance rights on the grounds that half the crew was his kid and the other half had a standing ride reservation in his airplane.

Griff gave a speech. It was terrible and everyone loved it — it began with the fuel vendor putting them on cash-on-delivery in October and ended, by a route nobody could reconstruct, with Ellen's insurance money and the phrase "the safest place it's ever been," at which point he had to stop and study the ceiling for a while, and Wren, who had said those words to him in a doorway in the fall, found the ceiling extremely interesting herself.

Then Manny hung the plaque on the tool crib wall, between the airworthiness posters, and Voss clicked his pen twice at it, and Priya cut the cake with a fuel-sampler she'd washed for the purpose, and Theo — standing at the edge of it with cake on a paper plate and his arm warm against Wren's shoulder in front of everybody, the copies all updated now, nothing left to hide from anyone — leaned down and said, quietly, just for the crew: "For the record, Captain. Best outfit I never planned to end up at."

"Cleared as filed," Wren said, with her mouth full of grocery cake, and it was somehow the most romantic thing either of them had managed all week.

---

The summons to tea arrived Friday evening, on cream cardstock, by courier, because Adaeze Okafor did not text.

She received them in the Fairmont suite with the pot already steaming, and she let Wren get exactly one sentence into the season-end gratitude speech before raising one hand, pouring three cups, and saying, serenely:

"I knew, of course. From the first morning. Before the first morning was over."

The suite went very quiet.

"My dears." Okafor settled back, cup and saucer balanced like avionics. "I have spent fifty years reading rooms in which everyone was performing. I walked into that little briefing room and found two strangers pointing at the same chart — strangers, plainly; you held yourselves like people who had known each other for forty minutes, because you had — and I watched Marcus panic and invent a romance in real time with the fluency of a man who has never once successfully lied to his own scheduler." She sipped. "It was the worst cover story I have ever been told. I have been told cover stories by *governments*."

"Then why—" Wren started.

"Why did I sign? Why did I demand the couple crew, book the season, aim my photographer, reserve the tables, arrange—" a queenly gesture encompassing, apparently, all of it, the inns and the galas and the reservation under *Marsh, party of two* "—the weather, where I could?" Okafor set down the cup. "Because, Captain, I did not buy the story. I bought the *potential*. I watched a woman who checked every rivet of her airplane look at a man like he was the one system she had no checklist for. I watched him brief his exits from everything except her. I am seventy-one years old, I fly a great deal, I am very rich, and there are only so many entertainments left that money cannot buy. Watching two magnificently competent people take an entire winter to discover what a hotelier saw in four seconds—" the smile went wide and unrepentant "—was the finest one I have purchased in years. The flights were merely also excellent."

"You let us lie to you for a season," Theo said, wondering.

"I let you *think* you were lying to me, dear, which is a different transaction entirely — I was never deceived, so no harm ever reached me; the only people your performance fooled were the performers." She refilled his cup. "And while we are settling accounts: you may be interested to know that Pacific Corporate Air no longer flies for the Ellison group, the Marchetti family, or my lodge consortium. It seems a certain silver-templed captain was heard at *my* gala, in *my* club, retailing a private matter about *my* crew as cocktail entertainment. I made no decisions before tea, naturally. After tea I made several, and then I made some telephone calls, and this coast's aviation market is a small pond with a long memory." For one instant the warm brown eyes showed the entire executive underneath, and both pilots made a silent note never, ever, to be on the other side of that ledger. "Nobody quotes my aviators out of context twice. Now — where was I." "And when the captain sat where you are sitting and told me the truth unprompted, with her company's whole contract on the table — that, children, is when I signed the *next* three years. Which my assistant will send to Marcus on Monday, and which specifies — in calligraphy, if I can manage it — *the current crew*." She raised her teacup. "To Meridian. To honest instruments. And to my aviators — who are, at last, exactly what it said on the invitation."

---

The real first date happened two weeks later, and Theo planned it the way Wren had once described to a client in a story she'd invented at eleven thousand feet: precisely, ridiculously, and with an alternate.

He did it in the Bonanza — Sam's V-tail, gleaming from its annual, with Sam's blessing bordering on shoving ("take the good headsets; if you propose over my airplane's intercom I want it logged") — and he did it by the book of her own myth: *somewhere neither of us had been. She picks the destination. He builds the whole thing on the kitchen table — route, weather, fuel. And an alternate. A whole second evening, in case the first one didn't work out.*

Wren picked a grass strip on a peninsula neither of them had landed at, with a farm cafe famous for Sunday dinners at long tables. Theo planned it on her kitchen table — she drank coffee and watched him work with the specific joy of a woman being courted in her own native language — and he briefed her the whole plan, including, with a straight face, the alternate: a hangar he'd borrowed at a strip twenty minutes short of the destination, in case of weather, which contained, he would say only, "a fallback of adequate quality."

The Sound was silver, the flight was velvet, and the peninsula was rain. Of course it was rain — hard, sudden, socked-in coastal rain, the strip's windsock vanishing behind a marching gray wall while they were still fifteen miles out, and Wren, laughing — eighth documented occurrence, or possibly she'd lost count forever somewhere over the water — keyed the intercom: "Say intentions."

"Meridian's captain once told a client," Theo said, banking the Bonanza gently away from the weather, "*with my luck, we always need the alternate.*" He rolled out on the new heading, smug as a forecast that verified. "Diverting. As planned."

The alternate was the borrowed hangar with its doors open to the rain, a table set for two inside the span of a parked Cub's wings, string lights, a cooler, and Sam's ancient camp stove, on which Theo Marsh — narrating fuel-air mixtures like an instructor the whole time — cooked her dinner while the storm drummed the roof and the runway lights of the little strip burned through the silver dark beyond the doors.

"You planned the date," Wren said slowly, "assuming the weather would kill it. The *alternate* was the date."

"The forecast was garbage from Tuesday." He handed her a plate. "Anybody can plan the evening they want, Hale. You taught me to plan the one we'd actually get."

She looked at him across the table under the Cub's wing — this man who filed alternates for her stories and made them true, who had walked into her briefing room out of the rain and never once flinched at the full Wren Hale of it all — and she felt the last checklist item of a very long approach click complete.

"Take me home, Marsh," she said.

---

Her apartment was small and warm and, for the first time in the three years she'd lived there, not remotely lonely. Rain on the windows; his jacket over her chair like it had always hung there; the evening going unhurried and certain, a stabilized approach in zero wind.

But there was one thing first. She'd briefed it to herself for a week.

"Stay there," Wren said, and went to the shelf, and came back with the shoebox.

She set it on the coffee table between them and took the lid off, and let Theo Marsh look at the one archive no soul on earth had seen: the expired charts. The first-solo sectional, creased where her instructor folded it. The Alaska en route that still smelled, faintly, impossibly, of fish. Ketchikan, two hundred and a half, the plate soft as cloth from handling. Every first, every survival, every landing that had built her, kept in the dark and never once explained.

"Inventory," she said, quietly. "Complete disclosure. You once showed me a remarks column and I fell in love over it, so — this is mine. This is where I keep the things that mattered too much to say out loud." She reached into her jacket — the left pocket, the zipper — and took out a folded chart, and laid it on top of the stack. The low en route for the Pasco diversion, wind-wrinkled from the ramp, one course line drawn on it in her steady hand, southeast, away from the wall of weather, toward the flat land and the lit runway. "Tonight's addition. Filed and closed."

Theo looked at the box for a long time. When he looked up, his eyes were bright and blown, needles full-scale.

"Wren Hale," he said, "keeper of expired charts, planner of alternates, writer of clauses — I am going to love you for the rest of my life."

"Say it again," she said, unsteadily. "Slower. I want to remember exactly how it sounded."

"For the rest of my life," said Theo, and reached over, and turned off the lamp — and the rain kept the record of the rest of it, on the windows of a small warm apartment above a silver city, where two people with no audience at all, and no act left to perform, and nothing left to prove to anyone, held on to each other in the beautiful dark — and were, at last, simply home.

*End of Chapter Nineteen.*
