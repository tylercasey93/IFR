# Chapter Fifteen — Circling

*Wren*

The Yakima trip was supposed to be simple: run two of Okafor's lodge attorneys over the mountains for a signing, wait out the paperwork, bring them home after dark. December kept the first half of the bargain.

By the time they loaded up for the return, night had come down cold and moonless over the valley, and the wind had swung hard out of the northwest — fifteen gusting twenty-four, straight down runway two-seven — while the only approach in service, thanks to a NOTAM'd glideslope and a maintenance crew with a backhoe, was built to runway nine.

"So there it is," Theo said, chart up, as they briefed at the FBO counter. "The approach points east. The wind demands west. Which makes tonight—"

"A circling approach," Wren said. "At night. In the mountains." She let the words sit there, then pulled out her personal minimums card and laid it between them, because this was going to be a teaching brief in both directions. "First the machinery, then the philosophy. Categories. Go."

"Approach categories run on speed — born from one-point-three times stall in landing configuration, but *operationally* the category is set by the speed you actually fly the maneuver. The King Air's a Category B airplane by the book, but circling we'll maneuver at a hundred and twenty knots or better for the gust margin — which makes us Category C *tonight*, and we take Category C circling minimums, not B. The category isn't a birth certificate. It's a speedometer reading."

"Protected area."

"The circling MDA guarantees obstacle clearance only inside the protected arcs drawn around the runway ends — and the size of those arcs depends on which era of chart you're holding. Old-style TERPS: fixed radii — one-point-seven miles for Category C, tight. New-style — the charts wearing the little negative-C icon in the minimums box — expanded radii that grow with altitude, because somebody at the FAA finally acknowledged that true airspeed and wind exist. Tonight's chart has the icon: bigger playpen, higher MDA. Either way, the rule is absolute: wander outside the arcs at circling minimums at night in terrain, and you are test-flying an altitude nobody surveyed for you."

"The three commandments of the circle itself."

"One: stay at or above the circling MDA until the airplane's in a position for a *normal* descent to the landing runway — no ducking early, because the protected area's vertical guarantee lives at the MDA, not below it. Two: keep the runway environment in sight the whole way around — and the reg means it: lose visual contact with the airport while circling, and the approach is over, immediately. Three: fly the geometry you briefed — downwind, base, no improvisation, bank angles sane, because a skidding turn to final at circling altitude at night is the setup for the stall-spin that ends most circling stories the NTSB tells."

"And if it all goes wrong at the worst spot — say, midfield downwind, and the runway lights vanish behind a snow shower."

"The circling missed approach," Theo said, "and it's the best-designed counterintuitive maneuver in the book: *turn toward the landing runway*. Make a climbing turn over the top of the airport, and keep turning until you're established on the published missed approach course. It feels wrong — the procedure was built off the *other* runway's final — but the geometry works from anywhere in the circle *if* you climb toward the middle. The airport is the one piece of real estate you know is flat and surveyed. Overfly your safety."

"All correct." Wren tapped the minimums card. "Now the philosophy. My card says: no circling at night below a thousand-and-three, and none at all — night, mountains — below eight hundred above the airport, regardless of what the chart offers me. Tonight's circling MDA is about six hundred above the field. The *chart* will sell me that number. I'm not buying." She slid the card across. "Ceiling out there right now is twenty-eight hundred broken, ten miles under it, and the whole valley's VMC beneath. So here's the actual plan: we fly the approach for the procedure's protection, break out high, and by the time we're circling we're doing it with two thousand feet of margin in good visual conditions — a circle I'd fly on my worst day. If the ceiling had been at circling minimums tonight? We'd be driving these attorneys to a hotel, and Ms. Okafor would be getting the plain-sentences phone call. Some maneuvers you don't fly at the edge of the envelope at night. Circling is first on that list."

Theo looked at the card, then at her, with the expression she'd stopped pretending not to collect. "For the record," he said, "the day you brief a student on the difference between *what the chart permits* and *what the pilot accepts*, use tonight. It's the whole ballgame in one wind shift."

---

The flight ran exactly as briefed, which is the highest praise aviation offers.

Approach to runway nine, breaking out at twenty-six hundred with the valley spread black and gold beneath; the circle flown wide and unhurried at a hundred twenty knots, downwind for two-seven with the runway lights held steady off the left wing the entire way; and one piece of night-flying doctrine delivered on short final, because the landing itself had teaching in it:

"Black-hole conditions," Wren narrated, stable at five hundred feet, the runway floating ahead in a darkness with no town behind it. "Unlit terrain short of the threshold — the classic illusion. With no ground lights under the approach path, the eye swears you're high, and pilots answer the lie by descending into the dark. Counter-measures: fly the PAPI — two red, two white, non-negotiable — and cross-check the descent rate against the groundspeed math. At a towered field the reg makes the PAPI mandatory anyway: at or above the visual glidepath until landing assurance. Out here it's not tower that makes it law. It's physics."

The mains touched in the touchdown zone; the attorneys, oblivious, applauded the wind for dying down; and Theo, running the after-landing flow, said quietly: "Nice circle, Captain."

"Nice *margin*," Wren corrected. "The circle was ordinary. The margin was the airmanship."

---

The complication was waiting in her inbox when they got home, wearing a subject line in corporate cheerful: **RAINIER CLUB — FINAL GUEST LIST & PROGRAM, Saturday.**

She read it at her kitchen table, coffee going cold at the standard rate, charts of the season stacked at her elbow. Okafor's people had built an evening: cocktails, dinner, a program of toasts — *including remarks by Ms. Okafor honoring Meridian Air Charter and its crew* — and four attached pages of names. Lodge investors. Hotel people. Half the moneyed West Coast.

And there, page three, under a fractional-ownership company's table of eight: **Calloway, J. — Capt., Pacific Corporate Air.**

Of course. Jax's outfit flew for two of the investor groups; of course the gala that celebrated her fake-turned-real relationship would also contain the one man on the coast actively recruiting her, whose chief pilot's offer email — generous, patient, twice re-sent — was still sitting unanswered in the folder where she kept things requiring a decision she'd already made but hadn't yet mailed.

She'd tell him no at the gala, politely, in person, and be done with it.

That wasn't the thing that kept her at the table after midnight, though. The thing that kept her there was the program itself — *remarks honoring Meridian and its crew* — and the small, relentless voice that had been getting louder for weeks, the one that sounded like her own instruments:

*She's going to stand up in front of two hundred people and toast a love story, and the first chapter of it is a lie you told her for money.*

The renewal was signed. Meridian was saved. The relationship was — God, the relationship was *real*, realer than anything she'd built since Alaska. But the foundation stone had a crack in it, and Wren Hale had spent her whole adult life learning exactly one lesson in every language the sky spoke: cracks don't heal under load. They propagate.

She called Priya at seven the next morning, because Priya was the only soul at Meridian who knew every layer — act, amendments, all of it.

"We're telling her," Wren said. "Okafor. The truth. The whole origin — Griff's panic, the fake couple, all of it. After the gala, before the season ends. Theo and I, together — we already agreed we'd update everyone's copy after Saturday; hers gets the unabridged edition."

Priya was quiet for a second. "She might laugh. She might also feel like a fool in front of her whole world, three days after toasting you. Wren — the contract—"

"The contract was signed by a woman who deserved the truth and got a performance." Wren watched the rain come in over the Sound, gray on gray, ceiling coming down. "I've flown on falsified paperwork exactly once in my life, Priya. Cascade. I told myself the logbooks were the captain's problem. It cost me two years and half my name. I'm not doing the airborne version of that to a seventy-year-old woman who calls us her aviators." A breath. "The whole *couple* thing started as an act — as a lie we told a client to save a contract. And what it turned into doesn't clean the origin. Only the truth cleans the origin. She hears it from me."

"From *us*," Priya said. "Fine. Yes. After the gala. Have you told Theo the plan?"

"Saturday morning. I want the words right first." She almost laughed. "I've briefed missed approaches with more composure. How do you tell the most romantic woman alive that her favorite love story started as a fuel-state problem?"

"You'll find the words," Priya said. "You always do. You're just the only person I know who has to file them first."

---

Thursday after the schedule closed, Priya drove them both to a consignment shop in Georgetown that she described as "where good dresses go to get a second career," on the grounds that Wren's one dress had now been photographed twice and Adaeze Okafor's gala operated, sartorially speaking, in RVSM airspace.

Wren tried on four dresses with the enthusiasm of a woman clearing customs. The fifth was the color of a night sea, and when she came out of the fitting room Priya put down her phone, which had never once happened in the history of their friendship.

"Okay," Priya said. "That one. That one is a TFR. Nobody's getting through that without authorization."

Wren looked at herself in the three-way mirror — turned once, checking the lines the way she checked control surfaces — and the strange thing, the thing she'd remember, was that the woman in the glass looked *lit from somewhere*, and it wasn't the shop's fluorescents doing it.

"He's going to make a scene," she said, to the mirror, trying the thought on with the dress. "You know that. He'll see it and he'll say something ridiculous in front of two hundred people, and I'll have to pretend it's part of the act, and it isn't." She smoothed the fabric down, one pass, spine straight. "None of it's the act, Priya. I keep waiting to mind."

"And?"

"And I don't mind." Wren met her own eyes in the mirror and said it plainly, the way she'd have read back a clearance, because some things you should hear yourself transmit at least once: "I'm happy. Certifiably. Log it — Thursday, eighteen-forty local, W. Hale reported happy, unable to determine cause."

"Oh, we can determine the cause," said Priya, already back on her phone, photographing the dress tag for the budget. "The cause is six-one and files continuation training plans for fun. You're buying the dress."

"It's not in the—"

"You're *buying the dress*, Wren. Saturday's a big night. Toast, confession weekend after, whole life starting — " Priya waved a hand at the general future, breezy as a terminal forecast, unaware, as forecasts are, of what was actually inbound. "Wear the good armor."

Wren bought the dress. It rode home in the crew car's garment hook next to her flight jacket, silk and Cordura, the two uniforms of the whole strange season, and she caught herself smiling at them together at a red light like an absolute civilian.

*Happy,* she thought. *Filed and logged.*

She would think about that log entry many times in the week that followed, the way you think about the last routine radio call before the frequency goes quiet.

---

The debrief that closed the chapter of the week happened at the hangar Friday evening, the two of them under the wing in the yellow light with the gala one sleep away — Theo in a garment bag's custody dispute with a tuxedo, Wren with her dress hanging in the crew car like a co-conspirator.

"Circling," she said. "Final recital before we go pretend to be fancy."

"Category is a speedometer, not a birth certificate — fly faster, buy the higher mins. Protected area: fixed arcs on old charts, expanding arcs behind the negative-C icon — outside them nobody surveyed your altitude. The commandments: MDA until a normal descent, runway in sight always — lose it and the approach is over — and briefed geometry, no skidding improvisations at night. Circling miss: climbing turn *toward* the landing runway, over the top of the airport, join the published miss — overfly your safety. Black hole: the dark lies and says you're high; the PAPI and the arithmetic tell the truth; at towered fields the glidepath's the law and everywhere else it's physics. And above all of it—" he zipped the garment bag with ceremony "—the chart offers; the pilot accepts or declines. Margin is the airmanship. The circle was ordinary."

"Adequate," Wren said, softly. "Plus."

She almost told him then. The plan, the confession, the crack in the foundation stone and the words she'd been drafting since midnight Tuesday — it was all right there, one breath from the transmit switch. But the hangar was warm, and he was laughing at his own tuxedo, and Saturday morning had a briefing slot with their names on it, and Wren Hale — planner of alternates, filer of everything — decided the words could hold one more night in the pending folder.

It was, she would think later, running the accident sequence the way she ran every one — link by link, honestly, at her kitchen table — the only approach of the whole long season she'd ever flown unbriefed.

*End of Chapter Fifteen.*
