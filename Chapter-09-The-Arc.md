# Chapter Nine — The Arc

*Wren*

The morning after the kiss that didn't count, Wren got to the briefing room first, which took doing, because Theo had a documented head start on every morning of his life. She'd beaten him there by arriving at five-forty, and she'd arrived at five-forty because she'd been awake since four, and she'd been awake since four because her brain had spent the night replaying a three-second event in the hangar with the thoroughness of an accident board.

*Post-event physiological discharge.* She'd actually said that. Out loud. To a man whose mouth she'd just—

Priya was at the dispatch counter when she came through at five-forty, because Priya's radar never slept, and she watched Wren badge in with the open interest of a controller acquiring an unidentified target.

"You're here before *him*."

"I'm here before everyone. It's a leadership behavior."

"You have never once beaten him in. He gets here at quarter to six like a cuckoo clock; you get here at six-ten specifically so you can find something he's already done and improve it. It's your whole morning ritual; I've timed it." Priya leaned on the counter. "So either the schedule changed, or something *happened*."

"Weather briefing," Wren said. "Big interference NOTAM. Complex airspace day."

"Uh-huh. And the fact that you just put your coffee in the refrigerator?"

Wren retrieved her coffee from the refrigerator with unbroken composure. "It's a warm morning."

"It's *November*—"

"Complex airspace day, Priya," said Wren, and walked to the briefing room with her spine at full extension and her ears on fire, pursued by the specific silence of a dispatcher deciding which page of her mental file to open.

"Morning, Captain." Theo came through the door with two coffees and a manila expression, and set hers down at precisely the usual distance, and opened the weather at precisely the usual angle, and proceeded to be so flawlessly, surgically normal that it constituted its own form of commentary. Fine. Good. Two could fly that pattern.

"NOTAMs," she said. "There's a fun one."

"I saw. Read it to the class."

She pulled it up. "Flight advisory: GPS interference testing. Mountain Home complex, today, fourteen hundred to twenty-two hundred Zulu — GPS signal *may be unreliable or unavailable* within a two-hundred-fifty nautical mile radius at flight level four-zero-zero, tapering down to fifty miles at four thousand." She looked over the top of the screen. "Our route to Boise sits inside the cone most of the way. So today, the entire modern navigation stack — both GPS units, the pretty magenta line, the RAIM predictions we ran, all of it — is officially on notice. The military is going to yell into the spectrum, and our receivers may or may not hear the satellites over the yelling."

"And Ms. Okafor's hotel closing is at two."

"And Ms. Okafor's hotel closing is at two. So brief me the fallback, Marsh, because today we go to Boise the way God and Victor airways intended: green needles standing by."

He grinned — the first unmanufactured expression of the morning, and something in the room eased half a turn. "Ground-based nav. The catechism. VORs: tune, *identify* — the Morse code, every time, because a VOR undergoing maintenance can radiate a signal with no identifier, and a signal without an ident is legally and morally unusable. Then orient: the radial is *from* the station, always — you can be southwest of the station flying northeast on the two-two-five radial, and half of instrument confusion is people forgetting radials point outward. To find yourself: center the needle with a FROM flag and read the bottom of the dial, or center with a TO and read the top, and cross-check DME. To go somewhere: pick the course, twist it under the index, and the needle becomes a picture of geometry instead of a suggestion."

"Intercepts."

"Inbound from off-course: turn to put the head of the course needle somewhere ahead of the wingtip — a thirty-to-forty-five degree cut for normal work, steeper if you're close and the needle's pegged, shallower if you're far and it's crawling. The angle is a *decision*, not a default: how fast is the needle moving, how far to the fix, how much wind. Then bracket: needle drifts, correct twenty, hold the new heading, watch; re-center, take half of it back. Tracking is just a long conversation with the wind in ten-degree sentences."

"And the part you're saving because you love it."

"The arc," Theo said, with the reverence of a man announcing dessert. "Boise's giving us the ILS most days, but the initial segment I filed for — if the GPS goes stupid — brings us around the fifteen-DME arc off the Boise VOR to intercept the localizer. DME arcs: the elegant dinosaur. Constant distance from the station, flown with nothing but a bearing pointer and arithmetic. And since I know you know it, I'll trade you: you fly it today if the box dies, and I'll narrate for Ms. Okafor, because an arc deserves an audience."

"You've got it backwards. You fly, I narrate. You're the one building an upgrade file." She said it evenly, and watched it land — the small recalibration, the captain pushing the first officer up the ladder — and Theo looked at her for a moment with the manila expression nowhere in evidence.

"Copy," he said. "I fly."

Neither of them mentioned the hangar. The hangar rode along anyway, third crew member, jump-seating everywhere they went now.

---

The interference started, with military punctuality, eleven minutes after they leveled at one-six-thousand.

It didn't announce itself with drama. That was the insidious part, the part Wren made sure the flight deck said out loud for the record: the boxes just began to *doubt*. First a quiet annunciation on the number two — LOI, loss of integrity, the unit no longer able to promise its position was honest. Then the number one joined it, position source degrading, the moving map's confidence visibly loosening, and the autopilot still smoothly tracking a magenta line that had become, in the space of ninety seconds, a beautifully rendered guess.

"And there it is," Theo said. "Integrity flags on both. RAIM's job in one act: the receiver cross-examines its own satellites, and when it can't guarantee the answer, it *says so* — that's the entire moral difference between GPS and a liar. The box isn't wrong yet. It's honest about the possibility. But honest doubt doesn't navigate an airplane, so: green needles. Also, I've named the jammer Gary."

"You've — named the military's electronic warfare exercise."

"Gary," Theo confirmed, spinning the OBS. "Personalizing your adversary builds healthy contempt. Instructor technique. Gary thinks he's ruined our morning; Gary has never met a VOR."

"If you put 'defeated Gary' in the trip report, I'm sending it to the FAA unredacted."

"That's fair. He'd want the recognition."

They executed the fallback like they'd briefed it, because they'd briefed it. Nav One spun to the Boise VOR — ident on, the Morse spelling itself out, *dah-dah-dit-dit-dit, dah-dah-dah, dit-dit* — course set, needle alive and steady, an eighty-year-old idea coming off the bench without a word of complaint. Theo cross-checked DME against the last trustworthy GPS distance, called the delta — half a mile, sane — and Wren keyed the radio.

"Salt Lake Center, Meridian Three-Five. Be advised we're showing GPS unreliable, both receivers, consistent with the interference NOTAM. We're navigating VOR from here — no assistance required — reporting per ninety-one one-eighty-seven."

"Meridian Three-Five, roger, you're the fourth this hour. Report Boise VOR."

She unkeyed. "And that's the reg working as designed. Under IFR in controlled airspace, malfunctions of nav, approach, or comm equipment get *reported* — what failed, how much it hurts us, what we need. Center's building a picture out of everybody's bad morning: four reports means the interference is real and regional, and the fifth crew gets warned before their boxes even flinch. The additional-reports list says the same thing — loss of VOR, GPS, ILS capability, say it in the air. Narration—"

"—is free," Theo finished. "And assumptions are not. I have the airplane and the green needle. You have the microphone and the moral high ground."

From the cabin speaker, serene: "Captain, my map has stopped drawing our little airplane. Should I be composing farewells?"

"Ma'am, your map ran on the same satellites the Air Force is currently shouting over. The airplane is navigating on radio beacons your late husband would recognize, your crew trained for exactly this before coffee, and your closing is safe. Farewells would be premature and, frankly, off-brand."

"My husband," Okafor said, with relish, "proposed to me *because* I read a map upside down in the Ngong hills and refused to admit it. Carry on, aviators. I find the beacons romantic."

---

Boise Approach was working the interference like a coat-check line — "expect the full procedure, radar services limited this sector today" — and so Meridian Three-Five got the whole dance: cleared direct the VOR at eleven thousand, then the arc.

"Meridian Three-Five, cleared VOR-DME runway one-zero-right approach via the one-five DME arc, maintain eleven thousand until established."

Theo flew it, and Wren watched him fly it, and narrated for the cabin because a deal was a deal.

"Ms. Okafor, what happens next is the aviation equivalent of walking a fence line. There's a radio beacon on the field. We're going to fly a circle around it — fifteen miles out, exactly, all the way around to the northwest until we line up with the runway's approach course. No satellites, no computers. Distance readout and a compass and a pilot doing arithmetic."

"How does he hold the circle?" Chidinma this trip too, leaning into the speaker's range. "It's not painted on anything."

"Watch him and I'll call it. He's arriving on a course pointed straight at the beacon. Half a mile before fifteen — the lead, because the airplane needs room to turn — he banks ninety degrees to the arc." The King Air rolled smoothly right, DME counting 15.1, 15.0. "Now the trick called turn-ten, twist-ten: he flies straight — a chord across the circle, not the curve itself — while the beacon drifts behind his wingtip. Distance creeps out a tenth or two. Then ten degrees of turn back toward the beacon, reset the course needle ten degrees, and fly the next straight bite. A circle made of small honest corrections. If the wind shoves us wide — sixteen miles — he steepens the cut toward the station; sucked inside — fourteen — he holds off and lets the arc come back to him. Fence line the whole way."

The DME sat on 15.0 like it had been bolted there. Wind out of the southwest quartering the arc; Theo carried his correction like a waiter carrying a tray, adjusting by feel, saying almost nothing, and Wren watched his hands and had the realization she'd been holding at arm's length since the hangar arrive with the gentle finality of a needle centering.

*You are doing this exact maneuver,* the realization said. *You have been flying a fifteen-mile arc around this man for weeks. Constant distance. Careful corrections. Never inbound.*

"Lead radial," Theo said, breaking her out of it — the charted radial before the final approach course, the arc's off-ramp. "Two-niner-five set. There it comes." The needle came alive, swung; he rolled off the arc onto the inbound course, wings level on final approach geometry, eleven miles out, and reached up and clicked the ident one more time — old habit, verify before you bet on it — and shot her a glance across the pedestal. "Fence line complete. Requesting grade."

"Adequate," Wren said, and meant *magnificent*, and suspected — from the way the corner of his mouth moved — the translation was landing fine.

They broke out at two thousand over the brown Idaho fall, runway ahead, the interference and the satellites and the whole doubted modern world irrelevant behind them, and Wren keyed the cabin mic one last time: "Ma'am — beacons, needles, and arithmetic. On time for your closing."

"I never doubted," said Adaeze Okafor, who had built an empire on knowing exactly when she was being flown around at a careful constant distance, "that *someone* aboard would eventually turn inbound."

Wren did not look at Theo. Theo did not look at Wren. The King Air settled onto runway one-zero-right, honest as a needle, and rolled out long into the morning.

---

Clause Three happened over paper cups in the Boise FBO while Okafor closed on her hotel, and it ran long, because the material was rich and because neither of them was in a hurry to leave a table the other was sitting at, a fact Wren had stopped pretending not to notice.

"GPS," she prompted.

"Trust it because it doubts itself: RAIM and integrity monitoring mean the box tells you when the answer's no longer guaranteed — LOI isn't 'wrong,' it's 'no longer promising,' and the correct response is a briefed fallback, not denial. Interference NOTAMs are real ops, big footprints; brief green needles before you need them. Report the trouble — ninety-one one-eighty-seven, plus the additional-reports list: loss of nav capability gets said out loud so the system can warn the next crew."

"Ground-based."

"Tune, *identify* — no Morse, no nav; maintenance can leave a signal radiating with the ident pulled. Radials point from the station, forever. Intercepts are chosen angles — thirty to forty-five normal, adjusted for distance, needle speed, wind — and tracking is bracketing: correct, hold, halve. The arc: lead the turn by half a mile at our speeds, turn-ten twist-ten, chords around the circle, steepen toward the station when wide, hold off when tight, and the lead radial is the off-ramp. And ident *again* before final, because faith is for passengers."

"And the elegant dinosaur speech."

"The whole conventional structure is the ballast that lets the shiny stuff be trusted," Theo said. "The day the constellation gets shouted down, eighty years of radio geometry is still standing there, smelling faintly of the nineteen-fifties, holding the sky up." He crushed his cup, grinned. "Arcs forever. Fight me."

Wren turned her own cup a quarter-turn — his tell; she'd caught it off him like a cold — and heard herself say, in a voice that was not entirely the captain's: "Fifteen miles is a stable arc, Marsh. Good winds, honest DME. A person could fly it indefinitely." She looked up. "But it's not an approach. At some point you take the lead radial or you admit you were never landing."

The FBO hummed around them. Theo went very still, the way he had on the Friday Harbor ramp, a man watching a needle come alive.

"For the record," he said carefully, "I've had the inbound course set for weeks. I'm just waiting on the clearance."

"I know," said Wren Hale, and stood, and squared the paperwork, and — because some transmissions can only be sent from the doorway, on the way out, half over the shoulder — added: "Expect it."

---

The closing went through at four-fifteen, and Adaeze Okafor celebrated the way she did everything: decisively, and with staff. By seven the hotel's rooftop bar had been partially annexed, the lodge attorneys were being taught some kind of clapping game by their new owner, and a bottle of champagne had been dispatched to a corner table with a card reading, in the assistant's hand at Okafor's dictation: *For my aviators. You circled beautifully today. — A.O.*

"She means the DME arc," Theo said, examining the card.

"She absolutely does not mean the DME arc."

They drank her champagne anyway, at the corner table above the cold Boise night with the airport beacon turning green-white-green in the middle distance, and the conversation stayed professional for one entire glass, which was a company record for rooftop conditions. It was Theo who finally set his down and looked at her with the lights in his eyes and said, "So. 'Expect it.' Am I allowed to file a request for—"

"No."

"You don't know what I was—"

"You were going to request an estimate," Wren said. "A time on the further clearance. And the answer is no, because the controlling authority is currently—" she considered her own glass, and the beacon, and the ridiculous lightness in her chest that champagne did not adequately explain "—working other traffic."

"Copy," Theo said, entirely too gently, and refilled her glass instead of pushing, which was the whole problem with him, the entire structural problem: the man never once flew through her turbulence when he could ride the smooth air beside it and wait.

Across the roof, Okafor raised her own glass in their direction — to the closing, ostensibly — and called out, with the timing of a woman who missed nothing: "To *expectations*, my dears!"

"To expectations," the whole roof chorused, obediently, having no idea.

Wren drank to it. It seemed dishonest not to.

*End of Chapter Nine.*
