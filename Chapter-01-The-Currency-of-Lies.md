# Chapter One — The Currency of Lies

*Wren*

Wren Hale had a rule about first impressions: trust them exactly as far as you'd trust a fuel gauge. Useful for trend information. Never bet your life on one.

Her first impression of Theo Marsh was that he was late.

She stood at the wing of the King Air with the fuel receipt in one hand and her coffee going cold in the other, watching the October rain make a mess of Boeing Field. The overcast had been sitting at eight hundred feet since dawn, the kind of Seattle morning that made instrument flying feel less like a skill and more like a subscription service. Somewhere above that gray lid the sun was doing its job. Down here, Meridian Air Charter's entire fleet — one King Air 350, tail number November-One-Three-Five-Mike-Alpha, and a broken coffee maker — dripped quietly on the ramp.

"He's not late," said Priya, appearing under the wing with a clipboard and the self-satisfied look of a scheduler who knows things. "You're twenty minutes early. There's a difference, and the difference is your personality."

"If you're on time, you're late."

"That's not a regulation, Wren."

"It should be." Wren drained the coffee anyway. "Tell me about him again."

Priya flipped a page she clearly didn't need to flip. "Theo Marsh. Thirty-one. Commercial, multi-engine, instrument obviously. CFII — like, a *serious* CFII, the kind students fight over. Twenty-one hundred total, four hundred multi. Flew a Navajo for a freight outfit before the instructing years. Griff hired him Tuesday because our last first officer ran off to the airlines like they all do, and because Theo can start yesterday."

"Three years out of one-thirty-five flying," Wren said. "Three years teaching in Skyhawks."

"Three years teaching *instruments* in Skyhawks."

"In a simulator half the time. There's a difference," Wren said, "and the difference is a hundred and eighty knots."

She wasn't trying to be cruel. She was trying to be a captain, which some days shook out to the same thing. Meridian was small enough that a weak first officer wasn't an inconvenience; he was a hole in the only lifeboat. And Meridian's lifeboat was already riding low — Griff hadn't said the words *one bad quarter from folding*, but he'd started doing the books with the office door shut, which said them for him.

A blue Subaru with a mismatched fender pulled through the gate, parked crooked, and produced a man in a rain shell who did not run through the rain. He walked, unbothered, like weather was a colleague he'd worked with for years. Flight bag on one shoulder. Headset bag on the other. He came straight to the wing, put his hand out, and smiled like the ramp wasn't gray at all.

"Captain Hale. Theo Marsh. I'd say something charming about the weather, but it's Seattle, so — nice ceiling, if you're into ceilings."

"Eight hundred overcast," she said, shaking his hand once. "Tops reported around nine thousand. You current, Marsh?"

If the question insulted him, he didn't show it. "As of last Thursday, yeah. Want the confession anyway?"

"The confession?"

"Everybody's got one." He set his bags down under the wing, out of the drip line, which she noticed and filed away. "Mine goes like this. Three years instructing, I flew approaches every single day — from the right seat, watching students try to kill me gently. Foggles, hood, the whole circus. And about five months ago I looked at my own logbook and realized that in the previous six calendar months, *I* — my hands, my scan, me as a required pilot — had logged exactly four approaches, no hold, and I couldn't tell you the last time I'd intercepted and tracked for my own account instead of grading somebody else's."

"So you were out of currency."

"I was out of currency," he agreed, cheerfully, the way other men admitted to jaywalking. "Which is the thing nobody tells you about instructing. You can stand next to the airplane all day and never be *in* it. Sixty-one fifty-seven doesn't care what you watched. It cares what you did."

Wren felt the first, faint, unwelcome tug of approval. "And what does it want you to have done?"

He held up a hand and counted it off on his fingers, and she recognized the cadence at once — a teacher's cadence, worn smooth from use. "Within the preceding six calendar months: six instrument approaches. Holding procedures and tasks. Intercepting and tracking courses through the use of navigational electronic systems. I teach it as *sixty-six-HIT* — six months, six approaches, Holding, Intercept, Track. No hour requirement, which surprises people. You could do it all in one greasy afternoon."

"And if the six months lapse?"

"Then I get another six months to fix it — with a safety pilot or in a sim — but I can't act as PIC under IFR until it's fixed. And if I sleep through *that* window too, now it's an instrument proficiency check with an instructor or examiner, the full ride. Which is where I was headed in May, so I called in a favor, borrowed a Seminole and a safety pilot, and spent a day doing approaches at Bremerton until the tower knew my voice."

"Your safety pilot," Wren said, testing. "What did they need to be?"

"Private pilot or better, category and class for the airplane — so, multi-engine for the Seminole — a current medical, and the airplane needs dual controls. And *they're* a required crewmember while I'm under the hood, so they log the time as SIC if they want it." He tipped his head. "You're checking whether I know the difference between the answer and the reason for the answer."

"I'm checking whether you're going to be a passenger in my right seat."

"Fair," he said. "For what it's worth, Captain, I got current in May and I've stayed current since — approaches, the hold, the tracking, all logged, dates and places, because sixty-one fifty-one says if I want credit for it, it goes in the book. But." He shrugged. "Current's the floor. It's not the airplane I'm walking up to today. I haven't hand-flown a three-fifty in three years. I'll be honest about that every day until it isn't true."

And there it was — he'd said her own sermon back to her before she could preach it, which was either the best thing a new first officer had ever done or the most annoying, and she genuinely could not tell which.

"Current isn't proficient," she said anyway, because it was her line and she wanted it back.

"No, ma'am. Currency is what the FAA can measure. Proficiency is what your passengers are actually buying." He picked his bags back up. "Where do you want me?"

---

Priya caught her at the dispatch counter twenty minutes later, while Theo was off surrendering paperwork to the copier.

"So," Priya said.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've been saying it since he parked. You say it with your whole face." Wren initialed the fuel release and did not look up, which with Priya was a tactical error, because Priya read averted eyes the way Wren read weather depictions.

"He walked through the rain," Priya said. "You watched him do it. I watched *you* watch him do it, and I've worked this desk for four years, and captains don't watch new hires walk. They watch them preflight."

"I was assessing his judgment."

"Uh-huh. And?"

"His judgment appears adequate," Wren said, and Priya made a small triumphant noise, because *adequate* was the top of Wren's scale and they both knew it.

The truth — the part Wren didn't say, because saying it would have given it aerodynamic surfaces — was that she had rules about this, written the hard way, the same winter she'd written her first personal minimums card. Rule one: never fly with someone you're sleeping with. Rule two: never sleep with someone you fly with. The rules had a name and the name was Danny Ruiz, and the ink they were written in had cost her a company, a reputation, and two years of her life, and no first officer with good hands and a teacher's cadence was worth reopening the file.

"There's also this." Priya slid a printout across the counter, lowering her voice into the register she saved for actual problems. "Came in while you were on the ramp. Fuel vendor's put us on cash-on-delivery. Again. And—" a second page "—Griff's truck is on one of those auto-sale sites. He listed it Tuesday night. He doesn't know I know."

Wren looked at the pages for a long moment. The Meridian sign out front had been missing its second *i* since August, and Griff kept saying he'd ordered the letter, and everyone kept pretending to believe him. Twelve people worked here. Priya was putting her little brother through Green River on this desk. Manny's kid had braces on the company's dental plan, which Griff paid for out of a line item Wren was fairly sure was labeled *miscellaneous* because *personal savings* looked bad in an audit.

"How bad," she said quietly.

"Okafor-or-bust bad," Priya said. "If she renews, we make spring. If she doesn't—" Priya took the pages back, folded them with dispatcher's precision, and made them disappear. "So fly nice today, Captain. And be civil to the new guy. We can't afford turnover *or* your standards."

"My standards keep this operation alive."

"Your standards," Priya said, "are why you drive a truck older than my brother and check the fuel caps twice. Which — yes. Keep doing that. But Wren?" She glanced across the lobby, to where Theo Marsh was walking back from the copier reading his own new-hire paperwork like it might be on the test, mouthing something to himself — memorizing, actually memorizing the ops manual excerpts — and Priya's voice went gentle in a way that was worse than the teasing. "Not everyone who walks through rain is walking away from something. Some of them are just walking toward the airplane."

Wren signed the last line of the release harder than the paper required.

---

Griff's office smelled like burned coffee and lease payments. He had the crew schedule on the whiteboard, OKAFOR written across four dates in a marker that was giving its life for the cause.

"Adaeze Okafor," Griff said. "Hotels. You've flown her — Wren has, anyway. She books the airplane like other people book a table. Seattle, Portland, San Francisco, Santa Barbara, back around. She is, no exaggeration, forty percent of our revenue, and her contract's up for renewal at the end of the month." He looked at Theo. "Which is why the new guy rides today, and the new guy is perfect."

"The new guy passed his one-thirty-five checks yesterday," Theo said mildly. "The new guy would like his window seat."

"Two-ninety-three's fresh, the instrument check's fresh," Griff agreed. "Dale Voss said — and I'm quoting a man who has not used an adjective since the nineties — *adequate*."

"He wept," Theo said. "It was moving."

Wren picked up the release and read it instead of laughing, because she'd decided at some point in the last hour that not laughing at Theo Marsh was going to be a policy, and policies only work if you follow them when it's inconvenient.

One more thing happened in that office, small at the time. Griff asked Theo how his dad was doing, and Theo's whole weather changed — still smiling, but the ceiling came down a thousand feet.

"Better," Theo said. "Walking farther. Cussing at his physical therapist, which the therapist says is a good sign. He keeps asking when he gets to fly the Bonanza again, so, you know. That's the project."

"Medical?" Griff asked, gently, one old pilot to another's son.

"That's the question mark. His AME won't touch a special issuance for a while yet, so we've been reading up on BasicMed — he qualifies to try for it, held a regular medical way back, so now it'd be the physical with any state-licensed doc, the online course, the checklist in the logbook. And it'd cover the Bonanza fine — six seats, under the weight, under two-fifty knots, under eighteen thousand, day, night, IFR if he's able. Private flying only. It won't ever cover *this*—" he knocked the doorframe of the charter office "—no flying for hire on BasicMed. But Dad doesn't want the King Air. He wants Tuesday breakfast in Friday Harbor." He shrugged, and the smile came back up like it had never moved. "Anyway. He'll get there or I'll fly him there."

Wren looked at him a half-second longer than she meant to. Later she would mark that half-second as the beginning of the whole disaster, but at the time it filed itself under *nothing*.

"Fuel's ordered," she said. "Brief in twenty."

---

The trouble started, as most of Wren's troubles did, with her being right in a way that didn't matter.

They were set up in the little briefing room off the FBO lobby — charts up, release signed, Okafor's inbound car twenty minutes out — when Wren reached for the crew logbook binder and found Theo's currency page still open. She wasn't snooping. It was face-up on the table between them, and captains read what's face-up.

Neat columns. Every approach logged with date, place, approach type. And in the remarks column, in handwriting that took its time: *"Current ≠ proficient. Fix: hand-fly one approach per trip minimum, no FD, until further notice."*

He'd written himself a training program. Nobody had asked him to.

"This is good," she said, tapping the page — and if she'd stopped there, history goes differently, Okafor charters some other airplane, and nobody has to fall in love. But Wren, being Wren, kept going. "So here's how today runs. You're monitoring. I'm flying. Radios and checklists are yours; the airplane is mine. You've been in Skyhawks for three years and this is a revenue leg with the client aboard, so today is not the day you hand-fly anything. We'll get you your approach on a repo leg. That work?"

It was reasonable. It was even kind, by her math — she'd built him a runway. But it came out in the voice, the freight-dog voice, the one that had gotten her through Alaska and through Cascade Air Group and through the year after Cascade Air Group, and she watched it land on him like ice on a warm wing.

"That works," Theo said slowly, "and also, I'd like to fly the leg down and let you take it if I get behind. Because with respect, Captain — *watching* you be proficient doesn't make me proficient. You know that. You knew it before I got here; it's why you asked Priya to pull my currency instead of asking me." He smiled to soften it — almost worked, too. "Somebody in this room taught me that watching doesn't count."

"You taught yourself that lapsing four approaches was a rounding error until it wasn't," Wren said, sharper than the situation deserved, "so forgive me if I don't hand a client leg to your learning curve."

"That's fair, and it's also not what I—"

"It's exactly what you—"

"—asked, I asked for the *chance* to—"

"—and I'm telling you the answer is the repo leg—"

They were leaning across the table at each other now, both of them pointing at the same laminated chart like it had personally offended them, voices low and fast and overlapping the way voices only get when two people are, against all professional advice, *interested* in the argument —

— which is precisely when the briefing room door opened, because the car had been ten minutes out, not twenty, and Adaeze Okafor walked in wearing a coat that cost more than the coffee maker Meridian couldn't replace, took one look at the two of them frozen mid-lean, mid-point, mid-sentence —

— and lit up like a runway.

"Oh," she said, delighted, pressing a hand to her heart. "*Oh.* You two are together."

The silence that followed had a shape. Wren would remember it as roughly the shape of the rest of her life.

"Ma'am," Wren said, straightening, "we were just briefing the—"

"My late husband and I fought exactly like that," Okafor said, sweeping in, already extending a hand to Theo, who shook it on autopilot. "Both of us pointing at the same piece of paper. Forty-one years. You know how I knew he loved me? He never once let me win an argument he thought might hurt me later, and he never once won one in a way that cost me anything to lose. Ah—" she waved a finger between them, a conductor cueing the strings "—that's what this is. I *know* what this is. What are your names? No — you're Captain Hale, I remember you, you flew me in March, you were wasted on that dull first officer. And you are?"

"Theo Marsh, ma'am. First officer. And we're not—"

"First officer," Okafor repeated, with the satisfaction of a woman completing a crossword. "Flying with your captain. You know, I chose this little company over the big fractional people because it feels like a family, and now look. It *is* one." She turned to the door, where Griff had materialized with the desperate smile of a man watching forty percent of his revenue develop an opinion. "Marcus! Why didn't you tell me? A couple! Flying together! It's the most romantic thing I've ever heard. I want them. This crew, my flights, all season. Write it down."

"Ma'am," Wren tried again, "Mr. Marsh and I are not—"

"—not *supposed to say*," Griff said, loudly, from the doorway, in the exact tone of a man stepping off a cliff and deciding on the way down that he could probably fly. "Company policy. Crew pairings, personal relationships — we keep it very quiet. Very professional. You understand."

Adaeze Okafor looked at Griff. Then at Theo, whose face was doing something complicated. Then at Wren, for a long, bright, unhurried moment — and if Wren had been asked, under oath, she'd have said the old woman's eyes held something a lot more knowing than belief.

"Of course," Okafor said graciously. "Very quiet. Very professional." She patted Wren's arm on her way past, toward the lobby and the airplane and the future. "Don't worry, Captain. I'm famously discreet."

The door swung shut. The room exhaled.

"Griff," Wren said.

"I know."

"*Griff.*"

"I know. Renewal's in three weeks. We'll — figure it out after the trip." He was already backing out of the room, already gone in every way that mattered. "Great brief, guys. Wheels up in forty."

Which left the two of them alone with the charts and the wreckage of the last five minutes.

Theo cleared his throat. "So," he said. "I've been here three hours, and we're dating."

"We are not dating."

"We're *fake* dating," he conceded, "which — for the record, and this is going to matter later — I want noted: I logged an objection." He tapped the crew log, where his own remarks column sat face-up, and despite everything, despite the client and the contract and Griff and the rain, the corner of his mouth went up. "Right here next to *current isn't proficient*. Because I have a feeling, Captain, that this is exactly that kind of problem."

"Meaning what, Marsh?"

"Meaning we're legal on paper. Doesn't mean we know what we're doing."

Wren looked at him — at the neat logbook, the ruined argument, the eight-hundred-foot ceiling out the window that they'd be climbing through in half an hour, on instruments, into the sunlight everybody down here had to take on faith.

"Get your currency page off my table," she said, "and go preflight the airplane."

He went. And Wren stood a moment in the empty briefing room, tallying the score with the honesty she was famous for: Theo Marsh had been current since May, proficient since never in a King Air, employed for three days, and her fake boyfriend for four minutes.

It was going to be a long season. She picked up the release and went flying anyway.

---

*The climb checklist was done and the airplane was in the sunlight on top before either of them spoke again — nine thousand feet, tops below them like a field of snow nobody would ever walk on.*

"Debrief," Wren said, because ritual was how she apologized. "Back there, before the door opened. You were right about one thing. Say it back so I know you know which thing."

Theo didn't gloat, which she noted. "Watching isn't flying. Currency lives in what *I* logged — six approaches, holding, intercepting and tracking, in the last six calendar months, or I'm not legal to act as PIC under IFR. Lapse it, I've got six more months to fix it with a safety pilot before it takes an IPC to buy it back."

"And I was right about?"

"The floor's not the ceiling. Being legal isn't being ready — that's the proficiency piece, and it's on me, every trip, forever. Hence the remarks column." He glanced over. "And the safety pilot has to be rated for the airplane, current medical, dual controls — since you'll ask."

"I'll always ask."

"I'm counting on it," he said, and turned back to the window before she could decide what to do with that.

*End of Chapter One.*
