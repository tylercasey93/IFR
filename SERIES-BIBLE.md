## 1. The elevator pitch

Two pilots at a broke Seattle charter outfit — best friends, two years in one cockpit — discover that the whale client keeping the company alive is a semi-retired CIA officer, and that she needs their Gulfstream to smuggle a defector and her daughter home from Japan. The operation hands the first officer a cover assignment: fake-date the Agency's cover officer for the couple legend the exfil rides on. He is very, very good at it — and every gesture he manufactures for a stranger turns out to be one he has already made, unthinking, for the man in the left seat. Three books: the mission becomes a job, the friendship becomes the thing neither will name, and the secrets of the work nearly destroy the one thing that was never a cover. They get engaged on the last pages of Book Three, and not one minute sooner.

Tone: Tom Clancy with more aviation and romance — procedural authenticity, professional adversaries, danger with weight — while the crew's Fox-and-O'Hare chemistry (Cole's exasperated warmth, Jack's lethal polish) supplies the heart and the pressure-valve humor. Underneath it all: a spine of real instrument flying that quietly teaches the ACS.

## 3. The spy architecture

### Who Okafor really is
**Adaeze Okafor, 71.** The hotels are real — forty of them, built over fifty years, and they were always also infrastructure: safehouses, meeting grounds, listening posts across three continents. Recruited in Lagos in the late seventies (the harmattan story is true — her husband was her first case officer, which is why "everything that matters starts with weather" is also tradecraft). Officially retired from the Agency for a decade; actually runs a quiet contract program — **BRIDGEBELL** — building deniable civilian logistics on the Pacific Rim: couriers, exfil capacity, clean aircraft, crews who can keep their mouths shut and their headings exact. She does not lie to the people she recruits about anything except the one thing she must. Her tragedy: she is permanently, professionally alone, and the "matchmaking" is the one honest hobby she has left — and she has been watching these two pilots orbit each other for two years with a connoisseur's patience.

### Why she wants these two
- **Cole** — the Cascade file. A captain who reported his own captain's falsified logs, took the industry's punishment for it, and never once told the story to save himself. Integrity under documented pressure: the rarest asset profile there is. Plus standards discipline that runs the safest small flight department in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Jack** — the assessor's dream: a natural at absolutely everything he tries, a compulsive reader of rooms, a man who improvises cover stories for *fun* and walked away from his airline class for family without bitterness. The polish is real and so is what it protects.
- **Together** — a crew whose CRM under failure (she engineers small tests all through Book 1's first act) is better than most agency air branch pairs, bonded by something she can see and they refuse to look at. She never says the word; she just keeps booking the rooms.

### The recruitment ladder (series spine)
1. **Assessment, compressed** (Book 1, Act I): Okafor has been watching Meridian for months before page one — the G550 management contract *was* the first move, and Voss's check-airman grades on both pilots were the file's first pages. She runs two or three quiet confirmation tests (a no-go call she engineers, one odd routing) before deciding. The reader may clock the tests; the crew doesn't.
2. **The unmasking** (Book 1, ~30% mark): not a victory lap — a necessity. An asset's exfiltration route has collapsed; the service hunting her is watching airline manifests, borders, and government aircraft. What nobody watches: a scheduled charter full of "lodge investors" on a Part 135 certificate with ten years of boring paperwork. Okafor needs Meridian's Gulfstream and its crew, witting, in nine days. She tells them the truth because the truth is the only way they'll be good enough.
3. **The mission** (Book 1, Acts II–III): preparation — including the couple legend that pairs Jack with Wren — the outbound legs, the pickup, the run home. Detail-forward, Clancy-style: eAPIS and customs procedure, oceanic clearances, fuel criticality, the opposition's moves — all braided with the ACS teaching, because the mission *is* instrument flying done perfectly under pressure.
4. **Training & standing tasks** (Book 2): they said yes once; now the Agency wants it formal. Voss's sim syllabus, tradecraft school, courier legs, surveillance-detection routes — and the discovery that the opposition remembers the Gulfstream from Book 1.
5. **The cost** (Book 3): the mole question, sabotage, and the discovery that secrets — even authorized ones — behave exactly like lies inside a relationship.

### Opposition (build slowly)
- **Book 1:** no villain on-stage. Background static only: someone else is also watching Meridian (a second buyer probing Griff's debts; a "journalist" asking about the captain's Cascade history; Jax's fractional sniffing around the G550's owner trust).
- **Book 2:** the static resolves into **Pacific Corporate Air** — not evil itself, but its charter arm has been quietly bought by a logistics network fronting for a hostile service's West Coast collection effort. Jax isn't a spy; he's a useful idiot with a leased conscience, which is worse.
- **Book 3:** the network's professionals arrive — and its local contractor gets a face: **Danny Ruiz**, recruited 2025, now running the Kang channel for Verin's directorate. The Draft One electrical-cascade emergency returns **as sabotage** designed on Ruiz's insider knowledge. The gala misunderstanding returns spy-grade (§7, Book 3).

### Rules for the spy content (tone guardrails — Clancy calibration)
- **Competence is the aesthetic.** Procedure rendered lovingly and accurately — flight planning, customs, tradecraft, opposition surveillance — the way Clancy renders sonar rooms.
- **The danger is real and has weight.** People can die in this world; the asset's fear is genuine; the opposition is a professional service, not henchmen. On-page violence stays restrained — tension over gore.
- **A named, plausible adversary** (§3a) with its own competence and logic; no cartoon villains.
- **The tradecraft is as accurate as the flying:** real SDR logic, cover discipline, compartmentalization, "an asset is a person" ethics — taught with the same care as the 1-2-3 rule.
- **The Agency is neither halo nor devil:** Okafor is honorable inside a machine that isn't always; that tension powers Book 3.
- **The banter survives as a pressure valve** — crews joke in the worst weather; that's authentic, not tonal drift. But the jokes get shorter as the stakes rise.
- **The romance stays human** (standing policy): missions may *rhyme* with the relationship, but nobody flirts in cryptonyms, and the big beats are spoken plainly.

### 3a. The Book 1 operation — WINTER HARBOR (working name)

**The asset: Dr. Yelena Sorokina, 44.** Senior electronic-warfare engineer — navigation-warfare specialist (GPS jamming and spoofing). Decided to come over after her program was folded into targeting work she couldn't live with; passed material quietly for two years through a contact in Okafor's network. Burned three weeks ago by a counterintelligence sweep; ran mid-conference in Singapore with a go-bag and her teenage daughter, **Mara, 15**. Currently hidden in staff quarters of an Okafor hotel in **Sapporo, Japan** — safe for days, not weeks. Brilliant, exhausted, allergic to being handled; speaks flawless textbook English learned from aviation manuals — which gives the *teaching* a second instructor: the woman who builds jammers explaining, from the jump seat, exactly why green needles saved them.
**Why she matters (Clancy hook):** her head holds the adversary's nav-warfare order of battle. Every GPS-integrity lesson in the series is her subject matter. In Book 3, the tech she warned about is what the opposition uses on the crew.

**The adversary: the GRU's 8th Directorate technical-security apparatus** (fictionalized as needed). In Book 1 they are a search grid, not faces: manifest watchers, airport spotters, one traveling two-man team a half-step behind. Their competence is the clock.

**The couple legend (the fake-dating device, v5):** the exfil rides on a lodge-investment charter built from Okafor's real Hokkaido holdings, and the investor party's centerpiece is a couple — a legend Chidinma has been aging for months: married-adjacent, moneyed, dull. The female half is **Wren Hale**, BRIDGEBELL's cover officer. The male half has to be someone already woven into the charter's story who can also fly if the day goes wrong — and who can perform easy, photographable intimacy with a stranger. Okafor picks Jack, for exactly the gifts he's proudest of, and the assignment does two things no one briefs: it hands Aaron's exit its cause, and it makes Cole watch his best friend rehearse being in love. The device's punchline is Wren's — the professional who has run six of these legends clocks Jack inside a week: *she's* not the one he keeps performing for.

**The route (aviation spine of Book 1):** Boeing Field → Anchorage (tech stop; international ops teaching) → New Chitose/Sapporo under the lodge-investment charter → dwell day that goes wrong by hours, not days → return legs across the Aleutian chain with weather closing and a diversion decision that lands them at a gravel-adjacent Alaska alternate with the asset aboard → U.S. entry, customs, and the cold realities of arrival. Every leg carries ACS content; the finale's approach is flown to real minimums with everything on the line.

**The moral engine — the manifest problem:** Cole Bishop ended his career at Cascade rather than let falsified paperwork fly. The exfil requires a doctored manifest and two passengers who do not exist — and the pilot-in-command signs it. Okafor, who chose Cole *because* of the Cascade file, must ask him to cross the one line that defines him, and the honest answer to "how is this different from Ruiz?" is the beating heart of Book 1: *Ruiz lied to protect himself; this lie protects someone else, and Cole signs his own name to it.* Jack's version of the problem: lying is easy for him — what terrifies him is discovering how easy, and what Sam would think. The book takes both problems seriously instead of waving them off.

## 6. The airplane

**Gulfstream G550** — two-crew, ~6,750 nm range, FL510 ceiling, the workhorse of serious private aviation (and, in the real world, of more than one government's quiet fleets — the in-joke writes itself).

- **Ownership mechanic:** Meridian doesn't own it — they *manage* it for an owner trust ("Rainier Aviation Holdings LLC") that pays late, specifies odd routings, and turns out, in the Book 1 reveal, to be one of Adaeze's cutouts. The airplane was the first move of the recruitment; Meridian was chosen, not lucky.
- Tail: **N550MA** ("Meridian Alpha"). Callsign "Meridian Five-Fifty."
- Teaching consequences (all manageable): boots/prop-heat icing content becomes bleed-air anti-ice with the piston/turboprop material taught through Sam's Bonanza and flashback/instructing scenes; the multiengine-emergency ACS content (VII.B/C) moves to Voss's Book 2 sim sequences; high-altitude ops (RVSM, oxygen, coffin corner) become *bonus* teaching the King Air couldn't offer; international legs add ICAO flight plans and oceanic procedure flavor.
- Fuel-state, holding, approaches, lost comm, partial panel: all identical doctrine, bigger stage.

## 7. Trilogy architecture

Romance throttle (the contract, v3/v5): **three lines, one per book — Jack and Cole.**

- **Book 1 — the physical line.** Two years of friendship and shared crew rooms open the book; both are casually seeing other people (§ Casual partners, below); the mission compresses everything — and the fake-date assignment detonates it: Jack discovers, gesture by manufactured gesture, that the performance is a copy of something real, and Cole discovers he cannot watch. Proximity, adrenaline, and earned trust do the rest — the night after the asset is delivered safely, in the last shared room of the operation, they stop pretending. Crucially, Book 1 ends with the *fact* but not the *relationship*: no declarations, no definitions, two professionals treating a live grenade as a paperweight. The final-page cliffhanger is double: Okafor's job offer unanswered, and the two of them undiscussed.
- **Book 2 — the emotional line.** The "not a thing" thing keeps happening and keeps not being discussed, which stops working under operational pressure; jealousy, safehouse domesticity, and one honest scare force the words. Book 2 ends with the actual commitment — spoken plainly, per the standing language policy.
- **Book 3 — the vow line.** Fully together, then the spy-grade rupture (worse now: you can only feel *the-relationship-was-managed* betrayal about a real relationship), reconciliation in the crucible, engagement in the last chapter.

**Casual partners (Book 1, Act I):**
- **Aaron Brandt, 33** — Harborview ER charge nurse; he and Jack have had an easy, no-questions arrangement built on incompatible schedules. The couple legend ends it: Jack can't explain the woman he's suddenly photographed with, and Aaron exits kindly, with the observation Jack isn't ready to hear — *"I'm not competing with a girlfriend you can't explain. And honestly? It wouldn't be her I was losing to anyway. I've heard you talk about your captain."* First on-page confirmation that Jack's heart has a heading, delivered as biography, not announcement.
- **Elias Ward, 38** — marine structural engineer in Ballard; he and **Cole** see each other when the season allows, zero questions asked, which is precisely why Cole chose him. Cole ends it in person, with characteristic decency and no real explanation; Elias's reply — *"Figured. Good luck, Captain."* — tells the reader everything about what it was.
- Function: both exits are caused by the mission (the legend eats Jack's real life; the secrecy eats Cole's), which lets the reader watch the operation destroy the casual arrangements long before anyone admits why neither man minds.

### Book 1 — *WINTER HARBOR* (working title)

- **ACS load:** Areas I, II, III, IV (prep, weather, planning, procedures, clearances, holding, attitude flying, unusual attitudes) + international-ops bonus content the mission demands.
- **Act I — the crew (ch. 1–6):** Meridian's ordinary extraordinary: the G550 and its odd owner trust, the Okafor account and its strange routings, two pilots who finish each other's checklists, crew rooms with two beds and one alarm. Establish the friendship at full depth — the hospital-corridor history, the standing debrief ritual, the running jokes — plus the background static (a "journalist" asking about Cascade; someone probing Meridian's debts) and both casual arrangements (Aaron; Elias). Okafor's confirmation tests live inside the set pieces (the grounded-airplane no-go is a test she engineered; one strange routing request; the icing encounter proves the crew under real weather).
- **The unmasking (ch. 7, the 30% hinge):** Okafor cancels the week's schedule, books the briefing room, and retires the hotel-magnate mask — Sorokina, Mara, Sapporo, nine days. She offers them the truth, the stakes, and the door: anyone may walk away, once, now, no consequence. The chapter ends on Cole asking the only question that matters to him: "Who signs the manifest?"
- **Act II — preparation (ch. 8–13):** Clancy-mode planning rendered with love: building the lodge-investor legend from Okafor's real holdings; **Wren Hale arrives** with the cover paperwork and a fiancé-shaped hole in her legend that Okafor has already decided Jack fills; the fake-dating tradecraft taught straight (backstory drills, ring logistics, how couples stand); eAPIS, customs brokers, oceanic clearances, fuel criticality; Voss appears "coincidentally" for recurrent sims that are obviously rehearsal; Cole fights the manifest problem to a private decision — and tells no one what it costs him. Romance throttle: Cole briefing his best friend on a legend that requires him to hold someone else's hand; Jack flawless at it and quieter every day; one almost-moment in a crew room interrupted by op traffic; Aaron's exit; Elias's exit; nothing admitted out loud.
- **Act III — the run (ch. 14–19):** Boeing Field to Anchorage to Sapporo. Jack and Wren play the couple through the dwell day while Cole runs the airplane — and the dwell day collapses by hours; the pickup goes off-script (Mara's papers; a spotter at the FBO); the return across the Aleutians into deteriorating weather with the searchers a half-step behind — holding, the diversion decision, an Alaska alternate at minimums, and a customs arrival where the paperwork must be perfect *and* false. The adrenaline kiss lives here — Jack and Cole, on the ramp at the end of the worst leg — and this time it is not denied: the night after the asset is delivered safely, in the last shared room of the operation, they stop pretending. Written to the established heat standard (warm, open-door, human language).
- **Final chapter (ch. 20):** the morning after, executed with catastrophic professionalism by two people declining to have a conversation; the debrief; Wren's dry benediction on her way out ("Best fake fiancé I've had. Tell him, or I will"); Okafor's formal offer — not a mission this time, a *job* — delivered to a crew she can plainly see has crossed its own border. Double cliffhanger: the offer unanswered, the night undiscussed.
- **Romance ceiling for Book 1:** friendship, then the legend's tuition, then trust under fire, then the ramp kiss, then the night — and not one word of definition. The feelings remain the classified material.

### Book 2 — *POSITION AND HOLD* (working title)
- **ACS load:** Areas V and VI (nav systems, DME arcs, departures/arrivals, every approach type) + VII.B/C in Voss's sims.
- **Opening:** the answer to Book 1's cliffhanger — they say yes, separately, for different reasons (Cole's is the mission and the manifest debt he now carries; Jack's is, honestly, Cole — and Sorokina, whose safety made the work real).
- **Plot:** formal training braided with recurrent checks; standing courier legs with Wren as third crew; the GPS-interference day becomes a live exercise with Sorokina consulting from a safehouse; the Boise "closing" is a meet; Pacific Corporate revealed as the opposition's front — the GRU network hunting the leak that let Sorokina run has traced the exfil to a West Coast charter profile, and is buying its way into the industry to find the airplane. Jax circles Meridian professionally, blind to what he's carrying. Jack's standing-cover paperwork comes back drafted "simpler" — straight — and Jack makes them correct it, on Okafor's own doctrine: the best covers are true.
- **Romance throttle:** the Book 1 night was "just the mission" — except it keeps happening, unscheduled and undiscussed, an arrangement with all of the intimacy and none of the words. Book 2 is the cost of that: jealousy without standing (a training-cadre officer flirts with Cole; Aaron resurfaces once, politely, and Cole discovers he minds enormously), safehouse domesticity that feels more married than either admits, and one honest scare that makes the silence unaffordable. Sam tension: the first time in Jack's life he can't tell his father the truth — about the work or the man. **Final chapter:** snowed-in safehouse, no audience, no op, no exits — the words, finally, spoken plainly: this is real, this is exclusive, this is *us*.
- **Cliffhanger:** morning after: Chidinma at the door. One of Book 2's packages was flagged. There may be a leak inside BRIDGEBELL.

### Book 3 — *LOST COMMS* (working title)
- **ACS load:** Areas VII and VIII (lost comm, partial panel, emergencies, postflight) + full-series capstone review.
- **Plot:** together and operational — and compartmentalization starts doing to them what lies do to everyone else. The mole hunt puts Cole under quiet suspicion — and the suspicion is *engineered*: the network's feed is authored by Danny Ruiz, who wrote the first lie about Cole at Cascade and knows precisely which entries in his file read two ways (the Book 1 manifest signature is Ruiz's suggestion). The traitor is Elliot Kang, run by Ruiz, levered by a brother in a Vladivostok cell. The gala returns as an operational party where Jack overhears Cole being debriefed out of context — file language about "keeping Marsh committed to the program," Okafor's old assessment line about putting the mission inside the fence — and believes the one unbearable thing: *that the relationship was managed.* That he was an assignment Cole accepted. Eleven days of silence in which both keep flying the mission anyway. The sabotage flight (the electrical cascade, now enemy action) forces them back into one cockpit; they fly the partial-panel emergency as the crew they never stopped being; the truth lands on the ramp. Endgame: the network rolled up; Kang unmasked in Book 3's saddest scene and surrendered through the crew; Ruiz taken not in a gunfight but in the paperwork, with Cole granted fifteen minutes across a federal table to discover how *small* he always was; Okafor's program survives; and the crew sets terms: no secrets *between* them, ever again, whatever the clearance. Jax's blown gossip remains a contributing thread without being the answer.
- **Final chapter:** spring; Sam flies the Bonanza to Friday Harbor; the window-seat breakfast (Wren holding the counter seat she was asked to hold); the on-one-knee, plain-words proposal — Jack to Cole, the existing speech's spine rewritten for the man it was always about; "Yes. Obviously, yes."

## 8. Teaching mechanics across the series

- The **Crew Debrief** chapter-end ritual continues in all three books — Cole's standards discipline and Jack's instructor's craft trading seats — and gains a second voice in Books 2–3: flying debrief + tradecraft debrief, same discipline ("narration is free" turns out to be an agency proverb too — Voss taught it to Adaeze's pilots decades ago; the reveal that Meridian's whole cockpit culture descends from Air Branch doctrine is a Book 2 delight).
- The wiki stays the companion reference; each book's front matter maps its chapters to ACS areas.
