# Chapter Twelve — Decision Altitude

*Theo*

Medford in early December was doing its specialty: valley fog, packed into the Rogue like insulation, with the tops of the Siskiyous standing clear above it in hard winter sun. The ATIS made it official — ceiling two hundred overcast, runway one-four RVR two thousand four hundred, wind calm, ILS runway one-four in use — and Theo read it twice and felt the particular cold-water clarity that came with real minimums.

"Two hundred and twenty-four hundred RVR," Wren said, from the right seat.

That was the day's other headline: the right seat. His leg — but more than his leg. She'd stood in the flight planning room that morning, coffee in hand, and said, in front of Griff, "Marsh flies the ILS at Medford. I'll monitor," in the tone of a woman entering something into a training record. Okafor and Chidinma were in back with a lodge broker's brochures. A revenue leg, a client aboard, weather at mins, and Captain Wren Hale had handed him the approach like it was Tuesday.

"Brief it," she said now. "All of it. Pretend Voss is in the jumpseat eating a protein bar judgmentally."

"ILS one-four at Medford," Theo began. "Precision approach — which means, unlike everything we flew at Santa Barbara, this one has government-guaranteed vertical guidance, and the whole geometry gets us to a **decision altitude**: thirteen hundred and thirty feet, two hundred above the touchdown zone. The system itself, since you asked for all of it: the **localizer** — course guidance, transmitted from the far end, beam tailored three to six degrees wide so it's about seven hundred feet across at the threshold. Four times touchier than a VOR needle, which is why new pilots S-turn down every ILS in America until they learn to fly headings, not needles. The **glideslope** — the vertical half, three degrees here, crossing the threshold at about fifty feet. Full-scale deflection is only about seven-tenths of a degree, so the closer you get, the smaller the box gets: at the marker it's hundreds of feet tall, at the threshold it's a doorway. And the discipline nobody writes down: intercept the glideslope *from below*. Level at the intercept altitude, watch it come alive above you, and join it as it descends onto you — chasing one down from above at low altitude is how you find a false lobe or an early ground, and there's no version of that story with applause at the end."

"Rate of descent."

"Groundspeed rule: for a three-degree slope, groundspeed times five. We'll cross the fix at about a hundred and twenty knots over the ground — call it six hundred feet a minute, and I set that *before* intercept so the airplane's already in the descent habit when the needle centers. If the fpm I need to hold the slope creeps way off that math, something's lying — wind's changed or I'm chasing — and the number is my audit."

"Minimums. The law, this time. All the way through."

"Ninety-one one-seventy-five. At DA, one of two things happens, and I decide *at* DA, not below it — that's the difference from an MDA: the DA is a decision made in motion, and the geometry accepts that the airplane, already descending, momentarily dips below it during the go-around. Legal, planned for, protected. To continue past DA I need three things. One: the aircraft continuously in a position to land normally, in the touchdown zone. Two: flight visibility at or above the published minimum — RVR twenty-four hundred, and note it's *flight* visibility, my seat, my judgment, though if tower's calling RVR below mins inbound of the FAF the conversation gets formal. Three: at least one of the listed visual references. The list — Voss makes upgrades recite it, so." He ticked it on his fingers, flying with the other hand. "The approach light system. The threshold. Threshold markings, threshold lights. The runway end identifier lights. The visual glideslope indicator — the PAPI. The touchdown zone or its markings or its lights. The runway itself, its markings, its lights. Any *one*, plus visibility, plus position, and I can keep coming."

"The asterisk. The one everybody flunks."

"The approach lights asterisk," Theo said. "If the *only* thing I've got at DA is the approach light system — the ball of light in the fog, no runway, no threshold — I may continue, but only down to **a hundred feet above the touchdown zone elevation**, and I hold there, descending no further, unless and until I can see the **red terminating bars or the red side-row bars** — the red elements buried in the last part of an ALSF-style system. Red in sight, the runway's coming and I can continue. No red by a hundred feet — go around. It's the rule that saves the crews who see *something* at DA and let *something* pull them all the way into the ground short of the pavement."

"Critical areas, since it's calm and foggy."

"Days exactly like this. When the weather's under eight hundred and two, ground control protects the **ILS critical areas** — the zones near the antennas where a taxiing airplane bends the beams. That hold-short line with the ladder-looking sign is somebody protecting *my* glideslope from a King Air's tail. And in the air the same idea: a big jet crossing the localizer antenna as I'm at three hundred feet can put a ripple through the needle — short-final twitches in fog have causes, and half of airmanship is knowing which twitches are real."

Wren was quiet for a second, and then delivered the highest compliment in her arsenal: "No corrections." A beat. "Fly it exactly like you briefed it, and Marsh — hand-fly it. Flight director's yours, autopilot stays off. I want the logbook to say you did this, not that you watched it."

From the back, the cabin speaker, gentle: "Captain. Is it wise, the fog being what it is—"

"Ma'am," Wren said, without a flicker, "he's the best instrument pilot on this airplane, and I include myself. We're flying to a set of numbers with a decision built in, and if the numbers say no, we go to Portland and I buy everyone dinner. You're in good hands. I checked them myself."

The speaker clicked off. Theo flew the arrival with his ears roaring slightly, and it had nothing at all to do with the pressurization.

---

The fog took them at four thousand.

It was the smooth kind, the deep kind, gray wool all the way down, and the world contracted to the panel and the voices: Approach's vectors, Wren's readbacks, the localizer coming alive — *course alive* — the intercept from below exactly as advertised, needle descending onto them like something merciful — *glideslope alive, six hundred set* — and then the FAF, the marker's blue flash, gear down and final flaps and the before-landing checklist in Wren's steady cadence, and Meridian Three-Five rode down the wire into the Rogue Valley at six hundred feet a minute with two pilots breathing like metronomes.

The needles, Theo would remember, stayed inside a quarter dot the whole way, and he would also remember that it took everything — headings, not needles; tiny corrections; the fpm audit running; fixation prowling the edge of his scan like a wolf at a fence — the localizer tightening, the glideslope box shrinking, the radio altimeter counting down through five hundred, four hundred, three—

"One hundred above," Wren called. Gray wool. Nothing.

"Continuing."

"Minimums."

And there it was — not the runway, not the threshold — the **approach lights**, a smeared constellation rising out of the murk dead ahead, sequenced flashers stabbing toward them and vanishing.

"Approach lights in sight, continuing," Theo said, and the rule ran in him like current: *lights only — a hundred above touchdown zone and no lower without the red.* He held the wire, DA sliding behind, radio altimeter unwinding — two-fifty, two hundred — eyes out now with instruments in his fingertips, and the light-ball resolved, structure emerging, white bars, white bars, and then — there — **red side-row bars**, unmistakable, embers in the wool, and beyond them the green threshold lights igniting all at once like a decision being approved.

"Red side-rows. Runway," Wren confirmed, calm as a checklist. "Cleared to land, one-four."

The threshold swept under. The fog let them go at eighty feet the way it always does at real minimums — reluctantly, all at once — and the touchdown zone markings came up to meet the mains with the centerline running true, and Theo flew the King Air onto the pavement of runway one-four in an RVR of twenty-four hundred feet and made, if he said so himself, one of the great quiet landings of his career.

Nobody in the back said anything for a second. Then Chidinma, reverent: "We couldn't see *anything*. And then there were red lights, and then a runway existed."

"That," said Adaeze Okafor, "is the most honest description of faith I have heard in forty years of Sundays. Well flown, gentlemen. Both kinds."

Wren's hand appeared over the pedestal, palm up. Theo looked at it, at her — and she wasn't smiling the approach-lights smile. She was smiling the whole runway.

"Logbook that," she said. "Hand-flown ILS, two hundred and twenty-four hundred, client aboard, zero drama. Some upgrade candidate."

He took her hand. In front of God, the client, and the fuel truck. Neither of them let go on schedule.

---

The lodge broker took the client to dinner; the crew got the hotel bar's back patio, propane heaters, and the fog rolling past the railing like the day's approach hadn't quite finished with them. Wren had a bourbon and her jacket collar up. Theo had the day still humming in his hands.

"Debrief's done," she said — they'd run it on the ramp, the whole recital, localizer to red bars — "so this is just—" she gestured with the glass at the heaters, the fog, the two of them, off-duty and audience-free "—this."

"This," he agreed.

The propane hissed. Somewhere in the fog a train said something melancholy about Oregon.

"I keep thinking about the asterisk rule," Wren said, at last, to her bourbon. "You get to DA. You see *something* — lights, a shape, a promise. And the rule says: you may keep coming, but only so far, unless the red shows. The real thing. The unambiguous thing." She turned the glass a quarter-turn, and he watched her borrow his tell with the fondness of a lender who never expected repayment. "I've been at a hundred feet for a while now, Theo. Over the lights. Holding altitude. Waiting to see—"

"Wren."

"—the red bars," she finished, and looked up, and the fog light did something to her eyes that the FAA had no chart for, and Theo Marsh set his glass down and reached across the little table for her hand, and the phone rang.

*Griff.* Wren's phone, buzzing itself in a slow circle on the metal table, the screen bright with the little contact photo of Griff in his lucky Meridian cap, and they both stared at it with the loathing of two pilots watching a perfectly stable approach get sent around by a runway incursion.

She answered on speaker, because Clause One had spawned a corollary and the corollary was *no secrets on ops calls*. "Griff."

"Wren! Theo there? Good, good." Papers shuffling, the man audibly wearing his desperate-optimist voice. "Two things. Renewal's *signed* — season extension, numbers we don't deserve, Priya cried, don't tell her I said. And — the other thing. Okafor's throwing the closing party. Big one. The Rainier Club, Seattle, Saturday after next — investors, the lodge people, everybody. Black tie." A pause with a swallow in it. "Crew's invited. Specifically. By name. Both of you, together, on the invitation, in — uh — in calligraphy."

Beyond the railing, the fog moved. On the invisible approach somewhere above it, someone else's airplane droned down someone else's wire.

"A gala," Wren said. Flat as a plate.

"A gala!" Griff agreed miserably. "Fun! Formal! Extremely public!" The unsaid part — *the act, dressed up, under chandeliers, at maximum altitude* — filled the patio. "Anyway. Great flying today, Okafor already called me, she used the word *magnificent* — okay, bye!"

The line died. The heaters hissed. Wren put the phone face-down with exaggerated care, and when she looked up the collar was back up all the way, metaphorically speaking — armor re-seated, DA re-set, the moment gone around and climbing on the missed.

"Saturday after next," she said.

"Wren, what you were saying—"

"Was a hundred-foot conversation," she said quietly, "and we just lost the visual." She stood, squeezed his shoulder — one press, *readback correct*, achingly insufficient — and gathered her glass. "Get some sleep, Marsh. You flew the hell out of that ILS."

She stopped at the patio door, though. Half over her shoulder, in the doorway, where all her hardest transmissions came from:

"For the record. It's not a missed approach." A breath. "It's a hold. There's a difference, and the difference is the EFC."

Then she was gone, and Theo Marsh sat alone under the propane heaters in the moving fog with his heart at a hundred feet — level, lights in sight, descending no further — waiting, with everything he had, for red.

*End of Chapter Twelve.*
