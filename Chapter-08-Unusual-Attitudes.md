# Chapter Eight — Unusual Attitudes

*Theo*

The night it happened, they were flying home empty from Santa Barbara — the reposition leg, eleven p.m., no client, no cabin lights, just two pilots and a King Air climbing into a marine blackness so total that the coast simply ended and the universe began.

Theo was flying. Hand-flying, per the standing sentence in his own remarks column — *one approach per trip minimum, no flight director* — which Wren had quietly upgraded weeks ago to include night departures, because, as she put it, "the airplane you meet in trouble is the hand-flown one, so go meet it while trouble's off shift."

"Tell me what you're doing," she said now, from the left seat, monitoring. Not a challenge. The check-airman drill they'd worn smooth: narrate the invisible.

"Rotating, positive rate, gear up. And now the interesting part." The runway lights fell away behind them and the windshield went to pure nothing — no horizon, no stars below the cloud shelf, the black-hole departure over water where the planet gives you no further opinions. "Pitch to the attitude I know works — call it ten degrees nose-up — power set, and I *believe the attitude indicator*, because right now my inner ear is telling me a lie."

"Which lie?"

"The acceleration lie. Somatogravic. The airplane's accelerating, and the little accelerometers in my vestibular system can't tell acceleration from tilt — so my body swears we're pitching up too steep, *pushover* steep. Pilots have flown perfectly good airplanes into black water at night obeying that feeling — unload the 'climb' that was never too steep, descend into the sea at two hundred knots feeling fine. The defense is boring: instruments. The attitude says ten up, the VSI agrees, the altimeter's winding the right way, the airspeed's alive and sane. Four instruments outvote one inner ear."

"And the head game underneath it?"

"Attitude plus power equals performance. Control-performance method: set the attitude on the control instruments — attitude indicator, power gauges — then *check* the result on the performance instruments — altimeter, airspeed, VSI, heading. Don't chase performance with controls; set known pitch and power, trim, verify. The other school, primary-supporting — for any given flight condition, one instrument is primary for pitch, one for bank, one for power, the rest support. Straight-and-level: altimeter primary pitch, heading primary bank, airspeed primary power. Both schools land in the same church: *scan, don't stare*."

"Scan sins. Name yours."

"Fixation's mine," he admitted, leveling at one-five-thousand between layers, the last of the climb trim rolling off under his thumb. "Staring at one gauge like it owes me rent — for me it's the localizer inside the marker; I'll polish that needle while the airspeed wanders off to live its own life. Omission is skipping an instrument entirely — glass-panel pilots omit the standby until the day it's the only honest one left. And emphasis — over-weighting a favorite instrument even while technically scanning the rest. Every pilot's got a favorite. The scan is supposed to be a democracy, and it decays into a monarchy the minute you're tired or scared."

Wren made the small note-taking sound she made when banking material for later use. Outside, between a cloud floor lit faintly by nothing and a star ceiling that went up forever, the night was the kind of beautiful that instructors warn you about, because it's identical to the kind that kills you.

---

They talked, in the cruise, the way the last month had taught them to talk — shop on the surface, cargo underneath.

She asked about the instructing years, and he told her the true version: that he'd loved it more than he'd expected and less than he pretended, that teaching instruments in actual IMC with a student on the gauges was the most alive he'd ever felt in an airplane, and that every CFI in America is running a quiet experiment on themselves called *how long can you watch other people build the life you deferred*. That one of his students — Bergman, an ER doc, hands of stone, heart of absolute gold — had failed her first instrument checkride on the hold entry, called him sobbing from the FBO parking lot, retrained, passed, and now texted him a photo every single time she broke out at minimums with her kids in the back. *You built this*, the last one said, breaking out at four hundred and a mile, kids waving.

"That's the thing I'd have lost at the regional," he said. "Not the flying. The — evidence. That the hours meant something when they landed in somebody else."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "For the record," she said, "the evidence is sitting left seat of this airplane. Your remarks column retrained my whole cockpit." And then, before he could do anything reckless with that: "Ride report ahead. Sierra crossing. Look."

The forecast had said it plainly enough that morning — AIRMET Tango for moderate turbulence, mountain wave over the Siskiyous, winds aloft northwest at fifty knots across the ridgelines — and the PIREPs had spent the evening agreeing: light-to-moderate chop, occasional moderate, a Skywest crew calling it "sporty" at one-six-thousand, which in airline dialect meant *hold your coffee*.

"Autopilot's on for the crossing," Wren said. "Yoke's yours if it quits. Belts tight, loose stuff stowed—" she was doing it as she said it, kneeboards clipped, her water bottle wedged, the flight deck battened with freight-dog thoroughness. "And brief me the upset, Marsh. Right now, while it's academic."

"If we upset: I fly, you back me on the instruments and the callouts. Recognition first — *what's the attitude* — off the attitude indicator cross-checked against the standby, VSI, airspeed trend, because step one of every botched recovery is misreading which problem you have. Nose-low: airspeed increasing, VSI down, altitude unwinding — recover in order: *power off, level the wings, then pitch* — roll before pull, because pulling in a bank tightens the spiral and loads the wings, and the airframe's limits are not a rumor. Nose-high: airspeed decaying, VSI up — *power up, pitch down, level the wings* — get the nose coming down before it comes down on its own terms with a stall attached. Then, both cases: return to level, reset trim, re-engage the automation only when the airplane's earned it back. And the whole time, the body's vote doesn't count. It never counts at night in cloud. The panel is the electorate."

"Adequate," she said. "Now let's hope the atmosphere finds that as boring as I do."

The atmosphere did not.

---

The wave hit them twenty miles north of the ridge, and it wasn't the chop that started the trouble — the King Air shouldered through the first burbles like a horse through brush — it was the sequence. Later, debriefing it with the honesty that was the whole point of Clause Three, they'd reconstruct it link by link, the way the accident reports do, and Theo would feel the cold in his stomach at how ordinary every link had been.

Link one: moderate turbulence, sharper than forecast, the airplane bucking hard enough that the autopilot — faithful, overwhelmed — hit its limits and disconnected itself with its little electric cry. *Autopilot*, red on the annunciator, the yoke suddenly alive in nobody's hands, because—

Link two: Theo's hands weren't on it. He was head-down and right, retrieving Wren's chart tablet, which the first hard jolt had thrown off its clip into the footwell — a two-second favor, a nothing task, a distraction with perfect timing. And his head was *turned and tilted* when the airplane rolled, which meant—

Link three: the leans, and worse. Vestibular fluid, stirred by the head movement during roll — the Coriolis special, the one that turns a simple bank into a tumbling, corkscrewing conviction. His inner ear announced, with the sincerity of scripture, that they were rolling hard left and pitching up. His body was *certain*. He came upright with his hands full of tablet and his brain full of lies, and the panel said—

Nose-low. Right bank, thirty degrees and lazily increasing. Airspeed trending up through two-thirty. VSI down eight hundred and steepening. Altitude starting to unwind out of one-six-five. The wave had rolled them right and tucked the nose while his ear built a fiction in the opposite direction, and every muscle he owned wanted to roll *right* — into the real bank — to correct the imaginary one.

"Attitude," Wren said. One word. Voice like room temperature.

And two thousand hours of teaching it saved him from having to be brave. The procedure walked out of his mouth and took his hands with it: "Nose-low, right bank — recovering." *Power* — levers back, the speed trend arresting. *Roll* — wings level on the attitude indicator, cross-checked standby, the false tumble in his skull screaming through the whole turn and getting outvoted, four instruments to one ear, democracy holding. *Then pitch* — smooth, positive, one G and change, the VSI curling up through zero, altitude catching at one-six-one, airspeed bleeding back toward sane.

"Level," he said. Hands shaking now, which was allowed, now. "One-six-one. Climbing back to one-six-five. And my inner ear would like the record to show it dissents from everything the airplane just did."

"Noted and overruled." Wren's voice stayed level, but her hand landed briefly on his forearm — one press, there and gone, the touch equivalent of *readback correct*. "Good recovery. Textbook order. Now the part most crews skip: what do we fix?"

They flew out of the wave band and fixed it out loud, because that was the house religion. The tablet clip — inadequate, replace it, nothing gets retrieved in turbulence again; the airplane doesn't care whose chart is in the footwell. The autopilot disconnect — brief it as a *when*, not an *if*, in any moderate forecast: hands shadowing the yoke through the rough stuff, always, so the handoff has no gap. The head movement — the real lesson: in turbulence at night in IMC, the pilot flying's head stays *forward and level*; dropped items are the monitoring pilot's problem or nobody's. "And the biggest one," Theo said, "we just proved the brief works. We said the words while it was academic, and when it went live, the words flew the recovery. That's the whole argument for briefing things that will probably never happen. Probably has a failure rate."

From the left seat, quietly: "That one goes in my column too."

They flew a while in the smooth air beyond the wave, the flight deck settling, adrenaline draining down through the floor structure the way it does. And then Theo did the thing he did before every takeoff and, it turned out, after every survival: reached out, left hand, and tapped the glareshield twice.

He felt her see it. The question had been sitting in her since a sandwich shop in October — *trade secret; a gentleman never explains his preflight* — and he could feel it sitting there now, disciplined, unasked, at a careful constant distance like everything else she wanted.

"My mother," he said, to the windshield, before she could not-ask it again. "She knocked twice on the doorframe every morning when I left for school. For luck. Kept doing it when I left for college, for flying jobs — I'd be thirty years old backing out of the driveway and she'd be at the door, knock knock, like I was nine." The dark slid by, star-ceilinged, enormous. "She died six years ago. Spring. And the airplane's a doorframe, and I'm still leaving, so." He shrugged, one shoulder, the small one. "Now you know the trade secret. It's not a secret. It's just not for dinner parties."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone she used for entering things in logs: "It didn't go in the dossier."

"No."

"Good," she said — just that — and reached over and turned the flight deck lights down one notch against the night, which from Wren Hale was approximately a eulogy, and Theo flew the airplane home understanding that she had just filed his mother somewhere with a zipper on it, next to whatever she kept in her own.

---

Boeing Field at one in the morning was theirs alone — the tower closed, the field gone quiet, wind calm, the rain taking the night off. They put the airplane to bed in the hangar's yellow light, and the adrenaline, which had held professionally for ninety minutes, began looking around for somewhere to be filed.

Wren racked the last chock and ran Clause Three leaning against the wing, arms crossed, hair coming loose from the flight's long discipline.

"Debrief. Attitude flying, full recital, and then I'm buying you the vending machine's finest."

"Control-performance: set attitude and power, trim, verify on the performance gauges — don't chase. Primary-supporting: one instrument owns each axis per condition; straight-and-level is altimeter, heading, airspeed. Scan sins: fixation, omission, emphasis — the scan's a democracy that decays into monarchy under stress. The ear lies: somatogravic on dark accelerations, the leans and Coriolis when the head moves during roll — the body never gets a vote in IMC. Upsets: *recognize first*, off the full panel, because misreading the problem is step one of every bad recovery. Nose-low: power off, roll level, *then* pull — roll before pull, always. Nose-high: power up, pitch down, wings level. Re-trim, then re-earn the automation. Brief the upset while it's academic, hands shadow the yoke in the rough, and the pilot flying's head stays forward, because the airplane doesn't care whose tablet is on the floor." He exhaled. "And the standby attitude indicator is required equipment. No relief on that item. As somebody once told me."

"Adequate," said Wren Hale, "plus," and then she crossed the space between them, took a fistful of his collar with the same hand that had steadied his forearm at sixteen thousand feet, and kissed him.

It was not a stage kiss. There was no stage. There was a dark hangar and ticking engines and six weeks of load-bearing restraint, and Theo's entire instrument scan collapsed into a single gauge and he let it — fixation, unapologetic, monarchy declared — his hand finding the curve of her jaw, her breath unsteady against his mouth, the kiss going long and sure and then soft, and somewhere in it he understood that she'd flown this approach the way she flew everything: briefed privately for weeks, executed without warning, stable all the way down.

She pulled back three inches. Her eyes were enormous and appalled and shining.

"That," she said, not steadily, "was adrenaline. Post-event physiological discharge. Documented phenomenon."

"Uh-huh."

"It's not logged, Marsh. It doesn't count toward anything. It's — " she stepped back, re-armoring in real time, and he watched her do it with his heart going like a prop out of sync " — *not loggable*. There was no audience, so it wasn't the act, and it wasn't — it isn't — we have an *agreement*—"

"Wren." He said it gently, and she stopped. The hangar hummed. And Theo Marsh, who had recovered from one unusual attitude already that night by trusting the instruments over the feeling, looked at every instrument he had — her hand, still half-raised toward his collar; the kiss still warm on his mouth; six weeks of coasters and clauses and doorways — and made the call. "Okay. Not logged. Simulated instrument time — doesn't count without a safety pilot anyway." He picked up his flight bag, walked to the door, held it open for her, and let the smile go all the way up. "But for the record, Captain: I'd call your recovery technique *outstanding*, and my inner ear and I are no longer speaking."

"Marsh—"

"Nose-high," he said, tapping his own chest by way of diagnosis, stepping out into the dark. "Airspeed decaying, altitude increasing. It's fine. I know the procedure."

He heard her laugh — fourth documented occurrence, ragged and half against her will and worth the whole night — and behind them the hangar lights clicked off in sequence, and the two pilots of Meridian Three-Five walked to their separate cars through the quiet, both of them flying, by feel, in total darkness, an attitude neither of their panels had an instrument for.

*End of Chapter Eight.*
