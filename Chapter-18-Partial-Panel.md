# Chapter Eighteen — Partial Panel

*Theo*

The outbound leg to Sun Valley was the quietest ninety minutes of Theo's flying life. Checklists, callouts, altimeter settings — the airplane wanted for nothing. The crew flew like a machine, and like a machine, nothing in it was alive.

Dinner at the lodge was the cruelest kind: beautiful. The long table, the fire, the last-trip toasts — and the seating chart, which had spent all season conspiring to put them shoulder to shoulder, doing it one more time out of pure habit, so that Theo passed Wren the bread basket with the flawless courtesy of a stranger and their sleeves never once touched over ninety minutes of candlelight.

Chidinma found him at the coffee urn afterward. She didn't cross-examine — which from Chidinma was itself a verdict — just stood beside him fixing a cup she didn't want and said, quietly, eyes on the fire: "In my line of work, when a clean file suddenly reads dirty, the first thing we check isn't the file. It's whether somebody's been quoting it out of context." She clinked her cup against his, gently, like a gavel adjourning rather than ruling. "Goodnight, Captain-to-be. My aunt's not the only one in this family who did the diligence on you two."

Okafor herself watched them all evening — watched Wren excuse herself early, watched Theo study a glass of soda water like an approach plate — and said only one thing, at the foot of the great timber staircase, her hand light on his sleeve: "Theodore. When my husband and I fought — really fought, the twice in forty-one years it was real — the making up never once started with words. It started with weather." She patted the sleeve. "Sleep. Tomorrow has weather."

Tomorrow had weather.

They briefed it at six a.m. like the professionals they had never stopped being: the low was deepening and accelerating, the front due across the Cascades by midday, and the fight-or-wait math said *go early* — launch at eight, ride the high pressure's last hours east of the crest, land at Boeing Field before the system's leading edge. Freezing level at seven, tops in the mid-twenties ahead of the front, moderate rime in the climb; alternates fat — Yakima, Pasco, both forecast flyable all morning behind them like stepping stones. Legal, conservative, sound. Okafor boarded with her tea and her assistant and, for once, no questions at all.

For fifty minutes it went exactly as filed. They crossed the Sawtooths on top at flight level two-two-zero in hard sunlight, the storm a great white wall filling the western horizon, beautiful the way examiners are beautiful. They started down early for the Cascades crossing, into the gray, boots cycling, the airplane shouldering through building cloud at one-six-thousand — solid IMC, light rime, everything briefed, everything normal.

Then the right generator quit.

A flicker of annunciator — R GEN — the load meters swinging as the left generator picked up the whole airplane, and Theo, pilot flying, felt the non-event of it with almost relief: a checklist, a task, something for the machine-crew to do. "Right generator's off the bus," Wren said, flat calm, already into the QRH. "Load's on the left, within limits. Checklist: attempt one reset—"

The reset didn't hold. And ninety seconds later — because the fault, they would learn weeks afterward from Manny's teardown, had never been in the generator at all, but in a corroded bus-tie contactor that had chosen this morning above all mornings of its twenty-two years to start arcing — the *left* load meter began to dance, the smell of hot electrical crept into the flight deck like a rumor, and the airplane, in the orderly cascading way of electrical systems protecting themselves, started going dark.

"Left gen's tripping — bus tie fault—" Wren's hands moved through the drill, isolating, shedding. "Load shed. Battery's carrying essentials only."

And the panel — the beautiful, deep, glowing panel that both of them had flown all season like an extension of their own nervous systems — folded down to almost nothing. Both primary displays: black. The big attitude spheres, the tapes, the moving map with its magenta certainties: gone. What remained, glowing stubborn in the sudden gloom of a gray-lit flight deck at sixteen thousand feet in cloud: the standby instruments — the little mechanical attitude indicator, sober since October; a standby altimeter; a standby airspeed; the whiskey compass swimming overhead; and the number one GPS navigator, alive on the essential bus, its small course needle steady as a lighthouse keeper.

"Comm One's dead," Wren said, working the audio panel. "Comm Two—" a burst of static, a syllable of Seattle Center, gone again. "Comm Two's intermittent. Transponder's alive on essentials." A beat — one single beat, the length of a breath — and then the voice he'd have known in any dark, on any frequency, said: "Well, Marsh. Standby's required equipment. No relief on that item." And Theo Marsh, hands on the yoke of a nearly-dark airplane in solid IMC over the Cascade Range, felt the fear in the flight deck reorganize itself into work — because she was in it with him, all the way in, both radios of *them* suddenly, absurdly, fully operational.

"I have the airplane on standby instruments," he said. "Partial panel. My scan is attitude, altimeter, airspeed, compass, GPS track. You have the failure, the radios, and the plan."

"Copy. Plan first, then narrate everything." Her voice was the one from the wave, room temperature. "We're not crossing the crest into that front on a battery. Behind us is Pasco — flat land, fat runway, RNAV approaches, weather nine hundred broken last sequence, no terrain between here and there once we're south of the ridge line. We're diverting Pasco. I'm declaring." She spun the transponder: **7700**. "Emergency's squawked. And now the comm doctrine: Two's intermittent, so I transmit blind — position, altitude, intentions, every few minutes, whether or not anyone acknowledges — and I *listen*, because receivers die less than transmitters and half of lost comm is only half lost. If Center's reading our ident they'll say so." She keyed, spoke it clean and slow: callsign, souls, the failure, *diverting Pasco direct, descending one-two-thousand*. Static answered. Then, thin and chopped through the intermittent box: "*—ian Three-Five, Seattle Center, if you hear... squawk ident—*"

Wren pressed the ident button, and eight seconds later the broken voice came back with the entire national airspace system audibly reorganizing itself in the background: "*—ident observed... cleared direct Pasco... altitude your discretion, emergency... all aircraft this frequency, stand by—*"

"They've got us," Wren said quietly. "One-way street with an ident lane. It's enough. The system can see us, hear us sometimes, and predict us always — so we fly *predictable*." She looked over, and in the gray light, for the first time in eight days, her eyes were entirely hers again. "Your airplane, Marsh. Fly us there."

---

What Theo remembered afterward, in the way memory files a crucible, was not fear. It was the *narrowing* — the world contracting to a two-inch mechanical horizon and a whiskey compass and his own voice, narrating, because narration was the crew's shared scan now:

"Standby attitude wings-level, pitch two down, airspeed one-eighty, altimeter one-five-two and descending, GPS track one-niner-five." The composite scan, the partial-panel discipline, everything he'd taught in Skyhawks for three years and drilled in the sim with Voss — *there's only one student in the airplane when it's real, and it's the airplane.* "Turning: standard rate is timed turns now — no heading tape, so it's three degrees a second and a clock: heading change over three equals seconds in the bank. Coming left forty degrees for direct Pasco: that's thirteen seconds, rolling in — " the little horizon banked its sober fifteen degrees, Wren counting the sweep hand aloud like a heartbeat " — and rolling out. Compass check." The whiskey compass, that drunken oracle, swung and settled and lied in all the ways he'd taught a hundred students it would lie — *undershoot north, overshoot south* — and confirmed, in its own crooked dialect, the GPS track's sober testimony: on course. Away from the wall of weather. Toward the flat land.

The ice stayed light and the boots stayed faithful. Wren ran the battery like a fuel ledger — shedding, timing, calling the minutes; the standby bus was rated for thirty and she planned for twenty — transmitted blind on schedule, pressed ident on request, briefed Okafor twice in plain warm sentences ("an electrical failure, ma'am; the airplane flies perfectly without the fancy screens; we have a sunny-side airport twenty minutes ahead and an entire government watching us walk there"), and somewhere over the Yakima valley, descending through eleven thousand with the cloud thinning to layers, she took the approach plate and built the arrival out loud:

"RNAV runway three-zero-right at Pasco, LNAV minimums — the navigator's alive and lateral guidance is honest, no vertical, so it's a non-precision approach on standby instruments: MDA niner-two-zero, that's about five hundred above the field, weather's nine hundred broken so we should break out comfortably above mins. Marsh — your CDFA math, one more time, for the airplane."

"Descent from the final fix: three miles a minute at one-forty groundspeed — call it six-fifty a minute for the three-degree profile, level at MDA if we're still blind, VDP's at one-point-seven off the threshold, and the miss—" his mouth almost smiled around it, even then "—the miss is briefed: climbing straight ahead to two thousand, then as published, and we will not be flying it, because the weather's better than the story requires."

"Confirmed on all of it." A crackle of Center, half a sentence — *"...Pasco tower's been advised... light the field up for you..."* — and Wren pressed ident twice, which had become, by unspoken treaty on a broken frequency, *thank you.*

They came down the wire of their own arithmetic in smooth gray silence, the standby altimeter unwinding, Wren calling the altitudes — "one thousand above... five hundred above..." — and at nine hundred and change the cloud let go of them the way it had at Medford, all at once, reluctantly, and there it was: the Columbia braided silver across brown winter flatland, and the runway, lit end to end, every approach light burning, the airport environment risen up out of the gray exactly where the promise had said it would be.

"Runway in sight," Theo said, and heard his own voice crack on it, and did not care.

"Continue," said Wren Hale, steady, warm, entirely unbroken. "You're stable. Bring us home."

The mains touched the numbers of runway three-zero-right at Pasco eleven days after a ballroom, one bus-tie contactor, and thirty-one minutes of the best instrument flying of either of their lives. The rollout was long and straight. The fire trucks, bless them, had nothing to do but wave.

---

The ramp at Pasco was cold and enormous under a torn gray sky, and the aftermath ran its course in the proper order, because they were who they were: passengers first — Okafor descending the airstair unhurried, magnificent, pausing only to take each of their hands in both of hers, saying nothing at all in a way that said everything; then the phone calls — the tower, Center's supervisor for the report, Griff (audibly aging), Manny (audibly delighted; contactors were his favorite villains); then the squawk log, which Wren filled out at the wing in longhand while the wind snapped at the pages: right generator, bus-tie fault, load shed, displays lost, comm one lost, comm two intermittent — the whole failure documented in her steady captain's hand, ninety-one one-eighty-seven satisfied twice over, because narration is free and the next crew's life is not.

And then there was nothing left to do. The airplane was chocked and dark, the client was warming in the FBO, the paperwork was done, and the two of them stood alone under the wing in the wind, the way they'd stood under wings all season, with eleven days of silence and thirty-one minutes of perfect partnership lying between them on the ramp.

Wren reached into the left chest pocket — the zipper one, the archive — and held out an envelope with his name on it.

"Blind transmission," she said. "I've been carrying it on schedule, every few minutes, for a week." Her voice, which had not wavered at sixteen thousand feet on a battery, wavered now. "In case the receiver ever came back up."

He read it right there, in the wind, in his winter jacket on a ramp in Pasco — three pages, longhand, structured like a safety report because of course it was, chronology, no varnish — and the sentence from the library arrived at last complete, with its middle and its end: *...and you're going to keep it to yourself, because in about an hour I'm done pretending; because it stopped being an act in a hangar in October; because I'm telling Adaeze Okafor the whole truth myself* — and the screenshot, timestamped Tuesday, *declined with thanks* — and the last paragraph, the one about promises and predictability and keeping the lights on, and the sign-off that undid him entirely: *receivers get checked before transmitters, Marsh. This is me, checking yours.*

Theo folded the letter with the care owed to a load-bearing document, and looked up, and found her watching him the way she'd watched the standby horizon: like the last honest instrument in a dark airplane.

"I fixated," he said. The wind took it; he said it louder, because confessions transmitted blind deserve full volume. "One instrument, hot and bright, and I abandoned the whole panel. Everything I teach — cross-check, verify, *ask when in doubt* — I did none of it. I heard one sentence with the middle torn out and I flew a perfectly good life into the ground on it. Ninety-one one-twenty-three, Wren — *when in doubt, immediately ask* — and I didn't ask. I invoked your own mercy clause and made you eat it, and you—" his voice went; he flew through it "—you kept the lights on anyway. You flew predictable. You stayed findable. For eleven days."

"Ninety-one one-eighty-five," Wren said, unsteadily. "It's a good procedure."

"It's the best procedure." He took a step. "Amendment Four. Proposed here on the ramp at Pasco, for immediate ratification: Clause Four survives — anybody can always leave — but no one *ever* goes missed on a misheard transmission again without flying to where the other one is parked and saying, out loud, 'confirm.' One word. Costs thirty seconds."

"Seconded," she said. "Ratified. Signed—"

But the signing was conducted without a pen, in the cold, under the wing of a dark and faithful airplane — Wren's fists in his jacket the way they'd been in a hangar in October, his hands in her wind-wrecked hair, the kiss long and fierce and salted with more weather than either of them would ever admit to — and it went on until, from the FBO's doorway, carried beautifully across the empty ramp on the winter wind, came the voice of Adaeze Okafor, empress of everything, addressed ostensibly to her assistant:

"You see? Weather. It *always* starts with weather. Now come inside, aviators — the tea is hot, the trucks are bored, and one of you still owes me the end of a story."

*End of Chapter Eighteen.*
