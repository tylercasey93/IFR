# Chapter Seven — Holding Pattern

*Wren*

Wren knew the day was going to get complicated when Portland Approach said the word "expect," because in her experience the word "expect" was how the National Airspace System broke bad news gently.

They were thirty miles out, descending through a stratus deck the color of wet aluminum, with Adaeze Okafor and her goddaughter Chidinma — visiting from London, twenty-six, observant in a way that ran in the family — settled in the back with tea and intentions. The ATIS at Portland had been unremarkable: eight hundred overcast, three miles in rain, approaches to runway one-zero-right in use. A workmanlike IFR morning.

Then: "Meridian Three-Five, Portland Approach. Portland's just closed the south runway — disabled aircraft, blown tires on rollout, equipment's rolling. Expect holding at BATTLE GROUND. Stand by for holding instructions."

"Here we go," Theo said, pulling up the chart, and there was no mistaking the note in his voice for anything but appetite. The man collected holds the way other pilots collected airports.

"Meridian Three-Five, hold *north* of the Battle Ground VOR on the *three-four-eight radial*, *five mile legs*, *left turns*. Maintain six thousand. Expect further clearance zero-one-one-five Zulu. Time now, zero-zero-four-two."

Wren flew while Theo read it back word for word, and then she said, because the ritual demanded it: "Grade the clearance. What did they give us, and what would we have been owed if they'd gone quiet?"

"Everything's there. Direction from the fix — north. The fix — Battle Ground. The radial to hold on — three-four-eight. Leg length — five miles, so we're timing with distance, not the clock. Turns — left, which makes it *nonstandard*, because standard is right turns and they only have to say it when it isn't. Altitude. And the crown jewel: the EFC. Expect further clearance zero-one-one-five — thirty-three minutes of our lives. The EFC isn't a courtesy, it's the lost-comm contract: radios die in the hold, that's the time we're allowed to leave it. No EFC, we ask for one. Always. And if they'd cleared us to a fix with no holding instructions at all and we got there uncleared beyond it — we hold anyway, standard pattern, on our inbound course, and start asking pointed questions."

"And the speed homework, before we get there."

"Below six thousand: two hundred knots indicated, max, in the hold. Six thousand one to fourteen thousand: two-thirty. Above fourteen: two-sixty-five. We're at six thousand — two hundred's our ceiling, and the technique is to slow *before* the fix — the book says begin the slowdown within three minutes of arrival — because crossing the fix at cruise speed and then decelerating in the turn is how you fly out of protected airspace with style. Also: fuel. Slowing early is free endurance. Every knot we don't fly in a hold is money."

Wren pulled the power back, watched the trend arrows settle. "Entry. Sell it to me."

Theo drew it in the air with a flat hand, instructor liturgy. "Holding north of the fix on the three-four-eight radial, left turns. Our inbound course to the fix in the pattern is one-six-eight. We're arriving from the southwest heading zero-six-zero-ish. So — the sectors. Picture the holding course through the fix, and lay the seventy-degree line across it. Arrivals from the side that contains the pattern get the **direct** entry — cross the fix, turn to the outbound. That's about half the compass, and when in doubt, direct is almost always defensible. Arrivals from the far side, on the holding-course side of the seventy line — **parallel**: cross the fix, fly the course *outbound* on the non-holding side, then turn back in. And the little seventy-degree slice on the other side — **teardrop**: cross the fix, cut thirty degrees into the protected side, fly a minute, turn to intercept inbound." He checked the drawn picture against the moving map, where the hold had bloomed as a neat left-handed racetrack. "Us, from here: teardrop. And note the machine agrees, which is nice, because the machine and I have had disagreements about entries that the machine won."

"The entries are recommendations," Wren said. "The protected airspace is not. Fly a recognizable entry, stay on the protected side, and nobody at the FAA loses sleep."

"You take all the poetry out of it."

"There's poetry in protected airspace, Marsh. It's just load-bearing poetry."

They crossed Battle Ground at two hundred slowing to one-seventy, and Wren flew the teardrop — thirty degrees off the outbound, the wet aluminum world wheeling gently, five DME miles out, left turn, inbound to intercept the one-six-eight course to the fix — and as the needle centered she keyed up: "Portland Approach, Meridian Three-Five, entering the hold at Battle Ground, six thousand, time zero-zero-five-one."

"Report it entering and report it leaving," she said, off-frequency. "Time and altitude. That's the mandatory-report list doing its job — non-radar or radar, we tell them when we arrive at a fix we've been cleared to hold at, and when we depart it. The system's picture of us is only as good as our narration."

"You sound like me."

"Someone in this crew has to."

---

The thing nobody tells you about holding is that it's domestic. Once the entry's flown and the pattern's established, a hold is just housekeeping in the sky — five miles out, left turn, wind correction, five miles back, left turn, report the fuel, watch the clock. It has the rhythm of folding laundry. It leaves, and this was the day's real hazard, entirely too much room for conversation.

The wind at six thousand was out of the southwest at thirty, quartering across the pattern, and Theo — pilot monitoring, fuel sheet already ruled into columns — talked the correction like a man setting a table. "Inbound leg I needed eight degrees left to hold the course. So outbound I'll carry roughly *triple* the correction the other way — call it twenty-plus degrees — because the outbound leg has no course guidance to lean on and both turns drift with the wind. Triple the inbound correction, applied opposite, and you come out of the far turn somewhere near the inbound course instead of in the next county. With five-mile DME legs we've got it easier than the timed folks — the box measures the legs for us — but the wind triangle doesn't care what's measuring."

"Fuel."

"Doing it now. EFC's zero-one-one-five. Holding burn at this weight, slowed up — call it six hundred pounds an hour combined. From now to EFC: three-thirty-ish. We landed the alternate question already — Salem's severe clear behind the front — so the math is: fuel to hold till EFC, shoot the approach when the runway opens, miss it once if we have to, *then* Salem, then forty-five minutes. We're fat. We're fat by an hour. Which I'm telling you not because you didn't know—" he clipped the sheet where she could see it "—but because a fuel state everyone can see is a fuel state nobody argues about later."

"And the words. If we weren't fat."

"Two rungs. **Minimum fuel** — advisory, not an emergency: it tells ATC we can't accept *undue delay*. No priority, no paperwork, just a flag on the strip that says don't send this one around the scenic route. And if it gets truly quiet in the tanks: **emergency fuel** — declare it, say the word, take the priority, fly the airplane, and let the paperwork find us alive. The mistake with both is the same mistake: pride. People treat the vocabulary like an admission of guilt and sit silent while their options burn." He said it lightly. He said everything lightly. But the pen was ruler-straight under the numbers, and Wren flew her left-handed laundry pattern and thought, not for the first time, that Theo Marsh's lightness was a finish coat over structure most people never checked for.

The cabin speaker chimed. Okafor. Of course.

"Captain. Chidinma wants to know why we keep turning in circles, and I have told her it is because the sky has a waiting room, and she says I am being poetic and demands the real answer. Settle a family dispute."

Wren keyed the mic. "Ma'am, your goddaughter is owed the truth: you're both right. Portland's runway is briefly closed while they tow a flat tire off it. When that happens, arrivals don't scatter — the system stacks us in assigned patterns at assigned altitudes, each airplane in its own protected box of air, and we circle at reduced speed to save fuel until they call our number. We have a promised time — quarter past the hour — and enough fuel to wait well past it, plus a sunny airport in our back pocket if Portland takes up permanent residence in our lives. It's the most organized boredom in aviation."

There was a brief off-mic consultation in the cabin, the specific murmur of two generations of the same formidable family reaching consensus.

"Chidinma says," Okafor reported, "that circling something at a careful distance while you decide whether to land is the most relatable thing an airplane has ever done. And *I* said—" the smile came right through the speaker "—that some crews do it for months, dear."

The transmission ended. The flight deck of Meridian Three-Five was very quiet, at six thousand feet, in a left-hand pattern, at a careful distance from everything.

Theo cleared his throat. "So, the wind correction—"

"The wind correction's fine, Marsh."

"The wind correction is *excellent*," he agreed, and wisely opened the approach chart.

---

Portland reopened the runway at zero-one-zero-six — nine minutes inside the EFC, because the system loves nothing more than making its promises look easy — and Approach's voice came back with the small warmth controllers get when a plan concludes: "Meridian Three-Five, runway's open. Fly heading one-four-zero, vectors ILS one-zero-right, cleared to depart the hold. Say fuel state."

"One-four-zero heading, cleared to depart the hold, fuel state four point one hours, Meridian Three-Five. And we're leaving Battle Ground at six thousand, time zero-one-zero-seven."

Theo raised an eyebrow at the last part.

"Report leaving," Wren said. "I don't care that he's watching us on radar. Narration is free and assumptions are not."

They broke out at seven hundred over the river, the city smeared silver-gray and beautiful under the deck, and Wren flew the kind of landing that made no anecdotes, and that, she'd have said, should have been the end of the day's turbulence.

Except that Adaeze Okafor, descending the airstair into the drizzle with her goddaughter's arm in hers, paused at the bottom, turned, and said: "Eight o'clock, Departure. The little place on Alberta Street — my assistant booked it under Marsh. The reservation is for two." She held up a hand against the incoming objection with the serenity of a woman who had chaired hostile boards. "You held for me for half an hour and never once made it feel like waiting. A couple who can wait *well* deserves a table. Chidinma and I are having dinner with dull investors, and you two are not invited. Go."

---

The little place on Alberta Street had eleven tables and no audience.

That was the problem, Wren thought, watching Theo argue cheerfully with the waiter about which Willamette pinot was "structurally sound." Clause Two was written for audiences. There was a client-shaped reason for the reservation, sure — the act had *booked* the table — but the act had then gone off to eat with investors, leaving two off-duty pilots at a two-top with rain on the window and no operational requirement to be anything at all.

They lasted through the appetizers on shop talk. It was the pinot that did the entries in.

"Question," Theo said. "Professional curiosity. Flight-standards inquiry." He turned his glass a quarter-turn, his tell, trim check before a transmission that mattered. "At what point does the crew acknowledge that we've been given holding instructions?"

"Careful, Marsh."

"I'm always careful. I'm *famously* careful. Direction from the fix, a radial, a leg length, turns—" he counted them off, gently, on the tablecloth between them "—we've got the full clearance, Wren. We've had it for weeks. We're established. Protected airspace, wind corrected, fuel logged. Textbook hold." He looked up, and the lightness was all the way off, structure showing plain. "The one thing nobody's issued is the EFC."

The restaurant did restaurant things around them: cutlery, weather on glass, other people's easy evenings.

"Expect further clearance," Wren said slowly, "is issued by the controlling authority. And the controlling authority in this arrangement wrote herself a clause. Number Four. Either party can call the miss, any time, no questions — I wrote that clause because holds without exits are how people get hurt, Theo. I've flown that pattern. I held for someone once until the tanks were dry, and the EFC never came, and I told myself the whole time that patience was airmanship." She set her glass down. "It wasn't. It was a fuel emergency I was too proud to declare."

"Cascade," he said quietly. Not a question anymore.

"Cascade." The name went down easier with pinot than it ever had with coffee. "So you should know what you're circling, before you burn another month of gas on me. The last man in my flight deck filed us as a crew and flew us as a solo. When it went down, he — took the vectors and left me in the hold." She heard her own voice do the thing, the flat Alaska thing, and let him hear it too, which was new. "I'm not her anymore. But the paperwork's still in the system somewhere. Ask your questions."

Theo was silent for a moment. Then he did the single most Theo Marsh thing she had ever witnessed: he took out his pen, took the paper coaster from under his glass, and wrote on it, in the neat remarks-column handwriting, three words. He turned it around and slid it across the tablecloth.

*FUEL STATE: FAT.*

"For the record," he said. "Filed and logged. I'm not circling on fumes, Wren. I'm not close. You want to know my state before you issue anything — that's it, in writing, dated." He left the pen on the coaster. "And there's no rush on the EFC. That's the entire point of doing the fuel planning honestly. The airplane that waits well—" the smile came up, but the eyes stayed level "—gets to wait as long as the mission takes."

Wren looked at the coaster for a long time.

Then she picked it up, folded it once, and put it in the left chest pocket — the zipper one, the one with the agreement, the one with exactly two documents in it now — and if her hand stayed on the zipper a beat longer than the hardware required, the only witness was a waiter who'd already decided they were married.

"Debrief," she said, finally, because the ritual was the only container that would hold. "Holding. Go."

"Clearance needs direction, fix, radial, leg length if DME, turns if nonstandard, altitude, EFC — and no EFC means ask, because it's the lost-comm exit. Speeds: two hundred to six thousand, two-thirty to fourteen, two-sixty-five above; slow within three minutes of the fix. Entries by the seventy-degree line — direct, parallel, teardrop — recommendations in service of the only law, which is protected airspace. Triple the inbound drift correction, opposite, outbound. Report entering, report leaving, time and altitude — narration is free. Fuel: log it where everyone can see it; *minimum fuel* means no undue delay, no priority; *emergency fuel* means say the word and live. And—" he nodded at her jacket pocket, once, "—know your fuel state before you enter. State it plainly to the crew. Pride is the only thing on this airplane with no legitimate function."

"Adequate," Wren said, and under the table, eleven tables from anywhere, in the most organized boredom of her entire life, she let her ankle rest against his and did not move it for the rest of the evening.

*End of Chapter Seven.*
