# Chapter Seventeen — Lost Comms

*Wren*

The week after the gala, Meridian Three-Five flew four legs, and its crew performed them with a precision that would have made any check airman weep.

That was the terrible discovery of the silence: the flying didn't degrade. The briefings were complete. The callouts came on time. Checklists ran like clockwork; the airplane never felt a thing. Two people who had built a shared language over a whole season now operated it stripped to the load-bearing minimum — *set, checked; gear down, three green; minimums, runway in sight* — and the passengers in the back experienced only the same smooth competence as ever, because both pilots were too professional to let the wreckage into the flight deck.

Sterile cockpit, Wren thought, watching Theo run a descent checklist with his instructor's cadence sanded down to bare procedure. They'd finally achieved perfect sterile cockpit. All it had cost was everything.

Clause Four hung between the seats where the jokes used to live. *No questions. Not that day, not ever.* She'd written it herself, in dry-erase-scented October, as a mercy — an exit anybody could take without being interrogated on the way out — and she had not once imagined being on this side of it: watching him two feet away, knowing to the syllable what he'd misheard, and being procedurally forbidden from demanding he sit still while she fixed it. She'd tried transmitting anyway, the first morning — "Theo, what you heard in the library—" — and he'd looked at her with the ruined politeness of a man holding an airplane together and said, "Captain, I'd fly better if we didn't," and *that*, the Captain, had ended the attempt like a stuck mic going quiet.

Both radios. All frequencies. She knew this failure. She'd studied it her whole adult life.

So — because she was Wren Hale, and because the only thing she'd ever known to do with a catastrophe was procedure — she flew the procedure.

---

Monday, she told Adaeze Okafor the truth.

She did it alone, in the end — the plan had been *together*, but the together had gone missed — in Okafor's suite at the Fairmont, tea steaming between them, and she laid it out the way she'd have briefed an incident to the FAA: chronology, no varnish, her own hand in it plain. The lobby. Griff's four-second invention. The contract, the act, the season of performances. And then — because the truth was the truth — the rest of it: the hangar, Pendleton, a legal pad with amendments, the whole absurd catastrophe of it turning real underneath the costume.

"And Saturday night," Okafor said, when she'd finished. The old woman had listened without a single interruption, hands folded, eyes unreadable as high cirrus. "Your first officer left early. Unwell."

"He overheard me describing the beginning," Wren said, "without the middle or the end. And the rules of our — arrangement — let him leave without asking questions, so he left. That part isn't your concern, ma'am. It's mine. What's your concern is that you renewed a contract and toasted a couple on the strength of a story that started as a lie, and you deserved to hear that from me, standing up, before you heard it any other way. If it changes your relationship with Meridian, that's — arithmetic we'll accept. It was never yours to absorb."

Adaeze Okafor looked at her for a long, long moment over the tea.

"Captain," she said at last, "I have buried a husband, built forty hotels, and been lied to by professionals on four continents. You will forgive me if I make no decisions before I've finished my tea." She lifted the cup; the eyes over its rim were bright and ancient and utterly unreadable. "I will say only this. In my experience, the stories that matter are rarely about how a thing began." She set the cup down with a click soft as a needle centering. "Thank you for the truth. It was — instrument-rated of you. Now go fix your radios."

---

*Go fix your radios.* As if she hadn't been trying. As if the failure weren't total, both transmitters, all frequencies — his phone rolling to voicemail, her one text (*whenever you're ready to hear the rest of that sentence, I'm on frequency*) delivered and unanswered into the December dark.

It was Priya who found the black box. Wednesday, arms folded in the dispatch doorway: "So the catering staff have a group chat, and a certain fractional captain got loud at the after-party. Bragging that the famous Meridian romance was — quote — a stage act, straight from the captain's mouth, and that Hale's coming to Pacific Corporate within the quarter." Priya's jaw was tight. "That's what Theo heard, Wren. Some version of that, live, from the hallway. Jax got the quote and Theo got the context-free feed."

"I know exactly what he heard." Wren was staring at the crew schedule, at OKAFOR — FINAL WINTER TRIP written across Friday in Griff's dying marker. "I've reconstructed the CVR about four hundred times."

"Then *tell him*—"

"He won't take the transmission, Priya. He asked me — as crew — to stop trying, and if I push harder I'm the thing Clause Four was written to prevent: someone who makes somebody stay in the room and defend their exit." She heard her own voice do the flat thing and was too tired to fix it. "You know what the actual procedure is? For lost comms? Everyone thinks it's about getting the radios back. It isn't. Ninety-one one-eighty-five doesn't say one word about fixing the radio. It says: *keep flying, predictably, exactly where you promised you'd be* — so that the system, which can still *see* you even though it can't *hear* you, can protect you anyway. The whole rule is a promise you make in advance: if we ever go silent, fly where I said I'd fly. Be findable." She looked up. "So that's what I'm doing. I show up. I brief. I fly the schedule as published. As assigned, as expected, as filed. And I stay findable, on every frequency, for whenever he starts receiving again."

Priya was quiet a second. "That's either the healthiest or the most aviation-brain-poisoned coping strategy I've ever heard."

"It can be both," said Wren, and went back to writing the letter.

Sam called her Thursday night.

She almost didn't answer — the caller ID said MARSH, S. and her whole chest did something unprofessional — but you answer for family, even borrowed family, even now, so she picked up and said, "Sam," and heard the good mug clink down on the kitchen table two towns away.

"I want it on the record," Sam Marsh said, without preamble, "that I am not calling to meddle. Ellen did the meddling in this family. I'm calling about the Bonanza." A pause precisely long enough to be a lie acknowledging itself. "The right tank's reading two gallons different from the dip. Probably the sender. Manny's booked out. You know senders."

"I know senders." She closed her eyes. "Sam."

"He showed up at my door Saturday night in the rain," Sam said, abandoning the sender without ceremony, "quoting furniture at me — *Clause Four*, whatever that is — and he's been flying your schedule all week and sleeping in his old room like it's 2012, and he won't say one word about it, which — Wren, I want you to understand — is not a Marsh trait. Marshes narrate. His mother narrated her own surgery. When this one goes quiet, it's not because there's nothing in the queue. It's because he's afraid of what transmits." The mug again, a man buying himself a second. "I'm not asking what happened. That's yours and his. I'm telling you the half I'm qualified on: whatever he thinks he heard, he's wrong about it — I raised him, I know his failure modes; the boy fixates like his instructor told him never to — and *also* he'd rather sit wrong in the dark than make you explain yourself, because some genius taught him that making people explain themselves is a sin." A breath. "So. Two aircraft, both squawking standby, each waiting for the other's transmitter. I've watched this weather before, kid. It doesn't clear by itself."

Wren sat at her kitchen table with the letter half-written in front of her, three pages of longhand chronology, and the whole ridiculous grief of the week sitting on her chest like ice on a wing.

"I'm working the procedure, Sam," she said, finally. "Findable and predictable. It's what I've got."

"I know," Sam said, gentler. "For what it's worth — when *he* was silent, all week, I didn't push either. I just left the porch light on and made bad coffee where he could smell it." The smile came right through the phone, lopsided and kind. "You two were built in the same factory; don't think I haven't noticed. Fly the procedure, Captain. And Wren — when the frequency opens up? Say the important part first. Don't lead with the chronology. I know you've got a chronology."

She looked at the three pages of chronology.

"Fix your fuel sender, Sam," she said, unsteadily, and he laughed his lopsided laugh and let her go.

She kept the chronology — she was who she was — but that night she rewrote the last paragraph, the one that mattered, and moved one sentence from the bottom of page three up into it, where it could be found first if the receiver only had a moment. *Receivers get checked before transmitters, Marsh. This is me, checking yours.*

The letter was the part the procedure didn't cover, so she'd invented it: three pages, longhand, structured — God help her, she knew it, she'd embraced it — like a NASA safety report. *Nature of event: communications failure, two crew. Chronology follows.* The library sentence, completed this time, all the way to the end that Jax had never let her reach. The offer, and the reply she'd finally sent — *declined with thanks* — screenshot attached, timestamped Tuesday. The amendments, quoted from the legal pad. And the last paragraph, which took her until two in the morning:

*Per the procedure, when communications are lost, each pilot flies to the clearance limit and begins the approach as close as possible to the time we agreed. You are the one who taught me the reason the rule works: because both airplanes trust the other one to be exactly where the promise said. So: I'll be at the airplane Friday, on the trip we're both assigned, at the time we filed, flying predictable and findable, the way you'd expect. No questions — the clause stands, it will always stand. But receivers get checked before transmitters, Marsh. You taught me that too. This is me, checking yours. — W.*

She sealed it, wrote his name on it, and did not mail it. Some transmissions you hold for when the receiver's in range.

---

Voss, of course, chose that week for her recurrent instrument check, because the universe builds its scenarios with a craftsman's cruelty.

The oral, in the Tukwila briefing room, arrived at its dessert with Voss's usual flat relish: "Lost comms. You're IFR in the soup. The radios die. Take me through it, Hale. All of it."

And Wren Hale, running on four nights of bad sleep and a sealed letter in her flight bag, recited the procedure for surviving exactly what she was living.

"First — it's almost never both radios. So troubleshoot like you believe that: volume, squelch, headset plugs, swap headsets, try the other box, go back to the *last* frequency that worked, try the next facility up the road, listen over the nav receivers' audio — controllers can talk through VOR voice channels — and if all else fails, a cell phone is a legal, honorable instrument of aviation. Squawk seven-six-zero-zero so the system knows. And check whether you're transmitting blind but *receiving* — or receiving nothing but they can hear you: acknowledge instructions with the ident button if they ask. Half the 'lost comm' events in history were one bad jack and a spiral of assumptions."

"It's really dead. Both boxes. What governs?"

"Ninety-one one-eighty-five, and the first gate is the weather. If I'm in VFR conditions, or I encounter them after the failure — I *stay* VFR and land as soon as *practicable*. Not as soon as possible — practicable: a real airport, a sensible landing, then a phone call to close out. The regulation does not want a silent airplane blundering around the IFR system one minute longer than necessary."

"You're in solid IMC. Route."

"The memory aid is AVEF, in order. *Assigned* — the route in my last clearance. If I was being *vectored* — direct from the point of failure to the fix, route, or airway the vector was taking me to. Absent those, whatever ATC told me to *expect* in a further clearance. And absent all of it — what I *filed*. The order matters because it's the order of the system's knowledge of me: fly the newest promise the controller and I both hold, so their picture and my airplane agree."

"Altitude."

"Highest of three, evaluated *segment by segment*: the minimum IFR altitude for the segment I'm on — MEA; the altitude ATC told me to *expect*; or my last *assigned*. Highest-of-three, recomputed at every leg, because the MEA climbs over the mountains whether or not anyone can hear me, and the rule refuses to let obedience fly me into a ridge. Terrain outranks the last thing a human said."

"When do you begin the approach?"

Wren looked at the table for half a second. The letter was in the bag at her feet.

"If I've been given an expect-further-clearance time — I leave the holding fix at the EFC. Otherwise, I proceed to the clearance limit, and I begin the descent and approach *as close as possible to the estimated time of arrival we filed and amended*. Not early — the airspace ahead of my ETA belongs to other airplanes the system is still sequencing. Not late — because the whole apparatus, the whole point of every one of these rules, is that both sides can *predict each other*. The airplane flies where it promised. The system protects the promise. Everyone converges at the time and place they agreed when they could still speak — and that's how two parties who cannot exchange a single word find each other anyway. Predictability is the last frequency to fail."

The briefing room was quiet.

"Correct," said Dale Voss. He closed the folder, clicked the pen, and then — first time in the years she'd known him — set it down and took off his glasses. "Off the record, Hale. Fifty-one years in this industry. I've seen every model of radio die every way a radio can. Never once saw one heal itself from being left alone in the dark." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Rides are Friday. Whatever's failed on your flight deck — and I sat that jumpseat, so don't insult the old man — the procedure you just recited has one more step you didn't say. When the silent airplane finally gets to where it's expected, and starts the approach — somebody at the airport has to have kept the lights on."

He put his glasses back on. "Pass. Ride's a formality; fly like you brief. Get out of my classroom."

---

Friday's trip sheet posted Thursday at four: OKAFOR — FINAL WINTER TRIP. Boeing Field to Sun Valley, dinner at the lodge, return Saturday. Crew: **Hale / Marsh** — because the contract named them both, because the client had specifically confirmed them both, because Adaeze Okafor, empress of a species of chess nobody else could see the board of, had booked her last flight of the season into the teeth of a forecast that the Aviation Weather Handbook would have used as a centerfold: a deepening low sliding down out of the Gulf of Alaska, moisture and mountains and a freezing level with opinions, arriving Saturday over the Cascades like an examiner.

Wren stood at the dispatch desk a long time, looking at the two names side by side on the sheet, the way they'd been side by side all season, the way they were currently not.

Then she went home, packed her overnight bag with the discipline of ten thousand overnights, and put the sealed letter in the flight jacket's left chest pocket — the zipper one, the archive, where the operating agreement lived, and the coaster with FUEL STATE: FAT, and every other load-bearing document of the best year of her life.

As assigned. As expected. As filed.

She'd be at the airplane at seven. Findable. On frequency.

And somewhere across the dark city, she had to believe it the way you believe an altimeter, the other half of her crew was packing a bag too — because whatever else had failed between them, no radio failure in history had ever once kept Theo Marsh from showing up for the airplane.

The approach would begin, as close as possible, at the time they'd filed.

*End of Chapter Seventeen.*
