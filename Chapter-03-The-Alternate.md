# Chapter Three — The Alternate

*Wren*

Wren Hale planned flights at her kitchen table because the table was big and the apartment was small and there was nobody to ask why the charts had colonized the place settings. Tuesday night, the night before the San Francisco leg, the spread looked like this: laptop with the filed route, iPad with the low en route chart, a legal pad of fuel math in her own shorthand, and a mug of coffee gone cold at the exact rate of the evening.

On the shelf behind her, in a shoebox she would have denied owning under oath, lived the other charts. Expired ones. The sectional from her first solo, creased where her instructor had folded it. The Alaska en route from her first week at the freight outfit, still smelling faintly of the Chieftain — oil and cold and fish, somehow, always fish. The approach plate from her first ILS to actual minimums, Ketchikan, two hundred and a half, hands shaking on the rollout and nineteen boxes of salmon in the back none the wiser.

Nobody knew about the box. The box was not information anyone had demonstrated a need to know.

Her phone buzzed. Priya: *SFO tmrw. TAF just updated. You'll want to look.*

Wren looked. Then she said a word her mother wouldn't have liked, pulled the legal pad closer, and started building the alternate.

---

The morning briefing room smelled like dry-erase and Theo's terrible gas-station coffee, which he drank with an optimism that offended her. He had the route up on the monitor when she walked in — she was early; he'd been earlier, which she had begun cataloging as a pattern rather than a coincidence.

"Morning, honey," he said, without looking up, in the flat tone they'd agreed made the endearments legal — Clause Two allowed rehearsal in private, and they'd discovered the words only stayed safe if you said them like checklist items. "Your destination's trending toward garbage."

"Saw it last night. Walk me through the route first. You filed?"

"Filed at oh-five-thirty. Boeing Field to San Francisco: radar vectors SUMMA, then the airway structure down — Victor airways over the Cascades foothills till we pick up the high structure, or honestly, tower en route would've worked as far as Portland if we were staying low. I filed us for one-six-thousand southbound."

"Why one-six?"

"Because ninety-one one-seventy-nine says direction decides. Magnetic course zero through one-seven-nine: odd thousands. One-eighty through three-five-nine: even. We're headed south — call it a one-seven-zero course most of the way — so odd would be the rule, *but*—" he raised the instructor finger; she'd stopped pretending it annoyed her "—that rule binds us in uncontrolled airspace. In controlled airspace, we fly what ATC assigns, and Center's going to assign what fits their flow. Still: file with the rule, because filing stupid invites questions."

"And you filed one-six, which is even, southbound, so—"

"So I misspoke to see if you were listening. FL two-zero-zero was what I filed, and don't grade the delivery, grade the content." He grinned; she did not laugh, on policy. "Altitude logic beyond odd-even: terrain. MEA on the airway segments through the passes runs as high as eleven thousand — MEA being the altitude that guarantees us both obstacle clearance *and* usable nav signal for the whole segment. There's a MOCA published lower on a couple of legs — the one with the asterisk — which guarantees the same obstacle clearance but only promises VOR reception within twenty-two nautical miles of the station. Fine for a check of position, not something I'd ride for an hour. And if we were going direct, off-airways, then it's on us: ninety-one one-seventy-seven, two thousand feet above the highest obstacle within four miles in designated mountainous terrain — which the Cascades very much are — one thousand elsewhere. The chart's OROCA numbers give you a sanity check per grid, but OROCA's advisory, not a clearance."

"MRA. MCA. Since you're showing off."

"MRA — minimum reception altitude — the altitude where an off-route facility's signal reaches you well enough to identify a fix. It's about *identifying the fix*, not about rocks. MCA — minimum crossing altitude — the altitude you must cross a fix at when the MEA jumps ahead of you, so the climb works out on the far side. The one people mix up is MOCA versus MEA, and the tell is the asterisk and the twenty-two miles."

"Oxygen, since we're pressurized and I want the answer anyway."

"Ninety-one two-eleven, unpressurized rules: crew on oxygen above one-two-five above twelve-five for more than thirty minutes, crew on it continuously above fourteen thousand, passengers *provided* it above fifteen thousand. In this airplane the cabin does the work — but the reg's still the floor if the pressurization quits and we're up high doing math."

She nodded and pulled the pad toward the fuel column. "Destination. Say the problem out loud."

Theo clicked the TAF up. "San Francisco, wind west eight, visibility six in mist, ceiling — here's the party — forecast eight hundred overcast around our arrival, improving late morning, TEMPO five hundred and two miles in drizzle. Marine layer's being stubborn."

"So. Alternate required?"

"Run the one-two-three," he said, and this time he waited — instructor patience, letting the student say it, and she permitted it because the ritual was becoming theirs. Clause Three in daylight.

"From one hour before to one hour after our ETA," Wren said, "if the forecast ceiling is less than two thousand feet, or visibility less than three statute miles, we file an alternate. Eight hundred overcast is less than two thousand. We file an alternate. We were always filing an alternate — the question is where."

"Oakland's across the bay."

"Oakland's in the same marine layer. An alternate that shares the destination's weather problem is a receipt, not a plan." She flipped the pad around to face him: her columns from last night. "Sacramento. Inland, out of the layer, forecast better than three thousand and five all morning. Now — legal test. What does Sacramento have to show us at *arrival time* to qualify on paper?"

"Alternate minimums. If the approach chart carries the black triangle-A, nonstandard mins, use what's published. Otherwise standard: precision approach available — forecast at least six hundred and two at your alternate ETA. Non-precision only — eight hundred and two. And if the field has no instrument approach at all, it can still be your paper alternate only if the forecast lets you descend from the MEA and land basically VFR, which is a fun trivia answer and a terrible actual plan. Sacramento's got ILSs stacked like cordwood. It qualifies fat."

"And the trap nobody briefs?"

"That the alternate minimums are a *filing* test, not a *flying* test. The six hundred and two is the bar the forecast has to clear for the field to go in the flight plan. If we actually divert there, we fly the approach to the real published minimums like anybody else. The paper test just proves the plan had margin when we made it."

"Fuel," Wren said. "Build it."

He built it, out loud, the way she'd have built it alone at her kitchen table — and there was a strange, specific pleasure in hearing her own private arithmetic in someone else's voice. "Ninety-one one-sixty-seven. Fuel to fly to the destination — with the approach. Then fly to the alternate — Sacramento, call it twenty-five minutes. Then forty-five minutes after that, at normal cruise burn. That's the legal floor, and the floor is not the plan: on top of it I want your taxi, your APU-if-we-had-one, and a contingency number, because the TEMPO says we might hold, and holding is where fuel goes to die. I penciled sixty minutes of pad above the reg. Tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong. NOTAMs."

"Pulled with the briefing, logged. The good stuff: SFO's got a runway two-eight-right ILS glideslope out of service NOTAM'd through Thursday — which quietly kills the lowest minimums on the favored runway and matters *enormously* if the TEMPO shows up while we're inbound. That's an FDC NOTAM, regulatory, changes the procedure. Plus the usual: taxiway lights, a crane near Oakland, a TFR over a stadium tonight that's after our window. The lesson I beat into students: the NOTAM you skip is always the one with your name on it. The glideslope one has our name on it."

Wren sat back. The plan was good. The plan was, in fact, exactly the plan she'd built at her kitchen table at eleven p.m., allowing for handwriting — same alternate, same pad, same flagged NOTAM — and the eeriness of that sat somewhere she didn't have an instrument for. Nine years she'd been building plans alone and checking them alone and carrying, alone, the specific low-grade weight of being the only person who'd catch it if it was wrong.

"Adequate," she said, in Voss's voice, and Theo clutched his chest like a man shot, and she did laugh, that time, briefly, on an exemption from policy she granted herself without review.

---

Adaeze Okafor boarded in traveling silk and a mood, kissed the air beside both their cheeks — "my *aviators*" — and settled in with her ginger tea, no ice, delivered at top of climb per the standing order. They were through eleven thousand when the speaker chimed and the day's interrogation began.

"Wren. Tell me about your first date."

Wren keyed the mic with the serenity of a woman who had, this time, briefed. In the right seat Theo's eyebrows announced that he'd been expecting mist and was watching a ceiling develop.

"He planned a flight," she said. "A pretend one. Somewhere neither of us had been — he had me pick the destination, and then he built the whole thing on the kitchen table. Route, weather, fuel. Where we'd stop. What we'd do if the weather went down — he had an alternate planned, a whole second evening, in case the first one didn't work out." She paused, listening to herself say it. "Most people plan the date they want. He planned the one we might actually get."

Silence from the cabin. Then: "And did you need it? The alternate?"

"Ma'am, with my luck?" Wren said. "We always need the alternate."

Okafor laughed like a woman who'd won something, and moved on to grilling Theo about rings — a subject on which he stalled magnificently, citing privacy, family traditions, and at one point, falsely, customs law — and Wren flew the airplane south along the airway and did not look at the right seat, because the story had come out of nowhere and somewhere, and the somewhere was a shoebox of expired charts belonging to a woman who had learned at Cascade Air Group what happens when you file no alternate for a person.

His name had been Captain Danny Ruiz, and the forecast had been lies, and she'd had nowhere to divert to, professionally or otherwise, when it all went down. Three years ago. She'd rebuilt at Meridian the way you rebuild after weather: slowly, with better planning, and with a standing rule about not flying into the same system twice.

"Meridian Three-Five," Oakland Center said, "San Francisco now reporting six hundred overcast, RVR on the two-eight approaches coming down. Say intentions."

And there it was — the forecast doing the thing forecasts do. Wren felt the crew settle into itself, felt Theo pull up the numbers without being asked.

"Bingo math," he said, off-mic, pen already moving. "We're forty out. Fuel on board says: one approach at SFO, the miss, direct Sacramento, plus the forty-five, plus my pad, no sweat. Two approaches at SFO and the math starts eating the pad. So — one look, and if we go missed, we don't get clever, we go to the plan."

"Agreed. And the glideslope NOTAM means two-eight-right is a localizer-only look with higher mins, so ask for the left." She keyed up: "Center, Meridian Three-Five, we'll continue, planning the ILS two-eight-left. We have fuel for one approach and then Sacramento, and we'd like the current Sacramento weather when able."

"Sacramento's showing four thousand scattered, ten miles."

"Of course it is," Theo murmured. "Inland alternates. Boring on purpose."

The marine layer off the bay looked, from above, like mercy — soft, white, absolute, hiding one of the busiest pieces of sky in the world. They came down the arrival with the checklists ticking, briefed the approach twice — once for the crew, once, shorter and warmer, for the speaker in the back — and broke out at seven hundred feet with the sequenced flashers leading them home and the city surfacing out of the white like a developed photograph.

Taxiing in, Okafor's voice came up one more time, gentler than the morning's.

"Captain. The pretend flight — the first date. Did he really plan an alternate?"

Wren watched the follow-me cart, and told the truth sideways, which was becoming the house style: "Ma'am, he's the kind of man who files one."

In the right seat, Theo looked out his window at nothing in particular for a while.

---

They did Clause Three at the FBO counter over terrible celebratory churros — Okafor had sent churros back to the flight deck, unexplained, queenly — and Wren ran the recap like a proper debrief because ritual was the only container the day would fit in.

"Route and altitudes," she said.

"Airways buy you MEAs; MEAs buy you obstacle clearance *and* signal. MOCA has the asterisk — same rocks, nav only inside twenty-two miles. MRA identifies a fix; MCA gets you across one climbing. Direct means ninety-one one-seventy-seven is yours to own: two thousand in the mountains, one thousand flat, and OROCA's advice, not clearance. Odd-even by magnetic course from ninety-one one-seventy-nine — odd east, even west, and ATC outranks the rhyme in controlled airspace."

"Alternate."

"One-two-three: within an hour of ETA, ceiling under two thousand or vis under three — file one. Filing test at the alternate: six hundred and two with a precision approach, eight hundred and two without, or the chart's own nonstandard numbers if it wears the triangle. No approach at all: descend-and-land-VFR trick answer. And it's a paper test — divert for real and you fly the published mins."

"Fuel."

"Destination, approach, alternate, forty-five minutes at cruise — that's the reg — then pad it like you'll meet the TEMPO, because you will. Bingo numbers *before* the weather asks. One look, then the plan."

"NOTAMs."

"The one you skip has your name on it. Ours was a dead glideslope that moved the whole approach conversation one runway left." He dusted cinnamon off the paperwork, and then, because he never could leave a clean stopping point alone: "Your turn. Where'd the first-date story come from? That wasn't improvisation. That had — structure."

Wren signed the fuel slip. Considered the exits. Took, deliberately, none of them.

"I plan alternates," she said. "For everything. It's an old habit from a bad forecast. Someday I might even tell you the METAR." She pocketed the pen. "Wheels up at eight tomorrow. Tell your dad the churros were structurally deficient and I said so."

It wasn't the story. It was the existence of the story, acknowledged — a fix identified, way off-route, signal faint but readable. From the look on Theo's face, walking out into the California evening, he knew exactly what he'd just received, and exactly what it had cost, and — this was the part that unsettled her, later, at a kitchen table set for one — he didn't push for more.

He just filed it, the way you file an alternate.

In case of weather. For someday.

*End of Chapter Three.*
